# Chorale performance: sources and a proposed model (task 3, for the owner's approval)

Status: **proposal**. Nothing here is built yet. Numbers marked *design* are choices of this
session, not figures from a source; the owner may change any of them.

## 1. What the sources say

Every reference was checked against an external record (Crossref DOI, publisher or journal page,
library catalogue, BachBib, a university repository, or the author's site). The eighteenth-century
German treatises were read in transcriptions that give the first editions' page numbers. The modern
English translations were not seen, so their page numbers are not given. "Content second-hand"
marks a work known only through a review or an abstract.

### 1.1 Fermatas in Bach's chorales

There is no consensus. The common modern reading is that the fermata marks the end of a hymn line
(*Zeilenschluss*), a place to breathe or articulate, not a hold of fixed length. Older and
practical readings hold the note.

- Koch defines the *Fermate* as lengthening a note or rest beyond its value. He does not speak of
  chorales. H. C. Koch, *Musikalisches Lexikon* (Frankfurt, 1802), "Fermate", cols. 561–567.
- C. P. E. Bach exempts "Fermaten und Cadenzen" from strict time. *Versuch über die wahre Art das
  Clavier zu spielen* I (Berlin, 1753), ch. 3, §8, p. 120.
- Hampson's thesis lists the readings on offer: ignore the sign, breathe, or hold for twice the
  value and then rest. It notes that in the organ chorales the other voices move under the fermata,
  so it cannot be a hold there. C. M. Hampson, "Pausing for Reflection: Re-evaluating Bach's Use of
  the Fermata", MMus(R) thesis, University of Glasgow, 2007 (abstract read).
- Franklin treats the fermata as a notational convention whose meaning in Bach is not the later
  hold. D. O. Franklin, "The Fermata as Notational Convention in the Music of J. S. Bach", in
  *Convention in Eighteenth- and Nineteenth-Century Music: Essays in Honor of Leonard G. Ratner*
  (Stuyvesant, NY: Pendragon, 1992), 345–381. Content second-hand.
- Schildkret, from a survey of chorale books, reads the fermata as the passage from one line to the
  next. D. Schildkret, "Toward a Correct Performance of Fermatas in Bach's Chorales", *BACH* 19/1
  (1988). Pages given as 21–27 and as 21–25 in different records; the year is given once as 1989.
  Content second-hand.
- No peer-reviewed measurement of fermata lengths or chorale tempi in recordings was found.

### 1.2 Breathing between phrases

- Türk: at the end of a period, lift gently from the last note. The small pause comes out of that
  note's own value. A full close is separated more than an *Einschnitt* (a comma). D. G. Türk,
  *Klavierschule* (Leipzig and Halle, 1789), ch. 6, §§21–23, pp. 341–343 (read). English: *School
  of Clavier Playing*, trans. R. H. Haggh (University of Nebraska Press, 1982).
- KTH "Punctuation" rule: a micropause after the last note of a melodic group, which is also
  lengthened. A. Friberg, R. Bresin, L. Frydén and J. Sundberg, "Musical Punctuation on the
  Microlevel", *Journal of New Music Research* 27/3 (1998): 271–292.

### 1.3 Ritardando at cadences

- Final ritardando modelled on stopping runners (velocity a square root of time). It was judged
  the best of the curves tried, and KTH means it for Baroque pieces in steady motion. A. Friberg
  and J. Sundberg, "Does Music Performance Allude to Locomotion?", *JASA* 105/3 (1999): 1469–1484;
  A. Friberg, R. Bresin and J. Sundberg, "Overview of the KTH Rule System for Musical Performance",
  *Advances in Cognitive Psychology* 2 (2006): 145–161 (read), p. 149. See also J. Sundberg and
  V. Verrillo, "On the Anatomy of the Retard", *JASA* 68/3 (1980): 772–779.
- Phrase arching, slow–fast–slow, applied at several levels at once, so that a deeper cadence slows
  more. N. P. McA. Todd, "A Model of Expressive Timing in Tonal Music", *Music Perception* 3/1
  (1985): 33–57; "The Kinematics of Musical Expression", *JASA* 97/3 (1995): 1940–1949. B. H. Repp,
  "Diversity and Commonality in Music Performance", *JASA* 92/5 (1992): 2546–2568. An alternative
  view: H. Honing, "Is There a Perception-Based Alternative to Kinematic Models of Tempo Rubato?",
  *Music Perception* 23/1 (2005): 79–85.
- A caution. C. P. E. Bach (1787 addition to I, ch. 3, §28, p. 129) allows a slight holding back
  on entering a fermata. In the same paragraph he warns against dragging, and asks for "die
  genaueste Gleichheit der Zeit=Maaße beym Ende eines Stückes". **A modest final ritardando, then.**
- Cadence classes over the 371 chorales' fermatas: T. de Clercq, "A Model for Scale-Degree
  Reinterpretation", *Empirical Musicology Review* 10/3 (2015): 188–206. The game's own classes
  (C15: PAC, IAC, HC, Phrygian HC, DC) serve the same end.

### 1.4 Tempo

- The claim that eighteenth-century congregations sang very slowly is contested. Several
  eighteenth-century authors complained that hymns were sung too fast, and a 1734 hymnal asked for
  slower singing. J. Herl, *Worship Wars in Early Lutheranism* (Oxford University Press, 2004),
  content second-hand through M. E. Frandsen's review, *Journal of Seventeenth-Century Music* 13/1
  (2007), §§9.1, 10.1 (read). The very slow, interluded English "old way" is an analogue, not
  Lutheran practice: N. Temperley, "The Old Way of Singing", *JAMS* 34/3 (1981): 511–544.
- Kirnberger's *tempo giusto* takes the tempo from the metre, the note values and any word.
  *The Art of Strict Musical Composition*, trans. D. Beach and J. Thym (Yale University Press,
  1982), vol. II/1, ch. 4 (pages unchecked). J. Dokter, *Tempo and Tactus in the German Baroque*
  (University of Rochester Press, 2021), content second-hand.
- **No sourced metronome figure exists. The default tempo is a design choice.**

### 1.5 Dynamics within a phrase

- Crescendo with accelerando towards the phrase's peak, then diminuendo with rallentando. N. P. McA.
  Todd, "The Dynamics of Dynamics", *JASA* 91/6 (1992): 3540–3550.
- KTH rules: "High loud" (louder with pitch, so a phrase's melodic peak is its loudest point),
  "Phrase arch" for loudness, and melodic and harmonic charge (Friberg et al. 2006, p. 149).
- Dissonances generally louder, consonances softer: C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch* I, ch. 3, §29,
  pp. 129–130. This bears on the suspensions at chorale cadences.

### 1.6 Balance: the melody forward

- On the piano the melody is louder and slightly early. C. Palmer, *JEP: HPP* 15/2 (1989):
  331–346; *Music Perception* 14/1 (1996): 23–56. The early onset is largely a by-product of the
  extra loudness: W. Goebl, "Melody Lead in Piano Performance", *JASA* 110/1 (2001): 563–572.
- In Bach's cantatas the closing chorale is usually doubled colla parte, the soprano by oboe,
  violin I, sometimes cornetto, horn or trumpet (scorings to be checked in A. Dürr, *The Cantatas
  of J. S. Bach*, trans. R. D. P. Jones, OUP 2005). On choir size see A. Parrott, *The Essential
  Bach Choir* (Boydell, 2000) and J. Rifkin, *Bach's Choral Ideal* (Klangfarben, 2002).

### 1.7 Repeated and tied notes

- Ties are held. Singers re-articulate a repeated pitch with the new syllable.
- Organ: a repeated note must be released before it is struck again. Current hymn-playing teaching
  repeats the soprano's repeated notes always and those of the lower parts often, though they may
  be tied (BYU OrganTutor, "Hymn Playing: Repeated Notes"; teaching material, not scholarship).
- KTH "Repetition articulation": a micropause between repeated notes (Friberg et al. 2006, p. 150).
- C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch* I, ch. 3, §§17–22, pp. 125–127: notes neither detached nor slurred are
  held for half their value. How widely this applies is debated.

### 1.8 Organist and choir

- The organist leading a congregation plays line by line, often with interludes between the lines.
  Evidence: BWV 726 and 738a. The Arnstadt protocol of 21 February 1706 complains of
  "wunderliche variationes" and "frembde Thone" (*Bach-Dokumente* II, Nr. 16). D. G. Türk, *Von den
  wichtigsten Pflichten eines Organisten* (Halle, 1787), trans. M. A. G. Woolard (Scarecrow,
  2000). D. Remeš, *ZGMTH* 16/2 (2019): 51–94 (read).
- A choir singing a cantata chorale has no interludes. The fermata is at most a breath or a short
  hold, the voices are doubled by instruments, and the director sets the tempo.

## 2. Proposed model

One module, `src/audio/chorale-performance.ts`:

```
perform(chorale, options) → { events, tempoMap }
chorale: four voices of timed notes (onset, length, pitch, fermata?), the phrase ends,
         each phrase's cadence class (PAC, IAC, HC, PHC, DC), the final.
```

The engine plays the events through its live loop (`playAll`). The live tempo slider scales the
whole map, so it applies at once, as do the voice switches (gates), the pause and the volume.

### Options the player chooses

| option | values (default first) |
|---|---|
| style | **choir** (a cantata chorale) · **organ** (leading a congregation) |
| fermata | **breath** (taken out of the last note, no time added; Türk) · **hold** (the note lengthened by the cadence's weight; Koch) · **hold and breath** · **none** (strict, as now) |
| tempo | the slider (default ♩ = 66, *design*) |
| expression | on · off (off is today's strict playback) |

### The tempo map

1. **Phrase-end rallentando.** Over the last two beats before each fermata, the tempo eases to a
   depth set by the cadence: PAC 12%, IAC 9%, Phrygian HC 9%, HC 7%, DC 6% (*design*, ordered by
   cadential weight as Todd's multi-level arch implies). The next phrase resumes a tempo.
2. **Final cadence.** Friberg and Sundberg's curve over the last three beats, ending at about 70%
   of the tempo (*design*; kept modest after C. P. E. Bach's warning). The final chord is held
   1.5 times its value and fades.
3. **Fermata.**
   - *Breath*: the fermata chord loses 25% of its value, capped at 0.4 s (*design*), and the
     silence fills the rest.
   - *Hold*: ×1.3 for HC, DC or Phrygian; ×1.5 for IAC or PAC (*design*), plus a 0.15 s gap.
   - *Hold and breath*: the hold, then the breath taken out of the added time.
4. **Phrase arch.** A gentle slow–fast–slow within each phrase (±3%, *design*). Off in the organ
   style, which keeps the congregation's steadier beat.

### Dynamics and balance

- The **soprano 3 dB above** the lower voices in both styles: the cantatas' doubling for the
  choir, registration for the organ (*design*).
- **Choir**: each phrase rises by up to 2 dB towards its highest melody note and falls back to the
  cadence (Todd 1992; KTH "High loud"). A suspension's dissonance is 1 dB louder (C. P. E. Bach §29).
- **Organ**: no dynamics within a phrase; an organ cannot make them by touch.

### Articulation

- **Tied notes** held through.
- **Repeated notes**:
  - Choir: re-struck, with a 40 ms gap (*design*, after KTH "Repetition articulation").
  - Organ: the soprano always re-struck; inner voices and bass tied over a repeated note, as
    current hymn-playing teaching allows (an option).

### Not proposed now

- Organ interludes between lines (Zwischenspiele). They are well attested, but building them means
  composing music, which goes beyond the sources' description. Possible later, if the owner wants.

## 3. What the engine needs (an API change; the other sessions will be told)

`playAll` places events by bars at the live tempo. The model needs one optional addition: events
carrying their own time in seconds at the reference tempo (from the tempo map), which the engine
scales by `reference tempo / live tempo` as it schedules. The live slider, gates, pause and loop
then work as they do everywhere else. Existing callers are unaffected.

## What the second round of sources adds

Checked as in round 1. "Snippet" marks a manual seen only through full-text search, so the exact
page is not always known.

- **Two camps.**
  - *A line mark, not a hold* (the historically informed mainstream and several conducting
    textbooks):
    - Koopman: "they should not be held". U. Golomb, "Interview with Ton Koopman", *Goldberg* 24
      (2003): 42–51 (read). His evidence includes Orgelbüchlein settings with semiquavers moving
      under the fermata.
    - M. Rudolf, *The Grammar of Conducting*, 3rd ed. (Schirmer, 1994), ch. 19 (snippet).
    - R. W. Demaree and D. V. Moses, *The Complete Conductor* (Prentice-Hall, 1995), ch. 6
      (snippet): Bach's fermatas "do not always imply an interruption of the meter". The book
      distinguishes three kinds: final; release and breath; continue at once.
    - R. L. Garretson, *Conducting Choral Music* (Prentice Hall, 1988) (snippet).
    - A Strube Verlag edition: "keine Tonverlängerung … wo Liedzeilen enden und daher geatmet
      werden kann" (no lengthening of the note; where lines end, and so a breath can be taken).
  - *A hold, with exceptions* (the traditional textbook view): H. D. Marple, *The Beginning
    Conductor* (McGraw-Hill, 1972), ch. 12, pp. 111–112 (snippet). Usually "double or sometimes even
    triple value", but not where the words forbid it: "a matter of taste and text".
- **Recordings, as critics describe them (not measured).**
  - Harnoncourt's St Matthew Passion holds each fermata about three beats; most early-music
    conductors barely hold. B. D. Sherman, "Performing Bach's St. Matthew Passion", *Early Music
    America* (2001) (read).
  - Norrington is mixed, sometimes a breath, sometimes a hold. Golomb 2002 (read).
- **Organists.**
  - Some fermatas are left out to keep the tune moving: A. C. Lovelace, *The Organist and Hymn
    Playing*, rev. ed. (Agape, 1981) (snippet).
  - The breath is taken out of the note: an eighth when the music moves in quarters, a quarter if
    the last note is a half or longer. The final chord is held a bar, then a quarter's rest. Repeated
    notes are broken in soprano and tenor, common tones tied. D. Heller, *Manual on Hymn Playing*
    (GIA, 1992), pp. 11, 14 (snippet).
- **Tempo.**
  - Quantz XVII.vii §§51–55 (pp. 264–267; read): a pulse of about 80 a minute; in common time, an
    Allegretto has a crotchet to the pulse and an Adagio cantabile a quaver; church music is "etwas
    gemäßigter" (somewhat more moderate).
  - Kirnberger, *Die Kunst des reinen Satzes* II/1 (1776), pp. 106–107: the natural tempo (tempo
    giusto) comes from the metre and the note values; alla breve and 3/2 are heavier, 3/4 lighter.
  - Strube: a calm tempo "damit die Achtelnoten ruhig fließen können" (so that the quavers can flow
    calmly).
  - **Still no published metronome range for the chorales, and no measured study of their tempi or
    fermatas.**
- **The names.**
  - One voice per part (OVPP), or one singer per part: J. Rifkin, "Bach's Chorus: A Preliminary
    Report", *Musical Times* 123 (1982): 747–754; A. Parrott, *The Essential Bach Choir* (Boydell,
    2000).
  - Bach's own words in the *Entwurff* of 1730: *Concertisten* and *Ripienisten*; see R. Herz,
    "Concertists and Ripienists", *American Choral Review* 29/3–4 (1987): 35–52.

## The revised model

The fermata is treated by context, and the player chooses a **reading** (the two camps, and the one
between them).

| reading | an internal fermata | the final cadence |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical (line mark)**: Koopman, Rudolf, Demaree, Strube | in time; a breath taken out of the last note (an eighth when the music moves in quarters, a quarter after a half note or longer) | a modest rallentando over the last line; the final chord held about a bar |
| **Traditional (held)**: Marple; Harnoncourt as reported | held about twice its value, then the breath | a clear rallentando; the final chord held long |
| **By context (default)**: Demaree's three kinds; Norrington and Herreweghe's later practice as reported | decided fermata by fermata, as below | as the historical reading, a little broader |

**By context, fermata by fermata.** The texts are not in the encoding, so their punctuation (the
first criterion of Marple and the forum) cannot be read. These stand in for it:

1. **Kept moving.** No hold and no breath where a voice moves on under the fermata (passing or
   neighbour notes in another part, as Koopman's Orgelbüchlein evidence). The game's incidental-note
   reading (C3) finds them.
2. **Breath only** at a half, deceptive or Phrygian cadence, and at the first line's cadence
   (Demaree: the first phrase's close weighs least).
3. **Held** (about 1.5 times its value, then the breath) at a full close (perfect authentic, or
   imperfect on the tonic). It is held longest at the most important internal cadence: the end of
   the repeated opening section (the *Stollen* of the bar form, which the encoding marks AAB), or a
   full close in another key.
4. **The last line**: the rallentando begins, and the final chord is held about a bar.

Each fermata's reading is shown on the score, and the player may change any single fermata (held /
breath / none).

**Tempo by default**:
- a crotchet chorale in common time or 3/4: ♩ ≈ 66, between Quantz's Allegretto and his Adagio
  cantabile, moderated as church music;
- alla breve and 3/2 chorales counted in minims, at about the same pace per note, a little heavier
  (Kirnberger);
- capped so that the quavers flow calmly (Strube).

These are interpolations, not sourced figures, and the slider changes them live.

**Unchanged from round 1**:
- the soprano a little forward (3 dB);
- small swells toward each phrase's highest note in the choir styles, none for the organ (which
  cannot make them);
- tied notes held;
- repeated notes re-struck. The organ follows Heller: soprano and tenor repeated notes broken,
  common tones in the inner voices tied.
