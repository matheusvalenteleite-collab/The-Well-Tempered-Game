# Contrapunctus XIV, four hands: articulation and dynamics (P46)

A working synthesis for the owner (secondo: tenor and bass) and his teacher (primo: soprano and alto). The evidence is in
`research/articulation.md` and `research/dynamics.md`. Those reports quote only what was read, and mark the rest
"unverified". Two of their central claims were checked again here against the Packard Humanities Institute edition
of C. P. E. Bach's *Versuch*: I.3 §22 (half value) and I.3 §29 (dissonances louder). Everything below that is not attributed is my reading.

## What the sources give us

- **The text.** The autograph (P 200, Beilage 3) is the primary source; the 1751 print copies it and stops about six bars
  earlier.
  - Moroney's Henle critical report lists no slur variants for this fugue, only ties and ornaments. So the autograph probably has essentially no articulation marks (unverified until the facsimile is seen). Every slur and dot in a modern edition is an editor's.
  - **One fact for the soprano:** in bar 114 the two As are *not* tied. The "tie" is a leftover half-note rest (Moroney). Re-strike the second A.
- **The time.** The print has C. The autograph's double-length bars in bars 209–228 and the stile antico idiom point to a half-note pulse. Quantz: in alla breve, the notes go twice as fast as in common time (XVII.vii §50).
- **No dynamics.** Bach marked none. The period's keyboards made loudness mainly by texture: fewer voices for piano, octave doubling for forte (Quantz XVII.vi §17). Both Quantz and C. P. E. Bach still prized the clavichord's and fortepiano's gradual nuance. So nothing licenses either a flat, "terraced" piano or a Romantic arch laid over the counterpoint.

## Articulation

1. **The default is not legato, but this piece asks for weight.** The treatises disagree on how long an unmarked note is held:
   - C. P. E. Bach: half its value (I.3 §22).
   - Türk: lifted only a little early.
   - Marpurg: the "ordentliche Fortgehen".

   All three, however, make fugues, alla breve, dissonant and serious pieces the exception. These are played *schwer*, with each note held to its full value (Türk §§43–49). Türk names "Sebastian Bach" as needing more weight than Mozart (§50). For subject I and every long note: full length, separated only by the smallest gap that lets each new note be heard as a new syllable.
2. **Leaps detach, steps connect.** Before a leap, especially subject I's fifth, make a slightly clearer separation (C. P. E. Bach §17; Quantz XI §16). Passing and appoggiatura-like notes are connected even when unmarked (C. P. E. Bach §18).
3. **Repeated notes are re-struck audibly**, the second slightly lighter.
4. **Suspensions.**
   - Release the tie cleanly.
   - The voice that moves against it must be clearly attacked: on the piano the held note has decayed, so the dissonance is *made* by the other voice.
   - Give the dissonance weight and join it to a lighter resolution (Quantz XVII.vi §12; C. P. E. Bach §29).
   - Lift after the resolution if the line goes on.
   - Make a tiny lift *before* a syncopated attack.
5. **Subject II (the running subject from bar 114).** No marks in the source, so choose one of two readings, and keep it for every entry, in all four hands:
   - (A) quasi-legato in small groups, with a light lift at changes of direction;
   - (B) an even non-legato, with the first and third of each four slightly weighted.

   Avoid hard staccato. A critic singled out "hard-bitten staccato in Contrapunctus 14" in the one commercial piano-duo recording.
6. **B-A-C-H (from bar 193).** Connect the four chromatic steps and separate before the cadential tail. Keep the same articulation in the inversion (bar 222), so that the two forms are heard as one idea.
7. **Independence by articulation.** With one voice per hand, the lines separate best when their breaths fall at different moments. Hewitt finds each voice's breathing places by singing it alone. Agree a shared articulation for each subject, because four hands replace Moroney's "single brain".

## Dynamics

1. **Dissonances louder than their resolutions.** C. P. E. Bach I.3 §29 (checked). Quantz grades dissonance types in three classes, mezzo forte, forte and fortissimo, scaled down in soft passages (XVII.vi §§13–16).
2. **Subjects get *Nachdruck*, but not a spotlight.** Quantz (XVII.vii §28) and Türk (§50) ask for the subject to be marked in every voice, "especially when it begins with long notes", which is subject I.
   - Quantz's way includes *softening the notes before an entry* when no rest precedes it.
   - Moroney argues against "bringing out" a theme that never changes. Rosen describes distinct lines "heard as part of an inseparable harmony".
   - My reading: rotate the emphasis. The hand with the entry marks its first notes by weight and length; the others make room. There is no fixed hierarchy, and the emphasis goes to whoever has the dissonance as much as to whoever has the subject (Quantz VII §29).
3. **Texture already makes the crescendo.** More voices, stretti, the combinations at bars 148 and 233, and the high entries around bar 180 already grow the sound. Add little on top. Quantz ties growth itself to "adding voices" (XVII.vi §15).
4. **A plan to try:**
   - **Section I (bars 1–114):** begins with the bass alone. Mezzo piano, growing only through entries.
   - **Section II (from bar 114):** lighter and more speaking. Grows through density to the stretto climax Hewitt places at bar 180.
   - **Section III (bar 193, after a cadence in G minor):** a new beginning, clear but not loud. Inward, not louder. The bass inversion at bar 222 is the most poignant chromatic moment.
   - **The end:** the half-close at bar 233 leads into the triple combination, and the music breaks off at bar 239.
5. **The end.** Rehearse two versions and choose between them:
   - (a) full presence, cut off: Hewitt's "the music stops … takes our breath away";
   - (b) withdrawal into the break.

   Agree whether all four hands lift together, and whether the silence after is part of the piece. That is where Hewitt and the duo Stephanie & Saar stop; Hofmann recommends the chorale after it, as in the 1751 print.
6. **Four hands at one keyboard.**
   - **Inner voices:** on a modern grand, the alto and tenor (the two inner hands) sink in the middle register. They may need a slightly higher nominal level than the outer voices to sound equal.
   - **Pedal:** the secondo normally pedals, and the pedal affects all four voices. Little or none: Kohn recorded the cycle without pedal.
   - **Stretti within one player:** in the primo's stretti (e.g. around bar 180), mark the later entry and let the earlier, now mid-subject, recede.
