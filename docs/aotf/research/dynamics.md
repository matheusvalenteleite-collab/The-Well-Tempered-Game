# Dynamics in Bach's unfinished fugue (*Die Kunst der Fuge*, BWV 1080, "Contrapunctus 14" / *Fuga a 3 soggetti*), for modern piano, four hands, one voice per hand

Research report, compiled 21 October 2026.

**Labels used throughout.** **[S]** = what a primary source or treatise says. **[I]** = scholarly interpretation. **[P]** = documented performers' practice. **[D]** = my own synthesis or suggestion. A quotation appears only where I read the words in a fetched source, and its URL is given. Translations from German are mine unless marked otherwise. Anything I could not check is marked **unverified**.

**How the voices map onto the four hands.** The fugue is in four voices; Moroney's track listing calls it "Contrapunctus 14 à 4 voix". So one voice per hand gives:

- primo right hand (P-RH): soprano
- primo left hand (P-LH): alto
- secondo right hand (S-RH): tenor
- secondo left hand (S-LH): bass

## 0. Structure: the map used in this report

**Section boundaries.** These come from Colin MacKnight's article in *The Diapason*, cross-checked against Angela Hewitt's Hyperion notes. Both are cited in the bibliography.

- **Section I** runs from bar 1 to the downbeat of bar 115. It uses Subject I alone, a slow subject in long values, together with its inversion.
- **Section II** begins with Subject II, the running subject, which enters in the alto on beat 2 of bar 114 (Hewitt: "In bar 114 ... in the alto voice, the second subject enters"). Hewitt: "In bar 148 ... these two subjects are combined for the first time." She places the climax of this section at bar 180: "beginning in bar 180 ... the second subject in the bass supports two entries of the first subject in stretto in the alto and soprano voices."
- **Section III** begins with Subject III, B-A-C-H, from beat 3 of bar 193 (MacKnight). Hewitt notes the inversion of B-A-C-H "in the bass in bar 222 ... produces some especially poignant chromaticisms". She also notes that "The half-close in bar 233 ... leads us to the point where Bach combines these three subjects together". The autograph then breaks off in bar 239.
- **Two qualifications.**
  - **[I]** Klaus Hofmann's Bärenreiter preface states that "the fragmentary Quadruple Fugue appears to have been abridged by seven bars in the print" (BA 5207, p. XII). The bar-239 break is therefore the autograph's (P 200, Beilage 3), not the 1751 print's. I could not verify the print's exact final bar number.
  - **[I]** MacKnight's article combines this structural map with numerological readings (bar counts summing to 14, a projected total of 288 bars). These are speculative and contested, and I use only his bar map.

## 1. The instrument question and what it implies for dynamics

### 1.1 What was the work written for?

- **[S]** The autograph and the 1751 and 1752 prints are in open score and name no instrument. The one exception is "a 2. Clav:" for the two-keyboard arrangement of the mirror fugue (Hofmann, Bärenreiter BA 5207 preface, p. XII).
- **[S, via secondary]** C. P. E. Bach's 1751 advertisement, as quoted in English in Hewitt's Hyperion notes, describes the work as "arranged for use at the harpsichord or organ". I did not see the German text. BachBib lists the notice as *Critische Nachrichten* (Berlin), no. 19, 7 May 1751, pp. 145–146 (bibliographic record only).
- **[I] The harpsichord thesis.**
  - Gustav Leonhardt, *The Art of Fugue: Bach's Last Harpsichord Work. An Argument* (The Hague: Nijhoff, 1952), argued for the harpsichord.
  - His arguments, as summarized by Wikipedia and Europadisc: open score was standard notation for contrapuntal keyboard music (Frescobaldi, Froberger); the voice ranges do not fit ensemble instruments; there is no continuo; and the work resembles the WTC. Because the bass sometimes rises above the tenor, the bass was not meant to be doubled at 16-foot pitch, which argues against the pedal organ.
  - I could not access Leonhardt's book itself (archive.org copy restricted), so these points are second-hand.
  - Donald Tovey had earlier, in 1931, argued for keyboard performance, favouring the piano. This is reported second-hand in a Glenn Gould mailing-list post and is unverified.
- **[I] Davitt Moroney** (Henle HN 423 preface, Paris 1989, pp. V–VII) is categorical:
  - "Bach must indeed have had the harpsichord predominantly in mind"
  - "Die Kunst der Fuge is a harpsichord work"
  - "Much of the overwhelming evidence proving this point has been published by Gustav Leonhardt."
- **[I] Klaus Hofmann** (Bärenreiter BA 5207 preface, 1998, p. XII) is more cautious. The work is "music for keyboard, preferably a harpsichord". He concedes that Bach's writing "occasionally surpasses the limits of what can normally be compassed by two hands on a harpsichord manual", especially in the mirror fugues. Of the four-voice mirror fugue he writes that it "is technically and acoustically satisfying only when performed with the support of a second keyboard instrument". He supplies a two-harpsichord arrangement that gives "the descant and tenor ... to the first player and the alto and bass to the second", and swaps the inner voices in bars 25–32 to avoid obscuring an entry. This is directly relevant to a four-hand layout (see §4.6).
- **[I] Joseph Kerman** (*The Art of Fugue: Bach Fugues for Keyboard, 1715–1750*, 2005, p. 31) says Bach "wrote the fugues to be played on a harpsichord". He leaves open whether that should "privilege harpsichord as their ideal performance medium". On p. xix he quotes Rosen: "The proper instrument is what one has at home: harpsichord, clavichord, organ or piano."
- **Disagreement to flag.**
  - Harpsichord: Leonhardt, Moroney, and Hofmann (with qualification).
  - Organ: a long tradition of organists (Walcha and others) and C. P. E. Bach's own advertisement.
  - Ensemble: Wolfgang Graeser in the 1920s held it to be ensemble music meant to be orchestrated (reported by Hofmann).
  - Study score: the open-score format has been read as music primarily for study (Wikipedia summary).
  - The consensus is "keyboard". Which keyboard remains argued.

### 1.2 What dynamic resources did each instrument have? (18th-century evidence)

**Harpsichord [S].** Quantz gives the exact means (*Versuch*, 1752, ch. XVII, sect. VI, §17, pp. 230–231):

> "Auf einem Clavicymbal mit einem Claviere, kann das Piano durch einen gemäßigten Anschlag, und durch die Verminderung der Stimmen; das Mezzo forte durch Verdoppelung der Octaven im Basse; das Forte durch eben dieses, und wenn man noch in der linken Hand einige zum Accorde gehörige Consonanzen mitnimmt; das Fortissimo aber, durch geschwinde Brechungen der Accorde von unten herauf ... und durch einen heftigern und stärkern Anschlag, hervor gebracht werden. Auf einem Clavicymbal mit zweyen Clavieren, hat man über dieses noch den Vortheil, zum Pianissimo sich des obersten Claviers bedienen zu können."

In summary:

- piano comes from a moderated touch and fewer voices
- mezzo forte from doubling the bass in octaves
- forte from that plus added consonances
- fortissimo from rapid spreading of chords and a more violent touch
- on two manuals, the upper manual gives pianissimo

So on the harpsichord, loudness was produced largely by **texture** (number of voices, octave doubling, spreading), not only by switching manuals.

**Organ and two-manual harpsichord [S].** C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch* II (1762), ch. 29, §7: loud passages go on the stronger manual, both hands move to the softer manual for piano, and pianissimo is reached by thinning the harmony. On doubling the bass in octaves he writes: "This doubling is very good in fugues, when the theme enters, and in imitations, if they should be brought out." German: "Bey Fugen, wenn das Thema eintritt, bey Nachahmungen, welche stark vorgetragen werden sollen …" Source: https://versuch.cpebach.org/v-html/part-II/chapter-29/II-29-p7.html

**Clavichord and fortepiano [S].**
- C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch* I (1753), Introduction, §15: "use the clavichord to learn good performance and the harpsichord to acquire the proper strength in your fingers". The same paragraph speaks of "the differentiated attack that only a good clavichord player can reproduce" (https://versuch.cpebach.org/v-html/part-I/introduction/I-intro-p15.html).
- *Versuch* II, ch. 29, §6: the clavichord and fortepiano "remain superior in many respects" "because of their gradual dynamic variation" (https://versuch.cpebach.org/v-html/part-II/chapter-29/II-29-p6.html).
- Quantz XVII.vi §17 (p. 231): on a pianoforte "kann alles erfoderliche am allerbequemsten bewerkstelliget werden" (everything required can be done most conveniently). On a good clavichord the playing is the same, "nicht aber in Ansehung der Wirkung; weil das Fortissimo mangelt" (but not the effect, because it lacks a fortissimo).

### 1.3 Implications for a modern piano [D]

1. **Nothing licenses either extreme.**
   - Not the "terraced, unchanging" harpsichord stereotype. Quantz describes graded dynamics on a single-manual harpsichord, and both he and C. P. E. Bach prize the gradual nuance of clavichord and fortepiano.
   - Not a nineteenth-century dynamic arch imposed from outside either. Bach marked no dynamics in this fugue, as Hewitt notes for the whole work: "Bach provides none" (a reviewer's paraphrase; see §3.3).
2. **The 18th-century link between loudness and texture is the most transferable principle.** On the harpsichord, more voices and octave doubling meant louder, and fewer voices meant softer. In the four-hand version, texture already does that work: when all four hands are playing, the piece is louder. That argues for restraint in adding a further crescendo on top of a textural one.
3. **Clavichord-style nuance within a single line is the historically attested model for per-voice shaping.** Each hand can shape its own line, since one hand plays one voice, rather than shaping a "melody over accompaniment".

## 2. 18th-century writers on dynamics (verified references)

### 2.1 C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen*, Part I (Berlin, 1753)

- **Part I, ch. 3 ("Vom Vortrage"), §29 [S].** Opens with the degrees pianissimo to fortissimo. It then states that "dissonances are played more loudly and consonances more softly". German: "daß die Dissonanzen insgemein stärker und die Consonanzen schwächer gespielt werden". The stated reason is that dissonances arouse the passions and consonances calm them. The same paragraph notes that composers often mark a repeated idea forte at one point and piano at another, and that a deceptive cadence meant to arouse strong feeling is usually played forte. URL: https://versuch.cpebach.org/v-html/part-I/chapter-3/I-3-p29.html. That site renders it as "I 3 p29", meaning Part I, ch. 3, paragraph 29.
- **[I]** The online edition (Packard Humanities Institute) frames this as a general tendency, not a rule. David Schulenberg's commentary notes that C. P. E. Bach rarely discusses such performance techniques for continuo.

### 2.2 Johann Joachim Quantz, *Versuch einer Anweisung die Flöte traversiere zu spielen* (Berlin: Voß, 1752)

Text read in the Deutsches Textarchiv transcription. Page numbers are those of the 1752 edition.

**Ch. XVII, sect. VI ("Von dem Clavieristen insbesondere"), §§12–17, pp. 227–231 [S].**

- **§13:** Dissonances differ in strength. One must always strike some more strongly than others ("immer eine stärker als die andere angeschlagen werden"). The harshest ones "erfodern noch mehr Nachdruck" and must be played by the accompanist "vermittelst eines stärkern Anschlags, noch kräftiger".
- **§14 (with the example Tab. XXIV, Fig. 1):** Quantz divides dissonances into three classes, marked **mezzo forte**, **forte** and **fortissimo**.
  - *Mezzo forte*: the 2nd with the 4th; the 5th with the major 6th; the major 6th with the minor 3rd; the minor 7th with the minor 3rd; the major 7th.
  - *Forte*: the 2nd with the augmented 4th; the diminished 5th with the minor 6th.
  - *Fortissimo*: the augmented 2nd with the augmented 4th; the minor 3rd with the augmented 4th; the diminished 5th with the major 6th; the augmented 6th; the diminished 7th; the major 7th with the 2nd and 4th.
  - Consonant chords in an Adagio should be accompanied "überhaupt mezzo piano", so as to keep room to go softer or louder.
  - Where a passage is marked piano or pianissimo, the dissonances are scaled down in proportion: "beym Pianissimo die Dissonanzen aus der dritten Classe, nur die Stärke von der ersten; und beym Piano die Stärke von der zweyten Classe bekommen".
  - He adds that one should watch "die haltenden Noten, welche die Lebhaftigkeit unterbrechen", deceptive cadences, and chromatically altered notes leading to foreign keys. Attending to these, one can "errathen" (guess) piano, mezzo forte, forte and fortissimo even where nothing is written.
  - The purpose is "eine Nachahmung der Menschenstimme".
  - *Correction to the brief:* the instruction is "pianissimo / mezzo forte / forte / fortissimo" classes, plus a scaling rule for piano and pianissimo contexts. It is not a simple "pianissimo vs forte" pairing.
- **§15:** When several dissonances of different kinds follow one another, and dissonances resolve into dissonances, the expression must grow "durch Verstärkung des Tones, und Vermehrung der Stimmen" (by strengthening the tone and adding voices). Some mild dissonances, such as passing ones and 7ths alternating with 6/4, "keinen besondern Ausdruck erfodern".
- **§16:** Dissonances in general must be struck more strongly than consonances. Close-spaced dissonances sound "viel härter" than widely spaced ones.

**Ch. XVII, sect. III, §13 (on the viola player), p. 210 [S].** The "Thema oder Hauptsatz einer Fuge" must be "durch die Stärke des Tones, mit Nachdruck erhoben und markiret werden". The same applies to long notes (quarters, halves, whole bars) that follow quick notes and interrupt the liveliness, especially when sharpened or naturalized.

**Ch. XVII, sect. VII, §§28–29 (on all accompanying instrumentalists), p. 253 [S].**

- **§28:**
  > "Ein Hauptsatz, (Thema) zumal in einer Fuge, muß in einer jeden Stimme, und zu allen Zeiten wenn er unvermuthet eintritt, mit Nachdrucke markiret werden; besonders wenn der Anfang davon aus langen Noten besteht. Es findet dabey weder eine Schmeicheley im Spielen, noch einiger willkührlicher Zusatz von Noten statt. Wenn im Fortgange der Fuge keine Pausen vor dem Eintritte vorhergehen; kann man die vorhergehenden Noten in der Stärke des Tones etwas mäßigen."

  In summary: mark the subject in every voice; no flattering softness and no added notes; and if no rest precedes an entry, soften the preceding notes slightly.
- **§29:** Tied notes ("Ligaturen") of quarter or half length may grow in loudness, because other voices form dissonances against their second half. Dissonances "in dieser oder jener Stimme" always require special emphasis.

**Caveats [I/D].**
- Quantz writes about ensemble and continuo playing, not solo keyboard fugue.
- His dissonance classes are figured-bass chord types, which have to be mapped onto contrapuntal sonorities.
- His fugue rule is the clearest 18th-century endorsement of *marking* subject entries. Note, though, his preference for achieving it partly by softening what precedes the entry.

### 2.3 Daniel Gottlob Türk, *Klavierschule* (Leipzig and Halle, 1789)

Text read in the archive.org OCR of the 1789 edition. Page numbers are those of the 1789 edition as printed in the OCR running heads.

**Ch. 6, sect. 2, §§12–15, pp. 335–337 [S].** Clarity of performance depends partly on the "Nachdruck" that certain notes receive (§10, p. 334f.). The notes needing "einen besondern Nachdruck (Accent)" are:

1. those on good beats (§13, with graded strengths for the first and second good beats)
2. the first notes of phrases and sections (§14, more strongly after a full cadence than after a half cadence or a mere caesura)
3. (§15) among others, "diejenigen Intervalle, die sich zu dem Basse ... selbst wie Dissonanzen verhalten", the notes that prepare tied dissonances, syncopations, notes foreign to the diatonic scale of the current key, and notes conspicuous for their "Länge, Höhe oder Tiefe" (length, height or depth).

Türk defers the reasons why dissonances are played louder to a later paragraph, which I did not read. Short passing notes are exempted.

**Ch. 6, sect. 3, §49, p. 363f. [S].** "Ein Tonstück mit vielen Dissonanzen muß nämlich schwerer vorgetragen werden." "Schwer" means a weightier, more sustained style of execution, not only louder.

**Ch. 6, sect. 3, §50, p. 364 [S].** Works by Handel and Sebastian Bach "muß nachdrücklicher vorgetragen werden" than a modern concerto. In the painters' language, "gewisse Stellen müssen Licht, andere aber Schatten erhalten. So muß man z. B. in Fugen oder gearbeiteten Tonstücken vorzüglich das Thema (Subjekt,) und die nachahmenden Stellen mit Nachdruck vortragen, damit sie desto hervorstechender werden." The OCR of the index gives the page as "369" for this entry, so page 364 versus 369 needs checking against a facsimile.

Türk also treats fugues as pieces for a "ernsten Zweck" requiring a "weit schwerern Vortrag" (§45, p. 360).

### 2.4 Synthesis of the 18th-century evidence [D]

The three writers converge on four points.

- **Subjects** get "Nachdruck". Quantz and Türk say so explicitly for fugue, and C. P. E. Bach endorses octave-doubling at fugal entries in accompaniment.
- **Dissonances** are louder than their resolutions, graded by harshness (Quantz).
- **Long notes, chromatic notes and phrase beginnings** receive weight.
- **Chains of dissonance** justify growth "by strengthening and by adding voices" (Quantz §15).

None of them discusses the *stile antico* specifically. Türk's "schwerer Vortrag" for fugues and dissonant pieces is the closest to a stylistic register. Note that "Nachdruck" is emphasis, which can be agogic or a matter of touch as well as loudness.

## 3. Modern scholarship and performers

### 3.1 Is the fugue "unfinished", and does it belong? [I]

- **C. P. E. Bach's note on the autograph [S]:** "Ueber dieser Fuge, wo der Nahme BACH im Contrasubject angebracht worden, ist der Verfasser gestorben" (quoted on Wikipedia).
- **Modern dating** places the manuscript c. 1748–49, before Bach's final decline, and so undermines the deathbed story (Wikipedia summary).
- **Christoph Wolff**, "The Last Fugue: Unfinished?", *Current Musicology* 19 (1975), pp. 71–77; repr. in *Bach: Essays on His Life and Music* (Harvard UP, 1991), pp. 259–264. Wolff hypothesizes a lost combinatorial sketch ("Fragment x") working out all four subjects together. He argues the fugue was probably composed or sketched to the end and that its continuation is lost. A University of Auckland thesis argues against this (abstract only seen).
- **Gustav Nottebohm** (1881) showed that the main *Kunst der Fuge* subject combines with the three subjects, so the fugue was presumably planned as a quadruple fugue (reported by MacKnight and others).
- **Gregory Butler**, "Ordering Problems in J. S. Bach's *Art of Fugue* Resolved", *Musical Quarterly* 69/1 (1983), pp. 44–61. Butler argued the fugue was meant to stand as no. 14, and Moroney's Henle preface follows him.
- **Disagreement:** Butler later repudiated this. Schulenberg reports that Butler "now argues that it is a 'myth' that Bach intended the *Art of Fugue* to include the incomplete *Fuga a 3 soggetti*". The citation is Butler, "Scribes, Engravers, and Notational Styles: The Final Disposition of Bach's *Art of Fugue*", in *About Bach*, ed. Butler, Stauffer and Greer (2008), pp. 111–23.
- **Moroney** (Henle preface) still calls it "the climactic 14th fugue". He notes the B-A-C-H signature appears "at precisely the end of the 14th fugue" and calls the chorale "irrelevant".
- **Hofmann** (Bärenreiter preface) recommends ending a complete performance with the fugue, "either in its surviving fragmentary form or in the completion presented in Appendix II/3". If the fragment is used, he recommends "following the historical precedent of the original edition and appending the Chorale".
- **The chorale.** In the 1751 print it is titled *Wenn wir in höchsten Nöten sein* (BWV 668a). *Vor deinen Thron tret ich hiermit* (BWV 668) is the related version that survives as an incomplete manuscript copy.
- **Walter Kolneder**, *Die Kunst der Fuge: Mythen des 20. Jahrhunderts*, 5 parts (Wilhelmshaven: Heinrichshofen, 1977; Taschenbücher zur Musikwissenschaft 42–45). Alfred Dürr's review calls it a demolition of 20th-century myths; it was not read.

**Why this matters for dynamics [D].** If the piece is played as an incomplete movement, the dynamic plan cannot rely on a "final climax". The largest textural event available is the triple combination after bar 233, and it is cut off.

### 3.2 Bringing out the subject: for and against

- **For [S]:** Quantz (III §13; VII §28) and Türk (VI.iii §50), quoted above.
- **For, in modern pedagogy [P]:** the standard advice in piano teaching, e.g. Living Pianos, "How to learn a fugue". This is a non-scholarly source and is cited only as representative of the practice.
- **Against or qualifying [I].**
  - **Charles Rosen.** I could not read *Piano Notes* (2002). A blogger paraphrases Rosen there as holding that emphasizing every subject entry is not what Bach expected: the instruments of the time had less dynamic range, players and students read the score, and the subject is not the most interesting element. This is **unverified second-hand**.
  - What is verified is Rosen's introduction to *Bach: The Fugue* (Oxford Keyboard Classics, 1975), p. 3, as quoted by Kerman (pp. xviii–xix): "Only the performer at the keyboard is in a position to appreciate the movement of the voices, their blending and their separation, their interaction and their contrasts. A fugue of Bach can be fully understood only by the one who plays it, not only heard but felt through the muscles and nerves. Part of the essential conception of the fugue is the way in which voices that the fingers can feel to be individual and distinct are heard as part of an inseparable harmony."
  - Rosen elsewhere (*Critical Entertainments*, Harvard UP, 2000, p. 47, quoted by Kerman p. 46): the *Art of Fugue* "must, indeed, be played many times before its deceptive lucidity can be penetrated".
  - The implication, which is my reading [D]: the ideal is an *inseparable harmony* of distinct lines, not a spotlight on the subject.
- **Hewitt [P]** differentiates subjects by *articulation* rather than volume. In Contrapunctus 8, the contrast between subjects lets them "easily stand out from each other", so "we must also choose a different articulation for each". In Contrapunctus 14 she notes that Subject II is "more flowing to stand out easily against the first subject": contrast of character, not loudness (https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dc.asp?dc=D_CDA67980).
- **Where they agree and disagree.** The 18th-century writers clearly endorse *Nachdruck* for subjects. Modern critics object to the *piano-era habit* of a constant forte subject over a soft background. That habit is not what Quantz describes: he asks for emphasis at entries and softening of the preceding notes, plus emphasis of dissonances in any voice (VII §29), which often falls in non-subject voices.

### 3.3 Texture as the source of intensity [I/D]

- **[S]** Quantz XVII.vi §15 explicitly ties growth to "Vermehrung der Stimmen", and §17 makes texture the harpsichordist's main dynamic tool.
- **[I]** Kerman (p. 116) notes in another context that Bach drew an extreme contrast between sections in Contrapunctus 14 ("allowing for one equivocal exception: the unfinished Contrapunctus 14").
- **[D]** In this fugue the intensity comes from three sources:
  - density: stretti, and the combinations at bars 148 and 233
  - register: the high soprano entries at the bar-180 climax that Hewitt identifies
  - dissonance and chromaticism: B-A-C-H and its inversion at bar 222
- **[D]** These generate rising intensity without any added crescendo, so the performer's dynamic layer can stay modest and *follow* the texture.

## 4. Actionable questions for four hands, one voice per hand [D unless marked]

### 4.1 Overall shape

- **Q1. Where is the architectural peak if the fugue stops at bar 239?** Candidate high points:
  - the Section II stretto at bar 180 (Hewitt)
  - the first entry of B-A-C-H at bar 193
  - the bass inversion at bar 222
  - the triple combination after bar 233

  A plan in which Section I stays mostly mezzo piano to mezzo forte (Quantz's "mezzo piano" baseline for consonance), Section II grows through density, and Section III turns *inward* rather than louder would let the break land in an intense but not climactic place. **Rehearse two versions**, "break at peak" and "break in withdrawal", and choose.
- **Q2. Section beginnings.** Türk §14: after a full cadence, the first note of a new period is marked more than after a half cadence. Bar 193 follows a cadence (Hewitt: "After a cadence in G minor ... in comes the third subject"), which argues for a clear but not loud new start. Bar 233 is a half-close leading into the combination.

### 4.2 Relative voice levels and roles

- **Q3. A default hierarchy?** The proposal: *no fixed hierarchy*, with a rotating "Nachdruck" instead.
  - The hand that has an entry marks its first notes (Quantz VII §28, especially for the long-note Subject I).
  - The other three soften slightly *before* the entry if it is not preceded by a rest (Quantz VII §28, last sentence). This is a concrete, historically grounded voicing technique.
- **Q4. Dissonance before subject?** Quantz VII §29 and XVII.vi §16 say dissonances "in dieser oder jener Stimme" need emphasis. When a free counterpoint creates a suspension against a subject note, the counterpoint may momentarily lead. Mark the 4–3, 7–6 and 9–8 suspensions in each part; the hand that holds the dissonance plays it with weight and resolves it lighter (C. P. E. Bach I.3 §29).
- **Q5. Grading dissonances.** Use Quantz's three classes as a rough guide. Mild suspensions get mezzo forte. Diminished-7th and augmented-6th sonorities (likely around the chromatic B-A-C-H passages) get the strongest weight, always proportioned to the local level (XVII.vi §14).

### 4.3 Shaping each line horizontally

- **Q6. Subject I in long notes.** On the piano these notes decay, so a held whole note cannot "grow" as Quantz allows for ties (VII §29). Options:
  - (a) Voice the attack slightly above the surrounding level and let the *other* voices' movement under it supply the motion.
  - (b) Time the moving voices so that the dissonance they make against the held note is heard while the note still sounds.
  - (c) Accept the decay as part of the line's shape, a diminuendo within each long note, and "re-start" each following note at a matched level so the line does not sag.

  Hofmann's harpsichord remedy (ornaments "to sustain a pitch which would otherwise decay too rapidly", BA 5207 p. XIV) is a period solution. It is optional on piano and probably unnecessary.
- **Q7. Subject II, the running subject.** Shape it by groups toward metrically strong goals (Türk §13: graded good beats), not by uniform volume. Consider whether its character, being "more flowing" in Hewitt's word, is better shown by articulation than by loudness.
- **Q8. B-A-C-H.** Chromatic notes "zu einer fremden Tonart" (Quantz XVII.vi §14) and notes outside the scale (Türk §15 d) receive weight. Do you mark each chromatic step of B-A-C-H, or only the A and H, which are the leading-tone dissonances? Agree on this between the two players, since the subject migrates between them.

### 4.4 Entries, stretti, combinations

- **Q9. Stretti (e.g., bar 180, alto and soprano both in primo).** The primo player must balance their own two hands. Usually the later entry is marked and the earlier one, now in mid-subject, recedes slightly.
- **Q10. Combinations (bar 148; bar 233 and after).** Decide whether all subjects are equally present ("inseparable harmony", Rosen) or rotate prominence. For the final bars, consider equal presence, because the audience's last impression before the silence is the three-way combination.

### 4.5 Cadences and the final bars

- **Q11. Cadences.** C. P. E. Bach (I.3 §29) and Quantz (§14) treat deceptive cadences as places for forte. Find any deceptive resolutions and decide whether to treat them as dynamic events.
- **Q12. Bars 233–239.**
  - Does the passage diminish into the break, so that the fragment fades as if it had been abandoned?
  - Or is it sustained at full presence and cut off, so the listener hears an interruption?

  Hewitt's "Then the music stops ... takes our breath away" suggests the latter effect. Duo Stephanie & Saar let it stop "mid air" (Hugill). Agree on *how* the last sound is released: pedal or none, and whether all four hands lift together. The silence that follows is part of the dynamics.
- **Q13. After the break.** The options:
  - silence only (Hewitt's position before her chorale; Duo Stephanie & Saar)
  - the chorale BWV 668a/668, as in the 1751 print and Hofmann's recommendation; note that the chorale does not fit four-hands-one-voice-per-hand exactly, and its dynamic level should probably start *below* the fugue's last level
  - a completion (Moroney, Tovey, others)
  - another piece, such as the augmentation canon (Duo Stephanie & Saar)

### 4.6 Balance between the two players at one keyboard

- **Q14. Distribution.** The natural layout (primo = soprano + alto; secondo = tenor + bass) means each player balances one outer and one inner voice. Hofmann's two-harpsichord layout (player 1 = descant + tenor; player 2 = alto + bass) is an alternative worth testing, because then each player holds one upper and one lower line. At one keyboard it is physically awkward, but it shows that the voice-to-player mapping is a dynamic decision. Hofmann even swaps the inner voices in places "to avoid obscuring the entrance of the subject" (BA 5207, p. XII).
- **Q15. Register bias.** On a modern grand the bass carries more and the treble cuts through, while the middle register (alto and tenor) is where lines vanish. Inner voices may need a slightly *higher* nominal dynamic than the outer ones simply to be heard as equal.
- **Q16. Pedal and the inner-voice crossing zone.** Where alto and tenor cross or share pitches, as in Hofmann's bar-32 problem in Contrapunctus 12, decide who yields. Kohn's no-pedal practice shows that pedal-free clarity is a documented option for this repertoire. One pedal shared by the secondo player (who normally pedals in four-hand music) affects all four voices, so any pedalling must be negotiated.
- **Q17. Rehearsal method.** Rehearse each pair, then each voice alone, then with one voice muted at a time. Hewitt's "singing each voice in turn" is her documented study method for the cycle.
