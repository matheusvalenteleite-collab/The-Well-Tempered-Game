# Articulation in Bach's unfinished fugue (BWV 1080, "Contrapunctus 14" / "Fuga a 3 Soggetti"): sources, treatises, scholarship, performers

Research report for a four-hand piano performance (one voice per hand). Compiled 21 October 2026.

**Labels used throughout.**
- **[S]** what a primary source or treatise says.
- **[I]** scholarly interpretation.
- **[P]** performers' practice or statements.
- **[D]** my own synthesis or recommendation.

**Verification policy.** I quote only text I read in a fetched source, and the URL is given in the bibliography. "Unverified" means I could not confirm a detail (wording, paragraph or page) in this session. In the KölnKlavier transcriptions, page numbers in angle brackets are the pages of the original 18th-century editions, as that site reproduces them.

## 1. What the sources say

### 1.1 The sources and their status
- **[S]** The fugue survives in Bach's autograph, Mus. ms. autogr. Bach P 200, *Beilage* 3 (Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin).
  - Moroney's Henle critical notes (1989) describe it as "Five loose leaves, written on one side of the paper only. No title. Keyboard score, written on two staves."
  - At the break, "after bar 239", stands the note "N.B. Ueber dieser Fuge, wo der Nahme BACH im Contrasubject angebracht worden, ist der Verfasser gestorben", which Moroney gives as "in C. P. E. Bach's hand?".
  - On the back of leaf 5, possibly in Agricola's hand, is "und einen andern Grund Plan".
- **[I]** Moroney concludes that the print "is derived without revision from this Ms (although there must have been an intermediate Ms in open score, prepared for the engraver); this autograph, and not the print, is therefore the primary source for the unfinished fugue" (Henle HN 423, Comments).
- **[S]** In the 1751/2 print the piece is "pp. 61–65, Fuga a 3 Soggetti". The print is shorter than the autograph:
  - "239: Ms ends here (6 bars after the ending in the print)".
  - "233: print ends on first beat" (Moroney, Henle Comments).
  - Klaus Hofmann's Bärenreiter preface likewise says the fragment "appears to have been abridged by seven bars in the print".
- **[I] Disagreement: was the fugue finished?**
  - Wolff ("The Last Fugue: Unfinished?", *Current Musicology* 19, 1975, 71–77; rev. in *Bach: Essays on His Life and Music*, 1991, 259–264) argues that a lost "fragment x" held the four-subject combination, so the work was essentially complete.
  - Anatoly Milka (*Bach-Jahrbuch* 100, 2014) argues from the ruling, the calligraphy and the paper that the first four pages are a fair copy ("Reinschrift") intended as engraver's copy. Only the fifth page is draft-like. He also disputes Butler's reading of Wolff.
  - Gregory Butler ("Ordering Problems in J. S. Bach's Art of Fugue Resolved", *Musical Quarterly* 69, 1983, 44–61) showed from the pagination that six pages were reserved for this fugue as the 14th piece, after Contrapunctus 13.
  - Leonhardt (1952) had doubted that the fugue belonged to the cycle at all. Moroney says Butler's study "should therefore have laid to rest the erroneous idea that Bach did not intend the unfinished fugue to form part of The Art of Fugue (an idea unfortunately supported by certain earlier writings by Gustav Leonhardt)" (Cal Performances notes, 2013).
- **[D] Why this matters for articulation.** If the autograph is (mostly) a fair copy, as Milka argues, then its sparing notation of slurs and ties reflects Bach's intentions rather than drafting haste. If it is a composing score, as Wolff argues, the absence of marks says less.

### 1.2 Are there articulation marks?
- **[S] In the Art of Fugue generally, yes, but few, and they differ between the sources.**
  - Hofmann's Bärenreiter preface reports that in Contrapunctus 6 the edition adds "ornaments and articulation marks that are only found in the autograph". His footnote: "Slurs rendered in our edition as dotted lines in bars 50–52, 54 and 69 are lacking in the original print but are found in the autograph."
  - Moroney reports the reverse for Contrapunctus 2: "The slurs in bars 4–21, all voices, are missing in the Ms." That is, they are in the print only.
  - His notes also list many single slur variants in Contrapunctus 8, 9, 11, 13 and the canons. Example: "39 S, T: appoggiature not slurred".
- **[S] For the unfinished fugue specifically**, Moroney's list of manuscript and print variants contains no slur variants at all. It lists only:
  - **missing ties in the print:** "89–90 T, 110 B 1–2, 111 B 1–2, 113–114 A, 189–190 T, 205–206 B, 214–215 B, 216–217 A, 219–220 A, 222–223 A, 224–225 T, 226 T 2–3";
  - **missing dots:** "(many dots are missing from the engraving, but have not been mentioned here)". The context suggests these are rhythmic augmentation dots, but **that reading is my inference**.
  - **ornaments:** e.g. "212 T 1: orn. E present (not in Ms)".
- **[D] Unverified conclusion.** I could not view the facsimile. The absence of slur variants in a detailed critical report strongly suggests that Beilage 3 carries essentially no slurs or staccato marks. Its notated "articulation" is then confined to ties, rests and ornaments. Treat this as **unverified** until checked against the facsimile or NBA VIII/2 critical report.
- **[I] The common claim is slightly overstated.** It is often said that the work has no articulation at all. Gerd Zacher's note says Bach left out articulation and dynamics; I read this in a search summary, not verbatim. Moroney's and Hofmann's reports show sparse but real slurs elsewhere in the cycle.

### 1.3 A "tie" that is not a tie (bar 114)
- **[S]** Moroney, Ms variants, bar 114 S: the four soprano notes A, A, G, F "were added as an afterthought. The mark which has traditionally been interpreted as a tie between the 2 As is in fact the halfnote rest (sitting on the 5th line) from the early version. The 2 As are not tied in the print either."
  - Print variant, bar 114 T: "2 half note As, tied to each other and to the As before and after; clearly a misreading of the Ms."
- **[D] Practical consequence.** Re-strike the repeated A in the soprano at bar 114. Check your edition: older editions follow the misreading.

### 1.4 Meter (C vs. alla breve), barring and what they imply
- **[S]** Moroney gives the print's signature as "t–s C" (common time) for the Fuga a 3 Soggetti.
  - His stated convention is to list the "original time signature, when it differs from the present edition". That implies his own edition prints something else, presumably alla breve, but **I could not verify** this against the score.
  - He gives no time signature for Beilage 3. **Unverified** which sign the autograph carries.
- **[S]** On barring: "209–28: barlines only every 4 half notes; hence the system of half bars used here (see remarks to Cp 1)". In other words, the autograph uses double-length bars of four half notes in that passage. This is the same notation Bach used in Ms A for Contrapuncti 1 and 2 ("bars twice as long, with 4 half notes").
- **[S]** Context in the cycle. Moroney reads several print signatures of C as engraver's errors for alla breve: Contrapunctus 5 ("clearly an engraver's error"), and Contrapuncti 9, 10 and 13 rectus ("I have regularized the signature … giving [alla breve] each time").
  - **Moroney on note values and tempo** [I]: "Bach changed the level of notation for almost all the fugues originally notated in 16th notes, but unfortunately he died before finishing his notational revisions so he did not apply the slower note values to the last couple of pieces. This means that some pieces look as if they should be played fast but in fact they should not." (Cal Performances notes)
- **[S] Quantz on alla breve tempo.** Quantz, *Versuch* (1752), ch. XVII, Abschnitt VII, §50, pp. 262–263: in alla breve, "welcher, es sey das Zeitmaaß langsam oder geschwinde, allezeit mit einem durchgestrichenen großen C angedeutet wird", the rule is "nur daß alle Noten in demselben noch einmal so geschwind genommen werden, als im gemeinen geraden Tacte".
  - §51, p. 264, gives pulse rates. In alla breve the pulse falls "In einem Allegro, auf jeden Tact ein Pulsschlag … In einem Adagio cantabile auf ein jedes Viertheil ein Pulsschlag".
- **[S] Türk on alla breve weight.** Türk, *Klavierschule* (1789), ch. VI, §47, pp. 360–361: "Je größer die Takttheile oder Hauptzeiten einer Taktart sind, je schwerer muß der Vortrag seyn." Even if one marked fast-metre examples Adagio, "würde ein guter Spieler dieselben Töne nicht so schwer vortragen, als im Allabreve".
- **[D] Synthesis.** Whatever the sign, three things point to a half-note tactus with a weighty ("schwer") delivery for subject I: the stile antico idiom, the double-length barring at bars 209–228, and Moroney's point about unrevised note values. In 18th-century terms, "schwer" is about holding notes their full value, not about loudness (see 2.3). Running quavers should then be heard as subdivisions of a broad half-note pulse, not as a fast surface.

## 2. Eighteenth-century treatises on default touch and articulation

### 2.1 C. P. E. Bach, *Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen*, Part I (1753), ch. 3 "Vom Vortrage"
Verified on the Packard Humanities Institute parallel edition, versuch.cpebach.org (ed. Knoll; translator of Part I Tobias Plebuch). The page does not show 1753/1759/1787 variants. Wikisource transcribes the 1759 printing with the same wording.
- **§5** [S]: "Die Lebhaftigkeit des Allegro wird gemeiniglich in gestoßenen Noten … vorgestellet", and the tender Adagio in "getragenen und geschleiften Noten". C. P. E. Bach stresses that he says "generally", since every kind of note occurs at every tempo.
- **§6** [S]: "Einige Personen spielen klebericht, als wenn sie Leim zwischen den Fingern hätten … Andere … spielen zu kurz; als wenn die Tasten glühend wären … Die Mittelstraße ist die beste; ich rede hievon überhaupt; alle Arten des Anschlages sind zur rechten Zeit gut."
- **§17** [S]: staccato is to be graded ("Man muß mit Unterschied abstoßen") according to note value, tempo and forte or piano. Such notes are "allezeit etwas weniger als die Hälfte gehalten". Staccato occurs mostly on leaps and in fast tempos (paraphrase).
- **§18** [S]: "Die Noten welche geschleift werden sollen, müssen ausgehalten werden".
  - "so kommen die Schleifungen mehrentheils bey gehenden Noten und in langsamer oder gemäßigter Zeit-Maaße vor."
  - Notes of the character of passing tones or appoggiaturas "werden in allerley Zeit-Maaße ohne Andeutung geschleift" (English: "passing tones or appoggiaturas, are slurred in all tempos, even when not indicated").
  - "Bey Figuren von 2 und 4 solcher Noten, kriegt die erste und dritte einen etwas stärkern Druck". The defining subject of that sentence is paraphrased.
- **§22, the default** [S]: "Die Noten, welche weder gestoßen noch geschleift noch ausgehalten werden, unterhält man so lange als ihre Hälfte beträgt", unless "Ten:" (gehalten) is written. The site's English: "Notes that are to be played neither staccato, nor legato, nor sustained are held for half of their notated value".
  - Per the site's second paragraph (paraphrased), this applies mainly to quavers and crotchets in moderate and slow tempos. Such notes should be played with spirit and a gentle push, not weakly.

### 2.2 F. W. Marpurg, *Anleitung zum Clavierspielen* (Berlin: Haude und Spener, 1755)
- **[S] Verified via a secondary source only.** Paul Simmonds quotes the second edition (1765), via Lohmann p. 246:
  - "Sowohl dem schleiffen als Abstossen ist das ordentliche Fortgehen entgegen gesetzet,"
  - "Dieses ordentliche Fortgehen wird, weil es allezeit vorausgesetzet wird, niemahls angezeiget."
  - Simmonds paraphrases the touch itself: the finger leaves the preceding key just before the next note sounds.
- The location in the 1755 edition is **unverified**. A Claremont *Performance Practice Review* article reportedly cites p. 29, but I could not open it.

### 2.3 D. G. Türk, *Klavierschule* (Leipzig and Halle, 1789)
- **The "gewöhnliche Art"** [S, via secondary sources]: "die gewöhnliche Art d.h. weder gestoßen noch geschleift". The finger "is lifted a little earlier from the key than is required by the duration of the note". Türk rejects C. P. E. Bach's "half value" as too short ("Taken in general this kind of playing does not seem to me to be the best").
  - Cited in the Simmonds article to the Haggh translation, pp. 345–346.
  - The paragraph (ch. VI, §40 by most citations) and the German wording are **unverified** first-hand: the Fraktur OCR I could access was unusable.
- **Verified first-hand (KölnKlavier transcription, ch. VI, Abschnitt 3e, "Vom schweren und leichten Vortrage", §§43–51, pp. 358–364)** [S]:
  - **§43**: in heavy delivery each note is "fest (nachdrücklich) angegeben und bis zur völlig verflossenen Dauer der Noten ausgehalten". In light delivery one lifts "den Finger etwas früher, als es die Dauer der Noten bestimmt, von den Tasten". "Schwer" and "leicht" "sich … mehr auf das Aushalten und Absetzen der Töne, als auf die Stärke und Schwäche derselben beziehen".
  - **§45** (p. 360): "Tonstücke, welche zu einem ernsten Zwecke geschrieben sind z.B. Fugen … erfordern einen weit schwerern Vortrag".
  - **§49** (p. 363): "Ein Tonstück mit vielen Dissonanzen muß nämlich schwerer vorgetragen werden". Also: "springende Passagen [werden] noch leichter ausgeführt, als stufenweise fortschreitende".
  - **§50** (pp. 363–364): "Ein Tonstück von Händel, Sebastian Bach &c. muß nachdrücklicher vorgetragen werden, als etwa ein modernes Konzert von Mozart, Kozeluch u.a.m."
  - **§50–51** (the § break is not visible on the page; exact § unverified): "So muß man z.B. in Fugen oder gearbeiteten Tonstücken vorzüglich das Thema (Subjekt) und die nachahmenden Stellen mit Nachdruck vortragen".

### 2.4 J. J. Quantz, *Versuch einer Anweisung die Flöte traversiere zu spielen* (Berlin, 1752)
Verified on the KölnKlavier transcription, with page numbers of the 1752 edition.
- **Ch. XI, §10** (pp. 104–105) [S]: "Es darf nicht scheinen als wenn die Noten zusammen klebeten." Also: "Gedanken, welche an einander hangen sollen, muß man nicht zertheilen: so wie man hingegen diejenigen zertheilen muß, wo sich ein musikalischer Sinn endiget".
- **Ch. XI, §11** (p. 105): "Die ausgehaltenen und schmeichelnden Noten müssen mit einander verbunden; die lustigen und hüpfenden aber abgesetzet, und von einander getrennet werden."
- **Ch. XI, §16** (pp. 107–108):
  - slurred notes in close intervals express "das Schmeichelnde, Traurige, und Zärtliche";
  - "die kurz gestoßenen, oder in entferneten Sprüngen bestehenden Noten" express the merry and bold;
  - "Punctirte und anhaltende Noten drücken das Ernsthafte und Pathetische … aus".
- **Ch. XI, §21** (bad delivery): includes playing "alle Noten ohne Unterschied schleifet oder stößt".
- **Ch. XII, §5** (p. 112): "immer die Hauptnoten ein wenig länger, als die durchgehenden gehöret werden müssen".
- **Ch. XVII, Abschnitt VI, §12** (p. 227): "Dissonanzen überhaupt stärker als die Consonanzen angeschlagen werden müssen". §13: some dissonances more than others.
- **Ch. XVII, Abschnitt VII, §28** (p. 253), directly relevant to subject I:
  - "Ein Hauptsatz, (Thema) zumal in einer Fuge, muß in einer jeden Stimme, und zu allen Zeiten wenn er unvermuthet eintritt, mit Nachdrucke markiret werden; besonder wenn der Anfang davon aus langen Noten bestehet."
  - If no rests precede the entry, "kann man die vorhergehenden Noten in der Stärke des Tones etwas mäßigen".
- **Ch. XVII, Abschnitt VII, §§50–51**: alla breve, see 1.4.
- **Unverified.** I did not locate first-hand Quantz's specific remarks on ties and syncopations, on "Anschlagen" of tied notes, or on leaps beyond XI §16. Do not cite paragraph numbers for those without checking.

### 2.5 Kirnberger and stile antico or alla breve
- **Unverified.** Kirnberger's remarks on alla breve are in *Die Kunst des reinen Satzes in der Musik*, II/1 (1776), in the section on metre. They are commonly paraphrased as describing a serious, weighty delivery at twice the speed of the note values, linked to church style.
  - I could not obtain readable text: the archive.org Fraktur OCR was unusable, and Zeno.org returned 503 for Sulzer's *Allgemeine Theorie*, article "Takt".
  - Quote nothing from Kirnberger until checked in Beach and Thym's translation (Yale, 1982), which is on archive.org (lending only).
- **[D]** Quantz XVII.vii §50 and Türk §§45, 47 and 49 already establish verified period support for the claim that a fugue in alla breve with many dissonances calls for the "heavy" (fully held, emphatic) delivery.

### 2.6 [I/D] Agreement and disagreement among the treatises
- **Default touch for unmarked notes:**
  - C. P. E. Bach: half value (§22);
  - Türk: lift only "a little earlier" than the value, explicitly against the half-value rule;
  - Marpurg: "ordentliche Fortgehen", a finger lifted just before the next note.
  - These three positions differ, and it is not one doctrine. All, however, treat unmarked notes as **non-legato by default**, and all qualify this by context.
- **What overrides the default:**
  - **towards sustained playing:** heavy or serious genres such as fugues, slow or large-beat metres including alla breve, dissonance, and stepwise passing notes (C. P. E. Bach §18; Türk §§43–49; Quantz XI §16);
  - **towards detachment:** leaps and quick lively figures (C. P. E. Bach §17; Quantz XI §§11, 16; Türk §49).
- Most of these sources are 1752–1789, after J. S. Bach. C. P. E. Bach and Marpurg are close to his circle; Türk is a generation later. Simmonds argues that this non-legato default held well before 1750, but that is his interpretation.

## 3. Modern scholarship

- **John Butt, *Bach Interpretation: Articulation Marks in Primary Sources of J. S. Bach*** (Cambridge, 1990).
  - The table of contents (Google Books) includes "Articulation marks in keyboard playing" (p. 51) and "Bach's keyboard articulation and the development of the keyboard idiom" (p. 164). It also has chapters on slurs as phrase marks (p. 180) and on articulation in analysis and interpretation (p. 186).
  - **Not read in this session.** I cannot report Butt's specific conclusions about keyboard default touch first-hand. Kimberly Marshall's review (*Performance Practice Review* 5/2, 1992) is behind a block.
  - **[I] Butt on instrumentation.** In his booklet essay for Podger and Brecon Baroque (Channel Classics CCS SA 38316) he writes: "virtually all the cycle is set out in such a way that it can be played on the keyboard, but the open score format of the original invites interpretation from any potential instrumental combination (or, indeed, even just a soundless reading by the highly trained musician)." **Contrast Moroney below.**
- **Davitt Moroney, ed., *Die Kunst der Fuge* (Henle HN 423, 1989), preface and Comments.** These are the key source facts used above: the status of Beilage 3, the bar-114 "tie", the ties missing in the print, the print's time signature, the barring at bars 209–228, and the Ms extending 6 bars past the print. Further points:
  - Moroney supplied "No fingerings … since modern systems of piano fingering are not appropriate to this music and, indeed, are usually misleading for harpsichordists".
  - He completes the fugue with 30 bars, arguing that the missing portion "cannot have been intended to contain more than about 40 bars".
- **Moroney, programme notes (Cal Performances, 7 April 2013), on voicing, which bears on piano articulation** [I/P]:
  - "Since the theme in a Bach fugue is precisely what never changes, 'bringing it out' on the piano is essentially drawing attention to what is static in the work".
  - "Bachian counterpoint absolutely requires equality of treatment among the themes, not bringing out one theme at the expense of the others."
  - On one brain at the keyboard: "the immeasurable benefits for the counterpoint when a single brain is in charge not just of playing all the parts but overseeing their exact harmonic and rhythmic interrelationships". **This directly challenges a four-hand, voice-per-hand conception.**
- **Disagreement with the treatises.** Quantz XVII.vii §28 and Türk (§50–51) both say the fugue theme should be delivered "mit Nachdruck". Moroney argues against "bringing out" themes dynamically. One reconciliation: the period "Nachdruck" can be achieved by weight and duration ("schwer" delivery, Türk §43) rather than by volume. That reconciliation is my synthesis [D], not either author's claim.
- **Klaus Hofmann, ed., *Die Kunst der Fuge* (Bärenreiter BA 5207, 1998; NBA Urtext), preface** [I]:
  - "more recent scholarly studies have proved that The Art of Fugue is indeed music for keyboard, preferably a harpsichord".
  - He notes passages that exceed two hands, and that Bach "himself pointed out one way of realizing these highly complex contrapuntal edifices in performance by arranging the Three-voice Mirror Fugue for two harpsichords."
  - He prints his own arrangement of Contrapunctus 12 for two harpsichords, in which "Each harpsichord is assigned two voices", with the voices temporarily exchanged at bars 25–32 "to avoid obscuring the entrance of the subject".
  - He includes Schulenberg's completion of no. 20 (from *The Keyboard Music of J. S. Bach*, pp. 371–373).
- **Gustav Leonhardt, *The Art of Fugue: Bach's Last Harpsichord Work*** (The Hague: Nijhoff, 1952).
  - [I] This is the classic argument for harpsichord. Moroney: "Much of the overwhelming evidence proving this point has been published by Gustav Leonhardt."
  - Leonhardt also doubted that the unfinished fugue belonged to the cycle (see 1.1).
  - I could not access the book's text, which is lending-only on archive.org. Its remarks on articulation, if any, are unverified.
- **Christoph Wolff:** see 1.1. I found nothing by Wolff specifically on articulation in this fugue.
- **Gregory Butler:** "Ordering Problems…" (1983), see 1.1. Also "Scribes, Engravers, and Notation Styles: The Final Disposition of Bach's Art of Fugue", in *About Bach* (Urbana, 2008), p. 122 n. 27, as cited by Milka.
- **David Schulenberg, *The Keyboard Music of J. S. Bach*** (Schirmer 1992; London: Gollancz 1993; 2nd ed. Routledge 2006). Chapter 2 is a general performance-practice chapter covering instrument, articulation, rhythm and ornament; the final chapter covers the *Musical Offering* and the Art of Fugue. The 2nd edition revises his completion of the *Fuga a tre soggetti*. His specific articulation advice is **unverified** (not read).
- **David Ledbetter, *Bach's Well-tempered Clavier: The 48 Preludes and Fugues*** (Yale, 2002). It has a chapter on "Stile antico", useful for the alla breve or stile antico subject type. Its content is **not read** and unverified.
- **Peter Williams.** I found nothing of his specifically on articulation in BWV 1080 in this session.
- **Joseph Kerman, *The Art of Fugue: Bach Fugues for Keyboard, 1715–1750*** (UC Press, 2005; open access 2015). It covers Contrapuncti 1 and 10, not 14. Kerman mentions the unfinished fugue only in passing, as an extreme sectional contrast ("allowing for one equivocal exception: the unfinished Contrapunctus 14").
- **A specific article on articulating the three subjects of this fugue: none found.** No article, dissertation or monograph turned up that deals specifically with this. That gap should be stated honestly.

## 4. Practical questions, subject by subject

The structural bar numbers below are from Hewitt's Hyperion notes [P]:
- subject II enters in the alto at bar 114;
- subjects I and II are first combined at bar 148, with a notable combination at bar 169 (F major);
- the climax of that section comes at bar 180;
- the B-A-C-H subject is inverted in the bass at bar 222;
- all three subjects are combined at bar 233.

Moroney gives bars 233–239 as the combination of the three themes in the lower three voices.

### 4.1 Subject I (long notes, stile antico)
- **[S]** Quantz XVII.vii §28 says the subject "mit Nachdrucke markiret", "besonder wenn der Anfang davon aus langen Noten bestehet". Türk §43 says heavy delivery means full value ("bis zur völlig verflossenen Dauer"). Türk §45: fugues require "einen weit schwerern Vortrag". Türk §47: alla breve is heavy.
- **[P]** Hewitt: "The first subject is drastically simple, hugely expressive despite its bleakness."
- **[D] Recommendations.**
  - Hold the minims and semibreves for their full value. Separate them only by the smallest "ordentliche Fortgehen" gap, enough for each new attack to be heard as a new syllable.
  - Do not use a lift that turns the subject into detached blocks.
  - On the leap (the fifth), Quantz XI §16 and C. P. E. Bach §17 associate leaps with detachment. In this grave, heavy context, I would make a slightly clearer separation before the leap rather than a short note. This is an inference from the treatises, not something they state about this subject.
  - Where the subject repeats a pitch, re-strike it clearly. See bar 114 (1.3): those As are **not** tied.

### 4.2 Subject II (running quavers)
- **[S]** Moroney describes the combined texture as "the constant flow of eighth-note movement". C. P. E. Bach §18: slurs occur mostly "bey gehenden Noten und in langsamer oder gemäßigter Zeit-Maaße", and in groups of two or four the first and third get "einen etwas stärkern Druck". Quantz XII §5: "Hauptnoten ein wenig länger, als die durchgehenden".
- **[P]** Hewitt: subject II is "more flowing to stand out easily against the first subject."
- **[D] Recommendations.**
  - There are no slurs in the source (unverified, see 1.2), so this is an interpretive choice.
  - **Reading A:** stepwise quavers played *quasi-legato in small groups* (2 + 2 or a 4-group), with a light lift at changes of direction or before leaps. This is supported by C. P. E. Bach §18 and Quantz XI §16.
  - **Reading B:** an even "ordentliche Fortgehen" non-legato, with metric accentuation (first and third of four slightly weighted). This keeps the quavers distinct from the held long notes of subject I.
  - Both are defensible. Choose one and keep it the same for every entry of subject II, across both players.
  - Avoid the extreme that a critic heard in one duo performance (see §5).

### 4.3 Subject III (B-A-C-H, chromatic)
- **[S]** Türk §49: many dissonances, heavier delivery. Quantz XVII.vi §12: dissonances "stärker als die Consonanzen angeschlagen". C. P. E. Bach §18: passing and appoggiatura-like notes are slurred even without marks.
- **[P]** Hewitt: "This is no less than Bach's name in musical notation… To the four notes are added a cadential gesture and trill." Moroney's completion uses a triple stretto of B-A-C-H in bars 258–261.
- **[D] Recommendations.**
  - Play the four chromatic notes connected, close to legato. Each semitone step functions as a passing or appoggiatura-like note, so this follows C. P. E. Bach §18.
  - Separate slightly before the cadential tail.
  - Treat the trill in period manner, starting from the upper note. This is general period practice and is not specified for this bar in the sources I read.
  - In the inversion (bass, bar 222), keep the same articulation, so that recto and inverso are heard as one idea.

### 4.4 Suspensions and resolutions
- **[S]** Quantz XVII.vi §12: dissonances are struck more strongly than consonances. Quantz XI §10: what belongs together must not be divided. C. P. E. Bach §18: "geschleift" notes are held, and slurs over two-note figures give the first note "einen etwas stärkern Druck".
- **[D] Recommendations.**
  - Re-strike the dissonant note on the beat with weight. On piano that means a slightly fuller attack, not a sforzando.
  - Join it to its resolution, with the resolution lighter.
  - Lift after the resolution if the voice continues, as a breath.
  - On piano, the preparation (the tied note) decays. The dissonance is therefore created by the *other* voice's attack: the voice moving against the tie must be clearly articulated at that moment.

### 4.5 Ties and syncopations
- **[S]** Moroney lists twelve ties missing from the print (see 1.2). Use an autograph-based edition (Henle HN 423, NBA, or Bärenreiter BA 5207, which follows Beilage 3 for this fugue) to know which ties are Bach's.
- **[P]** Hewitt on ties elsewhere in the cycle (Contrapunctus 10): "the soprano must breathe while the alto holds the tied note." On Contrapunctus 2: "a slight lift before the syncopated note makes the whole thing a bit jazzier." A Gramophone review, which I saw only in a search summary, says her lifting of the fingers after tied notes gave shape and profile to several fugues.
- **[D] Recommendation.** Make a tiny lift **before** a syncopated attack, and release the tie cleanly so the next attack is audible.

### 4.6 Cadences and section ends
- **[S]** Quantz XI §10: divide "wo sich ein musikalischer Sinn endiget". Quantz XVII.vii §28: before an unprepared entry, "die vorhergehenden Noten in der Stärke des Tones etwas mäßigen".
- **[D] Recommendation.** Let each voice "breathe" at its own cadence. Hewitt describes singing each voice separately to find its breathing points; this is a paraphrase of her Hyperion notes.

### 4.7 Voice independence on piano and the stopping point
- **[D]** With one voice per hand, independence comes mainly from **staggered articulation**: each voice's lifts fall at different moments. It does not need to come from dynamic layering, which Moroney criticises.
- **Proposed dynamic policy:** a common dynamic level, with subjects given Quantz's and Türk's "Nachdruck" through **duration and weight**. Episodes and free counterpoints are allowed more lightness and shape, following Moroney's "flesh around the bones".
- **Pedal:** minimal or none in the fugue. Hewitt mentions judicious pedal only in Contrapunctus 13, and warns that Contrapunctus 12 needs "a great deal of clarity if it is not to sound muddy." A Gramophone quote describes Aimard's fugues as using minimal pedalling and finely articulated ("neatly chiselled") lines; this came from a search summary and is a critic's description.
- **[P] Where to stop.**
  - Hewitt: "I have chosen to leave it incomplete", followed after a long silence by the chorale BWV 668a.
  - Moroney plays his own 30-bar completion.
  - DUO Stephanie & Saar end the fugue unfinished, "stopping mid air" (Planet Hugill, quoted on the label page), then play the Canon per augmentationem.
  - Decide where to stop: bar 239 (autograph) or bar 233 (print).

## 5. Four hands or two keyboards

- **[S] Bach's own two-keyboard precedent.** P 200 Beilage 2 contains Bach's two-harpsichord arrangements of Contrapunctus 13 (rectus and inversus), with a newly composed fourth voice. These are the "Fuga a 2. Clav." pieces of the print. Hofmann treats them as Bach's own pointer to sharing contrapuntal textures between two players.
  - Moroney's view differs: the arrangements "have no logical place in Bach's tightly constructed cycle" (Cal Performances notes). He therefore accepts them as authentic arrangements but not as part of the cycle.
- **[S] The unfinished fugue itself:** no source discusses a two-keyboard or four-hand version. It is written "on two staves" as a solo keyboard score (Moroney).
- **[I] Moroney's "single brain" argument** (see 3) is the main scholarly objection to splitting the voices. Butt's view that the open score invites any instrumental realization is the main counter-position.
- **[P] Duo recordings** (from the Wikipedia discography; verify before citing):
  - Joseph & Grete Dichler, 2 pianos (Westminster, 1954);
  - Ton Koopman & Tini Mathot, harpsichords (Erato 0630-16173-2, 1994); I did not find their notes on how voices are divided;
  - Gavin Black & George Hazelrigg, two harpsichords (2009);
  - DUO Stephanie & Saar (Stephanie Ho, Saar Ahuvia), piano duo (New Focus FCR181, 2017). The label page quotes a review saying Contrapuncti 1–8, 10 and 11 are four hands at one piano, and 9, 12 and 13 are on two pianos. The setup for Contrapunctus 14 is not stated there.
  - Alexander & Daykin recorded an early-20th-century two-piano transcription (Connoisseur Society, 1994); the transcriber is not named on Wikipedia.
- **[P] A cautionary review.** The American Record Guide review of DUO Stephanie & Saar, as quoted on the label page, praised "articulation and phrasing appropriate" to the score but criticised "the sudden eruption of hard-bitten staccato in Contrapunctus 14". Planet Hugill noted the duo's "uniformity of tone and articulation" for fugue subjects.
  - **[D]** This is evidence that heavy detachment in this fugue strikes informed listeners as out of style, consistent with Türk §§43–49.
- **[D] Practical model from Hofmann's two-harpsichord Contrapunctus 12.**
  - Assign voices so that each player has an outer and an inner voice, e.g. Primo soprano and tenor, Secondo alto and bass.
  - Consider exchanging inner voices where hands collide or an entry would be masked, as Hofmann does at bars 25–32.
  - With one voice per hand, decide in rehearsal a **shared articulation lexicon** for each subject, because consistency across four hands replaces the "single brain" Moroney prizes.
- **Other recordings:**
  - Gould 1981 (piano, includes Contrapunctus 14);
  - Koroliov (Tacet, 1990, piano);
  - Aimard (DG, 2008);
  - Walcha (organ, 1956);
  - Leonhardt (harpsichord, 1953 and 1969);
  - Emerson Quartet (DG, 2003);
  - Fretwork (HMU 907296, 2002);
  - Musica Antiqua Köln (1984);
  - Brecon Baroque (Channel CCS SA 38316).
  - I did not listen to any recordings in this session, so I make **no claims** about their articulation. For Zhu Xiao-Mei I verified nothing.

## 6. Summary of disagreements to keep visible

1. **Default length of unmarked notes:** C. P. E. Bach says half value; Türk says only slightly shortened; Marpurg says the "ordentliche Fortgehen". These are not one doctrine.
2. **"Bringing out" subjects:** Quantz and Türk call for "Nachdruck"; Moroney argues for equality and against piano-style highlighting.
3. **Keyboard vs. ensemble:** Hofmann, Moroney and Leonhardt argue for keyboard (harpsichord); Butt (2016 notes) says the open score invites any realisation.
4. **Whether the fugue belongs to the cycle or was finished:** Leonhardt (1952) doubted it belongs; Butler (1983) and Moroney say it belongs; Wolff (1975/1991) and Milka (2014) say it was essentially finished; Hewitt says "For me it does" belong.
5. **Meter of the print vs. the edition:** the print has C; Moroney regularises elsewhere to alla breve; for this fugue the autograph sign is unverified.
