## Book I no. 1, C major, BWV 846 (pp. 143–151)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Draws on two traditions (ch. 3 §4). (1) The toccata arpeggiata, going back at least to Kapsberger's chitarrone examples (Rome 1604): a "harmonic recherche" of ties and dissonances, the plucked-string counterpart of organ toccatas di durezze e ligature. (2) Beginners' preludes in basic chord shapes: Purcell's prelude in *A Choice Collection of Lessons* (London 1696), and, closer to Bach, J.C.F. Fischer's *Musicalischer Parnassus* (Augsburg 1738) (p. 143). Fischer's arpeggio preludes from the 'Clio' and 'Polymnia' suites belong to a tradition of notating "as 'dry' effects that are meant to sound 'wet'" (Ex.7.1) (p. 144). Note 1: Anna Magdalena's 1725 copy of Couperin's *Les bergeries* likewise simplifies notated tenues into plain semiquavers (p. 144 n.1). At one level the prelude is "an elaboration of the most elementary form of improvisation" (p. 144). It is hard to know how much of the significance read into it is justified (p. 144).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The opening four-bar harmonic formula was used by Italian cantata composers from Carissimi onwards to open a recitative (p. 145). The final version gets a stronger shape from sequential repetition (bb.8–11, cf. 16–19) and from consonant points of arrival that mark off its sentences (shown with commas in Ex.7.2b) (p. 145). The last-inversion major-7th chords at b.8 and b.16 give impetus to key changes (p. 143).
- **Harmony/figured-bass abstract:** Ex.7.2 gives figured-bass skeletons of (a) the first and (b) the final versions; the example is music only, and the prose around it survives intact [displaced, restored] (p. 145). The final version rests on the two commonest prelude formulas, "a descending scale and a pedal"; the dominant pedal from b.24 is dramatised by the chromatic bb.22–3 [filled] (pp. 146–147). At b.23 the question is whether b or c' is the harmony note: Schenker takes c' as inessential, whereas the unfigured-bass rule that the minor 6th degree takes a 6/4/3 chord makes the b inessential; a diminished-7th reading "is by no means to be ruled out", and the ambiguity may be intentional [filled] (pp. 146–147).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The first version (Ex.7.2a) continues the opening formula with a descending bass scale. It has a station on the dominant halfway down and a visit to the subdominant at the end, but no satisfying rhetorical structure (p. 145). The final version shows Bach "perfecting pieces by addition rather than recomposition" (p. 145). In the earliest version the second note of each left-hand bar was a plain semiquaver (p. 144).
- **Performance:** The player brings out the harmonic structure through subtle rubato, savouring dissonances such as bb.8 and 16 (p. 143). On whether the right-hand broken chords are sustained: Schulenberg (1992 p.167) says play them as written, since Bach could have used tenuto slurs as in BWV 1017 (Siciliano) and BWV 979 (Andante). Against this, harpsichord tradition used seamless repetition and did not notate it exactly. L. leans to the sustained, 'wet' sound (pp. 143–144). Bach's pattern has a fifth part that creates constant cross-rhythms in the RH repetitions (p. 144).
- **Other analysts cited:** Schulenberg (above). Schenker reads the prelude as a series of four-bar phrases. L.: this "seems to impose a Classical model" on a more fluid structure (p. 155).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Four (alto, cantus, tenor, bass) [filled] (p. 147). L. calls the fugue "a study of line, or perhaps melody" [filled] (p. 147). Entries in "four" parts in the quadruple stretto (p. 149).
- **Subject (extent, features):** Where the subject ends is disputed. Smend (1947 p.12) has it end on the e' in mid-b.2, the 14th note, "the sum of the letters BACH" [filled; corrects the guess "possibly Marpurg"] (p. 148). L.: "He may be right", but the semiquavers run on under the second entry without cadencing on the tonic, an indefinite, 'modern' ending (p. 148). Note 22: Marpurg also took e' as the last note (p. 148 n.22). The original version made the first six notes sound like an upbeat to e'. The added dot clarifies the upbeat nature of the first three notes (p. 150). Note 14 (hexachord; numbers 6 and 12, for Froberger, Corelli, Bach) probably applies here (n.14, p. 147–148 range).
- **Answer:** No separate discussion of the answer type; the only point made is the modal allocation of dux and comes by tessitura (see Exposition) [filled] (pp. 147–148). Notes 15–16: Bach uses the black-note canzona rhythm only once, in the bass entry at b.17, preferring the upbeat version elsewhere (as in the Book II D major fugue). Authentic/plagal tessituras are discussed (ch. 5 §1) (nn.15–16).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** Integration is so complete that "hardly a single note is non-thematic". This comes from three ingredients presented in bb.1–2, all related to the interval of a 4th: stepped, leapt, then decorated in semiquavers (a Couperin-style évolution) [filled] (p. 147). Integration comes through stretto and through combining the subject with its semiquaver continuation; Bach uses all of Kirnberger's combinations except the inversion at the 12th [filled] (p. 149). Ex.7.4 shows Kirnberger's combinations of the subject and its continuation: bars 2–3, inverted at the 12th; bar 4; bar 6; bars 11–12 (p. 150). Note 23: 'Verwechselung' (permutation) and alchemy; Johann Theile (n.23).
- **Exposition:** bb.1–7. A procedure "common enough in verset fugues of Kerll and Fischer" makes a "neatly rounded exposition" that leads straight into the first stretto section (p. 147). The procedure is "the old modal procedure": dux and comes are allotted by tessitura, alto and bass taking the dux and cantus and tenor the comes, so the entry order alto–cantus–tenor–bass gives dux–comes–comes–dux [filled] (p. 147).
- **Later entries:** Bass entry b.17 with the canzona rhythm (n.15). Bass variant of the subject at b.15 avoids a rising 7th in the cantus (pp. 150–151). The second half (bb.14–27) has 14 entries, "with one on each of the 7 tone[s]" (sic) [filled] (p. 150).
- **Episodes:** "an episodeless exposition fugue of conservative design": 27 bars divided at b.14 and again at the quarters, giving four expositions of 6–7 bars each [filled] (p. 149).
- **Stretto:** bb.7–14: close stretto at the 4th below (b.7) and the 5th above (b.10). bb.14–19: triple stretto (bb.14–15), then quadruple stretto (bb.15ff). bb.19–27: stretti over pedals (p. 149).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** Four sections: bb.1–7 exposition; bb.7–14 close stretto, moving to the relative minor with more dissonant harmony; bb.14–19 triple/quadruple stretto; bb.19–27 stretti over pedals (p. 149). Pedals: brief supertonic pedal at b.19, a longer dominant pedal at bb.21–2, and the longest, on the tonic, from b.24 to the end (pp. 149–150). The final two bars recall the scale at the end of Froberger's Lament for Ferdinand IV, perhaps climbing to Parnassus or to Buttstett's Shekinah, or just dissolving dense counterpoint into scales (as in the G major fugue and the A major prelude; no book is named, but the context is Book I [filled]) (p. 150).
- **Form reading:** A verset-tradition fugue with separate, clearly functioning sections (p. 149). Note 24: Riemann's eight-bar/ternary readings ignore Bach's cadences. Note 25: W. Korte (1940) sees 5 sections, the fifth being the final pedal bb.24ff, related to Balthasar Neumann and Leibniz (nn.24–25).
- **Genre/models:** Verset fugues of Kerll and Fischer: concise, continuously thematic, no episodes, using stretto and similar devices. Example: Fischer's *Ariadne Musica* Fuga 11, where the countersubject is the subject's inversion (p. 147). Ex.7.3: Corelli Op.3 No.12 Allegro bb.1–2½, and Albinoni in BWV 946 bb.15–19; the surrounding argument is the fugue's integration by stretto and by combining the subject with its semiquaver continuation (see Countersubject(s)) [filled] (p. 149). Notes 18–20: Corelli/Cazzati 'La Casala'; Bach knew Op.3 and wrote BWV 579 (authorship not certain); Albinoni Op.1 No.12 (Venice 1694) is the source of BWV 946 (authorship not certain) (nn.18–20).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The most important revision is the dot added to the subject's 4th note, in the third round of P 415 revisions, probably after 1736 (p. 150). The original version is in NBA V/6.1 pp.128–9 and BA I pp.6–7 (p. 150). Note 26: nothing supports Landowska's view that Bach did not add the dot (n.26). The dot forced changes in b.9, b.12 and b.15, made in this round and in the final round, "probably c.1740" [filled] (p. 150). Bach's final readings (given by Dürr and Jones) are "genuine enhancements": a jagged cantus at b.12 and a bass variant of the subject at b.15 (pp. 150–151).

## Book I no. 2, C minor, BWV 847 (pp. 151–156)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Some have read "the first five of the Clavier-Büchlein Praeludia as variations" on a common harmonic basis, in the manner of Niedt (n.29: Poulin and Taylor 1989) [filled] (p. 151). L.: "an element of validity", but the relation is only general: the bass descends to a dominant pedal with a single station, E♭ at b.14 [filled] (p. 151). The opening formula differs from the C major one (Ex.7.5). It also opens the B flat major two-part Invention (p. 151). Note 27: it began as a finger exercise ("étude", ch. 6 §3). Busoni suggested playing it in double octaves or 6ths (n.27; p. 152).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** Like the C major, it uses scalic descent in the bass to a dominant pedal, but has only one root-position station, on the relative major (E flat, b.14) (p. 151). The dominant pedal arrives at b.21 (p. 152). Change of figuration to cadenza style at b.25. The concert ending runs bb.26–end (p. 152).
- **Harmony/figured-bass abstract:** Concentrates on last-inversion dominant 7ths and diminished 7ths. The alternation of 2 and 6 chords is a standard harmonisation of a descending bass scale. Ex.7.5 is a figured-bass abstract of the earliest version (pp. 151–152).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** b.18: Bach ultimately chose c as the first bass note and B as the ninth (NBA V/5 p.21; V/6.1 p.131, p.245). Copyists repeated c as the ninth note, and Kroll adopted this for 'consistency'. L.: "the worst possible one", because Bach wanted to avoid a root-position tonic arrival that would weaken the dominant pedal at b.21 (pp. 151–152). The first version went from b.25 straight to b.35 and ended with one bar of C major (27 bars). An intermediate stage (NBA V/6.1 pp.244–5) had the Presto/Adagio cadenza but not quite the last three bars (37 bars) (p. 152). In P 415 the RH of the last bar is adjusted to end on the 3rd on the weakest beat. Now the first three notes of the fugue subject sound at the same pitch in the prelude's last bar (p. 152). Hermelink (1976 p.58): the new ending lets passion and rhetoric "break in" on the étude's discipline ('Fleiß') (p. 152).
- **Performance:** b.34, last quaver: Bach's original note values differ from the "more correct" values, which date from no earlier than Schwenke's copy. Adagio implies recitative freedom: do not treat bb.33–34 with "stiff, pedantic exactitude" (p. 152). Notes 28–32 (figurae and performance; Poulin & Taylor; C.F.A. Williams; Houle; Siegele) are placed as follows [filled]: n.28 follows a mention of Walther's figura with a dissonant 3rd note; n.29 goes with the variations reading of the prelude (see Type); nn.30–32 go with the fugue (see Fugue: Subject, Form reading) (p. 152).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Three (cantus, middle, bass; triple counterpoint) (pp. 154–155).
- **Subject:** The figure follows a 17th-century pattern (J. Krieger, Toccata ex D, bb.17–20½, Ex.7.6). It was used in Bach's early toccatas to engineer interlocking imitations, a tradition going back to Frescobaldi (cf. Art of Fugue Contrapunctus I). That is not the case here, but it is one strand feeding in (p. 153). Note 34: the subject is 'gnomic'; Knab's "Elfentanz" (n.34). Note 31: the subject is not in Mattheson's or Printz's rhythmopoeia lists; note 32: Siegele compares it to the Lullian chaconne anapaest (BWV 582) [filled] (p. 152 nn.31–32).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** Triple counterpoint treated in a free, 'modern' way. It is one of the clearest examples of the invention principle, with all material, including the countersubjects, growing from the subject: one of the most densely developmental fugues [filled] (p. 154). The triple-counterpoint block of bb.7–8 (idea 1 in the bass, idea 2 in the cantus, idea 3 as middle filler) acts as a ritornello with 'interludes' between its returns. Idea 3 is reshaped at b.12 and put down an octave in the bass at b.20 [filled] (p. 154). The codetta of bb.5–6 is inverted at the 12th in bb.17–20 (p. 155).
- **Exposition:** Idea 1 enters in middle, cantus and bass, in that order. The modulating version of the block is at bb.3–4. The bass entry is prepared by the codetta at bb.5–6 (p. 155). [Bar of the bass entry not stated]
- **Later entries:** b.15 middle voice, the unstable modulating version (corresponds to bb.3–4). b.20 cantus, tonic. b.26 bass, delayed to the second half of the bar (pp. 155–156).
- **Episodes:** bb.9–10 modulate. The interlude bb.22–5 does not, which gives a feeling of reprise (p. 156). Note 37: Bach's own term 'Zwischenspiele' (n.37).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** Pitch-level articulation: opening in the middle c'–c" octave, top c"' at the halfway point (b.15), bottom C at the end (p. 155). Second section bb.15–31 contains a complete exposition (p. 155). Cross-relations and dissonance rise in bb.17–20. Consecutive major 3rds in b.17, third beat, were 'corrected' in some sources (b auxiliary in bass b.17 beat 3; e' in alto b.18 beat 1) (p. 155). Tension subsides from b.20 with the return of the tonic (p. 155). bb.25–6 are "effectively a dominant pedal" (p. 156). Asymmetric shapes at bb.17–20 and 25–6 break the two-bar periodicity (p. 156). Idea 3 is weak as a cantus; in the final block (bb.27–8) Bach turns this into drama with "a double appoggiatura and a rhetorical pause (b.28½)" [filled] (p. 154).
- **Form reading:** Binary: the ideas run from dominant back to tonic after the midpoint, mirroring the first half (ch. 3 §6). The mirror is more rigorous in the Book I E minor fugue (p. 155). Note 30 attaches to "the old German Barform" among the forms read into this fugue [filled] (p. 152 n.30). Heinrich Schenker (1926, per n.38) is named in the text [filled]: he reads three Urlinie descents, bb.1–9 and 9–20 (a 5th each) and bb.20–29 (an octave), giving a ternary form with a central phase of tension from b.15 and a reprise from b.20 (bb.20–29). L.: this has "an element of validity", but resembles Riemann's ternary structures. Reading later structural principles back into Bach is artificial (p. 155). Note 38: Dreyfus 1996 ch.6 critiques Schenker (n.38).
- **Genre/models:** The direct model is a rhythm used in Venetian concertos: B. Marcello Op.1 No.2 in E minor, 2nd movement (Venice 1708), which Bach arranged as BWV 981 in C minor; and the fugal third section of Vivaldi Op.3 No.11 (Amsterdam 1712), arranged as BWV 596 (p. 153). Note 33: Fischer's *Ariadne Musica* B flat Fuga (Ex.8.26) is an interim phase between J. Krieger's Toccata ex D (Ex.7.6; Riedel ed., *Die Orgel* II/3 p.21) and this fugue [filled] (n.33). Note 35: Vivaldi Op.3 No.11 was also used in BWV 21 (n.35). "Fugue in the 17th century was regarded as a play of wit, not pedantry" (p. 154).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Note 36: P 203 and P 208 add alto quavers f' and e' on the third beat of b.28 (the note is attached at b.28½) [filled] (n.36). Bach himself added parts in the final two bars for sonority (n.36).

## Book I no. 3, C sharp major, BWV 848 (pp. 156–159)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Based on a common elementary exercise of expanding broken intervals (singers, keyboard or string players). It shows Bach the teacher making 'little connected pieces' from such patterns (pp. 156–157). The added section uses a "modern" batterie such as Rameau's (*Pièces de clavessin*, 1724) (p. 157). Isolated copies of the prelude alone (C.P.E. Bach and others) show its success as a "brilliant Solfeggio in an extraordinary key" (p. 156).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** Early version: an opening section of four permutations of the opening 8-bar phrase, up a sequence of rising 5ths "(C G D A)" [check: accidentals probably lost]. Middle section from b.31 on the hemiola figure, "(4 bars)" [check: probably a garbled number]. Reprise at b.47 starting on the subdominant, answered in the tonic. Final six-bar dominant pedal (p. 157). Long version: a single insertion of bb.63–98, essentially a long extension of the final dominant pedal. It is internally ternary: bb.63–72 return an octave lower at bb.87–96, with ten-bar and one- or two-bar units. The final phrase goes from six to eight bars (p. 157). Hemiola rounds off the opening phrase (p. 157).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The early version has 68 bars (NBA V/6.1 pp.134–5) [filled]. Expanded in the lost autograph draft (reflected in the Clavier-Büchlein version) to 104 bars. Entered in P 415 and revised at two later stages (p. 156). Transposition: Keller (1976 p.56) took the C sharp spelling as evidence of a C major original. L. disagrees: "no tell-tale signs" (such as naturals altered from flats), unlike the D sharp minor fugue and G sharp minor prelude (p. 156). Werckmeister (1697 p.36) foresaw players indifferent between 'c. oder cis' (p. 156). Opening figure (Ex.7.7): the original form was probably as at b.9 (a), then as at b.1 (b), to show the C sharp handshape with the thumb on a raised note, and finally as at b.25 (c) (KB V/6.1 p.314) (pp. 156–157). Later revisions conform all openings to Ex.7.7c and decorate the bass hemiola at b.8 with semiquavers (p. 157).
- **Performance:** The lively character "may point to a slightly unequal temperament" (p. 156). The cadential chords in the second-last bar resemble those of BWV 540/1. P. Williams (1984 p.203) notes staccato dashes on them in P 290 (p. 158).
- **Other analysts cited:** Riemann (1890 I p.15) places an accent on g" in b.3. L.: that is one aspect, but the phrase also grows to the top a", the "tensest third (apart from e")" in an unequal tuning (p. 157).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** L. calls it "one of Bach's sunniest fugues", free of the strain the extraordinary key might lead one to expect [filled] (p. 158). Jenne (1974) "convincingly" analyses it as a bourrée, after Kirnberger, so the start on the second half of the bar is remarkable (p. 158). The subject ends firmly on the tonic on a main beat (b.3), a 'full stop' Bach usually avoids in stile moderno and exploits richly here (p. 158). Links with the prelude: a rising 6th, then the 3rd leap e"–c" [check: accidentals lost], and closing 6th/7th leaps paralleling the LH of prelude bb.5–7 (p. 158). The subject ends in an emphatic descending scale outline (notes 5–1) (p. 158). Note 39: the turn plus rising major 6th as an expression of well-being (Wagner) (n.39).
- **Answer:** bb.4–5. It continues the descending scale down to g (p. 158).
- **Countersubject(s):** A block of triple counterpoint makes the ritornello element unusually definite (p. 159).
- **Exposition:** Subject b.1; answer bb.4–5; bass entry bb.6–7, carrying the scale down to c (p. 158). [Voice of first entries not stated]
- **Later entries:** Dominant entry at b.10, bass reaching G (Renwick 1995 p.15). Final entry in the tonic after b.51 (p. 158).
- **Episodes:** bb.7–10, with a root-position chord at each downbeat and scale steps continuing down to G (b.10). In the reprise the episode is adjusted to reach the bottom C (b.51) (p. 158). Episodes form hierarchies of structural independence (p. 159).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** First section bb.1–12. At b.13 the bass lands on the root of a diminished 7th, swinging into the central development. bb.1–12 are repeated da capo from b.42 to the end (p. 158).
- **Form reading:** "Bach's favourite combination of fugue with concerto da capo structure" (p. 158). Debate over separating episodes from ritornellos is "not clear-cut" (p. 159). Note 40: possible influence of Albinoni's later concertos (Butler 1995) [probably attaches here] (n.40).
- **Other:** The fugue probably did not come first, given the prelude's elementary material. It turns technical handshapes into a "musical jeu d'esprit" (p. 158).

## Book I no. 4, C sharp minor, BWV 849 (pp. 159–168)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** A siciliano. Most bars have the siciliano rhythm in the first half and only quavers in the second (p. 159). It is a cantabile study of the siciliano rhythm set against groups of six quavers, building on the E major prelude [filled] (p. 159). Notes 41–42 probably attach here: comparison with BWV 652, BWV 768 var.10, and the sarabande of BWV 1067. Nef (1904) on texture used expressively, e.g. the melodic peak in b.1 enhanced by a chord (Quantz) (nn.41–42).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** A long build-up from b.19 to b.35. Climax: siciliano rhythm end to end in the bass bb.30–1, down to the lowest note of the keyboard, then continuous quavers bb.32–4 falling into the final cadence (p. 159). The additions round out a sonata shape: a first section in three phases ending on the dominant (b.14), then a longer second section, much of it a prolonged dominant, with the subdominant and a Neapolitan at b.24 and the top b" at b.25 [filled] (p. 160). The added bb.14½–16½ bring a moment of relaxed two-bar periodicity (bb.15–18) before the second section's long development [filled] (p. 160).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** bb.35–8 were added in the final version to wind down by returning to the alternation of the two opening figures (p. 159). The additions do not radically change the shape (p. 159). Alterations of detail mainly serve playability (p. 160). The tie a'–a' in the cantus at bb.11–12 is not Bach's: it entered P 415 only in 1861, though it is in many early copies (KB V/6.1 p.217). Equivalent groups are tied (bass b.12, cantus b.13, b.11 of the early version). L. thinks its omission was probably an oversight. Dürr gives it; Kroll and Jones do not (p. 160).
- **Performance:** b.25: the only fingering in autograph P 415, a "5" on the third bass note, apparently for small hands. Probably added at stage A4 (Dürr, KB V/6.1 p.218), and no reason to doubt it is Bach's (p. 160). Arpeggio signs in bb.3, 8 and 34 date from the first copying; the rest from A4. Spread chords rhythmically and expressively (p. 160). Klotz's direction-of-spread theory is discussed under the E flat minor prelude. The only possible downward spread is b.29, and that is doubtful (pp. 160–161). b.16: should the cantus d" have an appoggiatura? It appears in only two sources (closest: Altnickol 1755, P 402). Against it: the figure is thematic and recurs in sequence at b.18. Some sources add an e" passing quaver to the last crotchet of b.15. L. concludes: play as notated in P 415 (p. 161).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Five by implication. Parts named: cantus, alto, second alto, tenor, bass. A "theoretical six parts" in the second half of b.96 (pp. 161–162).
- **Subject:** Subject 1 is in stile antico white notes and contains a diminished 4th: "mannered chromaticism", passus/saltus duriusculus (p. 162). The diminished 4th was probably the commonest dissonant leap in the 17th century (Constantini 1967 p.179) and "the most starkly expressive interval" of the seconda pratica [displaced, restored] (p. 163). Its chiastic shape is the cross figure: 'kreuzigen' in the St Matthew Passion, as in Schütz and Kerll; compare the diminished 3rd in the B minor Mass second Kyrie, and B-A-C-H (p. 163). It links to the chorale 'Nun komm der Heiden Heiland' (Mode II, Hypodorian on G; 7th sometimes sharpened, as in BWV 61 and BWV 660): the diminished 4th combines the cross motif with the notion of salvation ('Heil'), a conjunction the chorale emphasises [displaced, restored] (p. 163). Subject 2: running quavers. Subject 3: emphatic repeated crotchets. They contrast in note value: the fuga major (white notes, open score) is combined with the fuga minor (canzona/verset, black notes, keyboard score) (Riedel 1980) (p. 161).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** Triple counterpoint. The countersubject in the bass at bb.4–7 fills in the subject's 4th. Its crotchet and quaver shapes supply the rest of section 1 and the essentials of Subjects 2 and 3 (p. 162). Subjects flow into one another: S2 quavers into S1 minims at bb.48–9, and S3's head leads off S2's quavers at bb.65–6 (p. 162). P. Williams: the organ fughetta BWV 699 combines the chorale's first phrase with Subject 3 (Ex.7.8b). Note 46: BWV 699 is from Kirnberger's collection and is "earlyish" (p. 163; n.46).
- **Exposition:** Subject 1 has its own exposition (p. 161). Countersubject in the bass at bb.4–7 (p. 162).
- **Later entries:** Subject 2 is introduced at b.35 and combined at once with Subject 1. Subject 3 enters at b.49, starting the long section of triple-counterpoint permutations (p. 161). S3 entries at bb.67–8 and bb.71–2 (the autograph keeps the S3 tie only at bb.71–2, probably an oversight) (p. 163). Final section: S1 stretti among the top three parts: cantus on e" at b.94; alto on b' in the second half of b.95; f' in the second half of b.96 (incomplete, going straight into S3). The first two lack the subject's last note (p. 162). bb.100–7: S1 "migrating between parts" (tenor bb.100–1 has the first three notes; c'|b e'|d'|c' moves between the two altos at bb.104–7), leading to the full, prominent cantus entry at b.107 (p. 162).
- **Stretto:** From b.94: simultaneous stretti of Subjects 1 and 3. Subject 2 is not used (p. 161). See also bb.94–97 above (p. 162).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** Phases are marked off by perfect cadences (p. 161). bb.70–1 are "the golden section point of the fugue", where the notes BACH (Mi Fa Mi Fa) sound [filled] (p. 164; cf. n.49). The untied, emphatic form of Subject 3 is the intended one, since the final stretti (bb.94ff) depend on its repeated crotchets driving into dissonance [filled] (p. 164). Because Subject 1's diminished 4th falls off the beat, Subject 3 carries the suspension climax; the inganno is palindromic [filled] (p. 164). Final cadence: a summary of stile antico effects: major/minor 7th cross-relation at bb.108–10, a 'modern' diminished 7th at b.109, and the perfect cadence delayed by a 6th in the tonic chord at b.112 (pp. 164–165). The final pedal pattern from b.105 is identical to the D major prelude's from b.27 (p. 168).
- **Form reading:** "extraordinarily organic and continuous", in contrast to Strungk's Ricercar 'a 3 Suietti', which has the same first subject in the major and gives Subjects 2 and 3 a separate 'Stanza 2da' [filled] (p. 161; n.43 cites a related fugue in DTÖ 27 attributed to Georg Reutter the elder). Toch (1940–48) and others "torture the parts" in bb.100–7 to find S1 entries. L. reads migrating subjects instead, as common in French organ fugues (p. 162).
- **Genre/models:** Frescobaldi's *Fiori musicali* (Bach owned a MS copy) as the model of living, freely moulded parts (p. 162). Ex.7.9: Corelli Op.6 No.3 Grave and Georg Muffat, Sonata II Grave (*Armonico tributo*, 1682): in these Graves the texture is built up from the bass, as perhaps in Bach's exposition [filled] (p. 164). Pages 165–167 are mostly the example itself, and the closing text is the final-cadence summary above [filled] (pp. 165–167). Notes 44–49: Seiffert 1899; Theill 1985; Torelli Op.5 No.1 (1692); inganni (Harper, Macey, Chafe); comparison with Frescobaldi (Koptchewski 1988); the notes BACH appear in the fugue (Zacher 1988 p.246) (nn.44–49).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** b.96: the autograph's first cantus note is a semibreve f". Kroll and Dürr 'correct' it to a minim; Jones keeps Bach's semibreve. L. backs the semibreve: it answers the semibreve c" at b.97, and Kroll's part-crossing would obscure the alto entry at bb.95–6 (pp. 161–162). One early-to-mid-18th-century source, possibly from Bach's circle, marks 'Ped.' at the opening. Dürr thinks it inauthentic, and there is no other evidence of pedaliter conception (p. 168). Note 50: KB V/6.1 p.219; the low C was missing on many pedalboards (n.50). The Breitkopf source has a decorated version of bb.42–3 (p. 169).

## Book I no. 5, D major, BWV 850 (pp. 168–170)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Praeludium 4 of the Clavier-Büchlein [filled] (p. 168). In the Clavier-Büchlein it follows the D minor prelude (ch. 6 §3). It is a right-hand exercise with much hand displacement pivoted on the thumb, the counterpart of the C minor prelude (p. 168). Notes 51–52 (Wegweiser; Mattheson 1713 on the key: "frisch/ ermuntrend ... elevirt") probably attach here (nn.51–52).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** Additions for WTC: a subdominant reprise (bb.19–26) and a cadenza-like concert ending (bb.30–4) that prepares the fugue's manner (p. 168). Final pedal pattern from b.27 (p. 168).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Apart from the second half of b.29 (b.21 of the early version), Bach changed no notes substantially (p. 168). The crux is the bass of the first chord of b.33. It is "quite unambiguously A" in the autograph and B in some distant sources. Dürr: probably independent copyists' conjecture. Kreutz and Dehnhard: an alteration for small hands (AB I p.142). Tovey (1924) and Keller (1976 p.65) prefer B, for a line A|B|c' d'. L.: Bach meant A, a dissonant extension of the 6/4 chord of b.32 (p. 168). With A, the pedal pattern from b.27 is identical to that from b.105 of the C sharp minor fugue (p. 168).
- **Performance:** The second chord of b.34 lacks an arpeggio sign in the autograph (one is present in P 202 and P 402). The sign at the start of b.34 may cover all the chords in the last two bars. The signs at bb.33–4 undermine Klotz's direction theory (p. 168).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** Not literally a French overture: the fugue takes features of the overture's first section (dotted rhythms, tiratas, even semiquavers) into a fugue, perhaps updating the dotted verset fughetta [filled] (p. 169). Note 53 (BG XIV; Dolmetsch, Donington, Badura-Skoda) on overdotting (n.53).
- **Form reading:** Dreyfus stresses its underlying simplicity, with the working of its stylistic surface as its main aim (n.54: Dreyfus 1996 pp.150, 163; J.N. David 1962). L.: seen from the verset fughetta, Bach has done "an extraordinary amount" with a simple idea, building up "a large-seeming, grand and dramatic piece" [filled] (p. 169). Czaczkes' reading of four expositions in two pairs is too rigid. Four entries end on the dominant at b.6; a link leads to a tonic bass entry, then an entry moving to B minor (b.9). Perfect cadences fall in 3rds: B minor (b.11), G (b.15), E minor (b.17) [filled] (p. 169). The discussion ends by citing Werckmeister's continuo tutor (1698b p.21) for circling through keys by falling 3rds [filled; moved from the D minor prelude, where the digest had placed it] (p. 170).
- **Performance:** Whatever approach is taken to Kroll's "somewhat brutal rule" of overdotting should follow the general character [filled] (p. 169). Grammar is little help: overdotting gives parallel octaves between cantus and bass in b.4, while not overdotting gives parallels at b.22, beat 3. Objections to parallels between ornamental notes are often pedantic (p. 169). A Breitkopf source, possibly from Bach's circle, has ornaments in the subject (AB I p.143) (p. 169).

## Book I no. 6, D minor, BWV 851 (pp. 170–174)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** A moto perpetuo from the Corellian violin sonata type (p. 170). The Clavier-Büchlein prelude in D minor BWV 926 (No.4) looks like a precursor (p. 170). Notes 56–58: as the C major prelude outlines Ut Mi Sol, the D minor outlines Re Fa La. Niedt's *Musical Guide* II ch. 6. Mattheson's *Exemplarische Organisten-Probe*, whose first D minor piece resembles this prelude (Schenkman 1981) (nn.56–58).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The early version has "a masterly balance of even and uneven periods". The final version adds a single 12-bar addition: a tonic pedal, then a dominant pedal, beginning at b.15. b.23 takes up the pattern of b.15 on the dominant and continues into the cadenza-style final bars (p. 171).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Parallel octaves (b' a' / B A) in the chords of bb.25–6. Some sources omit the tie between the two d"s to soften them. The autograph tie is certain. If the chords are arpeggiated, the question hardly arises (p. 171).
- **Performance:** [filled; corrects: the Werckmeister 1698b citation at the top of p. 170 closes the D major fugue, not this prelude; see no. 5] On sustained chords: legatissimo is inappropriate for BWV 926, but for this prelude it is "very idiomatic and effective", letting the inner parts tell (pp. 170–171).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** A companion to the C major fugue: the same length, with complementary time signatures (C and 3/4). Both subjects step out the lower tetrachord and then the 6th; here it is the minor 6th, which Brossard (1705, n.60) characterises as expressing sadness, pain or exclamation [filled; corrects the digest, which assigned this character to the diminished 7th] (p. 171). Couperin's 'aspiration' sits on the b' of the subject: Bach writes a dot (Dürr, Jones), Kroll a wedge. Bach's dot signals emphasis as well as separation (Klotz 1984 pp.175–80) (p. 171). The subject ends unusually on the 5th degree, a "curiously questioning effect" implying the Phrygian cadence (p. 172). It belongs to a family of D minor subjects (D minor Invention, this prelude) that alternate the tonic with a diminished 7th. The diminished 7th suits inversion (cf. Art of Fugue). Closest comparison: the Allemanda of BWV 1004 (Ex.7.10) (p. 172). The trill sign is not in the Clavier-Büchlein Explication. At b.29 it is written with a turn, which ties it into the motivic network (p. 174).
- **Countersubject(s):** Built entirely from subject motifs (the tetrachord inverted in semiquavers, the 6th leap, the auxiliary figure) (Spitta onwards) (p. 174). The slur in the countersubject at b.3 covers either all four semiquavers (Dehnhard) or the last three (Dürr, Jones). Kroll omits it. L. favours all four (pp. 173–174). The lower auxiliary at the end of the countersubject is a turn figure, made explicit by the turn signs at bb.9–11 (p. 174).
- **Later entries:** After b.21, an exposition of the inverted subject (p. 174).
- **Episodes:** As in the C minor fugue, the episodic material builds up to the final tonic entries (p. 174). bb.9–11 (turns) correspond to bb.30–2 in the second half (p. 172).
- **Form reading:** Binary-sonata symmetry: the second half (from b.21) runs the first half's events from dominant back to tonic, with the subject inverted, as in allemandes and gigues. With nos. 1 and 2 it shows Bach "reconciling the fugue with the binary dance/ character piece/sonata" (p. 174).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** An ornamented version, the anonymous Breitkopf copy with its "fussy" articulation (NBA V/6.1 Anhang 3; BA I Anhang) [filled], shows that affective performance was expected (p. 172). Note 61: Dürr argues for the authority of the anonymous part of the Breitkopf MS (paper, Meißner composite copy, better unique readings, e.g. b.43) (n.61). Tovey (1924 p.57) calls the turns at bb.9–11 an anachronism from C.P.E. Bach, preferring the dots at bb.30–2. L.: a hint of empfindsam style is not out of place. Turns would be awkward at bb.30–2, and turns and dots were both added in A4, probably c.1740 (KB V/6.1 p.227) (p. 172). Ex.7.11 reproduces bb.1–22 from P 415 (p. 173).

## Book I no. 7, E flat major, BWV 852 (pp. 174–177)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** L. calls the prelude "something of an enigma" [filled] (p. 174). Its sectional form reverts to the organ-prelude type of Bach's youth, abandoned in later Weimar days for the single-span Venetian concerto type (p. 174). It is missing from the Clavier-Büchlein, perhaps an older piece held in reserve as too long and hard for Wilhelm Friedemann. It is "unique among preludes in the 48 in including a fully developed fugue" (p. 174). It may first have been for organ (the early version has fewer bass semiquavers in section 2, bb.10–24). Mattheson (1719) knew no toccatas or preludes in E flat because of organ tuning (n.62). Organ pieces in E flat do exist in Bach's area, dating from his youth: by Johann Pachelbel, in the Mylau tablature (1689–1700), and Bach's own 'O Mensch bewein'; the passage gives a probable dating of Weimar after c.1710 [filled] (p. 174). Note 63: dating discussed by Hermelink 1976 p.67 and Dürr 1988 pp.100–1 (n.63).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** Three sections. Section 2 (from b.10) is stile antico. Section 3 is a double fugue (pp. 174–175).
- **Sections in detail:** Section 2 has 'gebunden' dissonance treatment and fluid metre but, untypically, no rests once the voices have entered (p. 175). Section 3 is "a masterly double fugue" in counterpoint at the octave, 10th and 12th, based on the four-note handshape of section 1. Inversions at the 12th at b.47f (compare b.25) and b.49f; at the 10th at b.53f (pp. 175–176). Its semiquaver subject has repeated notes (as in Subject 3 of the C sharp minor fugue) that prepare the "cathartic bass entry" at b.61 (p. 176). The 9/7 chord at b.23 (p. 175).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Few changes: two bars added in section 1 (bb.3–4) and decorative semiquavers at bb.20–1. The bass decoration at b.22, originally demisemiquavers, became semiquavers moving down through the parts. This makes it quasi-thematic and links section 2's 4th/5th leaps to section 3's semiquaver subject (p. 175).
- **Performance:** A source annotation at b.10, in pencil and not in Bach's hand, says "not like an Alla breve, but continued as in the first bar" [filled]: no change of time signature or doubling of tempo at section 2 (p. 175). Note 64: the annotation predates 1783, may derive from Bach (Dürr), and is not legible in the facsimile (n.64).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Three (cantus, alto, bass named) (p. 177).
- **Subject:** It unites the E flat handshape of the prelude's opening with the outline of the stile antico subject of the prelude's double fugue. It expands 5th → 6th → 7th. A 'modern' tonal subject that modulates to the dominant (p. 176). Chromatic step between subject and continuation ("a' in b.1, a' in b.2") [check: accidentals lost] (p. 176).
- **Answer:** b.3, alto. Outlines "b e' d' g' f'" [check: flats lost], which matches the stile antico subject at prelude b.25 (p. 176). It does not modulate, a "pale imitation" of the subject. It "is more in the tonic than the subject itself" (pp. 176–177).
- **Countersubject(s):** Double, not triple, counterpoint (p. 176). The countersubject has a chromatic step at the start of b.6, replacing an octave leap (p. 176).
- **Exposition:** Subject b.1 [voice not stated]; alto answer b.3; bass entry b.6 (pp. 176–177).
- **Later entries:** b.11 cantus (answer). b.17 a "jokey, chromatically warped" version in C minor. b.26 bass (answer). b.29 cantus (subject). b.34 alto (corresponds to b.3), exploiting a rest and chromatic step (p. 177).
- **Episodes:** Linking passage bb.4–5 extends the chromatic step (p. 176). Solo-break equivalents: b.8, then again from the second half of b.12, extended into a modulation to C minor (p. 177). Rich chromatic sequences bb.14–16 (p. 176).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** First half (to b.17) almost entirely in the tonic. The second half visits the relative minor. Long tonic reprise from b.26 (pp. 176–177). Top keyboard octave at bb.4–5 is balanced by the bottom octave at bb.32–3 (p. 177). Chromatic falling line in the "Schumannesque" final bar recalls the prelude's last three bars (p. 176).
- **Form reading:** Fugue plus binary sonata, crossed with a Vivaldi concerto (Op.3) ritornello plan (ch. 4 §11). The cantus entry at b.11 is a ritornello repeat in the tonic, not a 'redundant entry'. The reprise reruns the opening in reverse (b.26 ↔ b.11, b.29 ↔ b.6, b.34 ↔ b.3) (p. 177).
- **Other:** Busoni's "dreadful idea" of following the prelude with the Book II E flat fugue (p. 176).

## Book I no. 8, E flat minor / D sharp minor, BWV 853 (pp. 177–183)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** The key is original (the g♯' of b.29 sits in a late addition) [filled] (p. 178). It has "something of the character of a tombeau, whose features are" very flat keys, dotted rhythms and tiratas [filled], but the reference is oblique. Sarabandes and French overtures "hardly come into it" (p. 178). Badura-Skoda (1993 p.215) suggests a lament for Maria Barbara (d. July 1720), "rather romantic" (p. 178). It continues the sequence of preludes in advanced keys after C sharp minor. Its aim is the technique of expressive projection: florid lines over steady repeated chords (Landowska 1965 p.186) (p. 178). It opens with another version of the harmonic formula of the C major, C minor and D minor preludes, and resembles the B flat minor prelude (p. 178). L. calls it a "Vivaldian slow movement" (p. 181). Note 65: BWV 819 Minuet II, Bach's only other keyboard piece in E flat minor (n.65).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The bleakness of the wide 3rd on g♭' at the opening is "resolved onto warm E flat major chords" in a rich low register from b.37 [filled] (p. 178). Final-version additions bring the top c"' (b.28) and the descent to the bottom D♯ (bb.32–5) [filled] (p. 179).
- **Versions, sources, revisions, transposition:** There is no real evidence of another key. b.29 has a sharp sign for the first g' in the autograph, which might hint at an E minor original. But Bach reverted to a natural at the end of the bar, and b.29 belongs to the final-version additions (KB V/6.1 p.188) (p. 178). The autograph beams long semiquaver groups together (Dürr, Jones). Older editions (Kroll, Dehnhard) group them in fours (p. 178). Rameau's 1722 key listing: minor keys run to D sharp at the sharp end and E flat at the flat end, the "interchange point". Transposing such pieces into flat keys is "a historical falsification" (p. 177).
- **Performance:** A slightly unequal temperament matters: a wide major 3rd on the first chord gives "chilling bleakness" (p. 178). Dolmetsch's overdotting "would be ruinous". Badura-Skoda's slight swing of the quavers, "in the manner of notes inégales", is "obviously what is required" (p. 178; n.66). b.13: the turn notation in the final autograph revision is clear (a delay, then a slight pause on the last note); no edition reflects it (p. 179). b.29: the semiquavers after a dot are a written-out ornament, so play them as an ornament. The last two semiquavers probably terminate an interrupted trill (as in the E minor prelude). The semiquavers of the next bar are thematic (Shedlock 1883 p.595) (p. 179). Extra ornaments in P 202/P 203 (Ex.7.12, bb.1–4). The mordent on the first cantus note shows it is the start of the melody (p. 179). The g" on beat 2 of b.4 was a chord note following a conventional tierce coulée phrase ending (compare b.8), so "The placing of this note is therefore important" [filled] (p. 179). Klotz's direction of arpeggiation (Andreas Bach Book, Ex.7.13): "impressive" evidence. On this theory: b.28 second-beat chord spread down, third-beat chord up. The chord at the start of b.35 would also go down, which weakens the low D and the c" lead into the RH cadenza. L.: applied rigorously it brings "an illogical fussiness"; players must decide (pp. 180–181). Note 67: "The division is later joined, at bb.17–18" [check: attachment uncertain] (n.67).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** Typical Mode I (Dorian) outline with the characteristic M shape. Comparison with 'Aus tiefer Not' is erroneous, since that is Mode III (Walther 1732 p.410) (p. 181). Its inversion is the subject of Froberger's Ricercar II (1656). Bach uses the M shape as the first subject of the Art of Fugue's incomplete fugue. The return to the 4th degree is typical of Mode I melodies such as *Ave maris stella* (p. 181; n.69). Trill on the penultimate note (e') in P 202 and P 1074 at b.3 and equivalents: "should surely be observed". The figure on the last beat of b.11 may imply a trill (p. 183).
- **Stretto/inversion/augmentation:** "the only fugue in Book I to use augmentation" (p. 181). Stretto recto at b.19½, inverted at the 12th at b.27 (p. 182). Augmentation entries are placed high and surrounded by increasingly complex stretti, culminating in a stretto of all three versions (normal, semi-augmented, augmented) at b.77 (p. 182).
- **Sections:** An exposition fugue "with brief links rather than episodes", each exposition bringing a new contrapuntal treatment (p. 181).
- **Form reading:** Belongs to the 17th-century fantasia/ricercar tradition of learned counterpoint. It resembles the A minor fugue and is probably roughly contemporary with it. Both contrast with the c.1720 fugues and with Book II's D sharp minor fugue (early vs late style) (p. 181). It is "the only piece in Book I" with a substantial modal element (p. 181). Note 70: survey of views in Ellis 1980 (n.70).
- **Versions, sources, transposition:** "virtual certainty" that it was originally in D minor, transposed by changing the key signature (pp. 177, 182). The b.15 scale went up to a note now beyond the Book I keyboard [check: pitch names garbled, "c"' ... c"'"]. Dehnhard alone gives the higher note, following J.G. Walther (P 1074). D minor accidentals survive (B natural for B sharp, E flat for E natural; KB V/6.1 p.188) (p. 182).
- **Performance:** How much ornament to add is a problem. Cf. the ornamented Canzona BWV 588 in the Mempell-Preller collection (Faulkner 1984) (pp. 182–183).

## Book I no. 9, E major, BWV 854 (pp. 183–184)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** In the Clavier-Büchlein it comes between the E minor and F major preludes [filled] (p. 183). It is a pastorale [filled] (p. 183). It introduces the arpeggio as a cantabile element (cf. p. 189).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** An aria-ritornello shape heard three times, with a pedal in place of a sequence, each time modulating: to the dominant at b.8½ and to the subdominant at b.15 [filled] (p. 183). Since the first section went from tonic to dominant, the last can be a literal reprise from subdominant to tonic [filled] (p. 183). The closing idea at bb.7–8 has a chromatic twist. A jeu d'esprit modulates to the dominant minor and ends the section in the major via a tierce de Picardie (p. 183).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Earlier versions (including Clavier-Büchlein) have d' in the tenor on the last beat of b.7 and d" in the cantus on beat 2 of b.8, with corresponding naturals in bb.21–2 [check: natural signs probably lost from the pitch names] (p. 183).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** An iambic-step subject is a commonplace of 17th-century versets. Frescobaldi built "Chinese-puzzle-type" interlocking textures from it; the example Bach knew is the 'Alegro' of the Epistle Canzona in the Messa della Domenica of *Fiori musicali* [filled] (p. 183). The subject outlines I–V–I, with four semiquavers as division-variation extension of the iambic step. A miniature head-and-tail (Vordersatz/Nachsatz) (p. 184). It is brought in over an important punctuating cadence at b.16 (p. 184).
- **Answer:** b.2. It "steps up to c"" [check: c#"], which supplies the one alternative tonal area (from b.16) (p. 184).
- **Countersubject(s):** The subject has no defined ending, and "pedantic efforts to define the subject exactly really miss the point", as does the debate about a 'proper' countersubject [filled; corrects the digest, which attached "miss the point" to the countersubject debate alone] (p. 184).
- **Exposition:** Exposition 1: tonic–dominant–tonic. Answer at b.2 (p. 184).
- **Later entries:** A second exposition, a "fugal inversion" from the end of b.6: tonic–dominant–dominant. Entry at b.16 over a cadence. A brief excursion to C sharp minor. Reprise in closer stretto from b.19: tonic, dominant (cantus, beat 2 of b.20), tonic. Last entry b.28, "pedantically" on the dominant: the quaver step gives an interrupted cadence, answered by the semiquavers' perfect cadence (p. 184).
- **Stretto:** Closer stretto from b.19 (p. 184).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Bach first ended with a large descending bass scale (stages in NBA V/6.1). This gave parallel 5ths into the last beats of bb.24 and 27 between the bass and the turn figure. Later revisions removed them, probably for educational reasons (p. 184).
- **Other:** "one of Bach's lightest and wittiest fugues" (p. 183).

## Book I no. 10, E minor, BWV 855 (pp. 184–188)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** This is the most altered of the Clavier-Büchlein preludes [filled] (p. 184). Bach took one of the Clavier-Büchlein Praeludia and added a florid melody and a Presto second half, which nearly doubles its length and makes it a concert item. The Presto bridges to the fugue's style, and, as Busoni points out, prelude and fugue now share one plan: the Presto starts in A minor and works back to the tonic, as the fugue's second half does [filled] (p. 184). The ornamentation resembles the Corelli Op.5 ornaments of the c.1710 Amsterdam print. Daube (NBR p.362) said that anyone who had not heard Bach improvise a concertante continuo "missed a great deal"; in this prelude "his playing lives on" [filled] (p. 185). Notes 71–72 (obbligato continuo style; Raison as source of the BWV 582 bass) probably attach here (nn.71–72).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Most demisemiquavers date from A4 (plainer readings in NBA V/6.1 p.49) (p. 185). The early version's chords have varying numbers of parts. In the final version they are strictly in two parts, with part-crossings at b.9 and b.15 to avoid parallels. Only Dürr and Jones observe them (p. 185).
- **Performance:** A trill type where the finger lifts before the termination (Raison; Agricola ed. Baird 1995 p.20, against the Berlin orthodoxy): the trills at b.10 and b.12 fade into rests [filled] (p. 186). A possible clavichord effect at b.12 on double-fretted clavichords: d" held while e" is restruck, a lute-style trill. This is "a unique situation for a specifically clavichord effect" in WTC (p. 186).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Two: "the only fugue in the 48 in two parts" [filled] (p. 186).
- **Subject:** A two-bar subject with two elements, spanning two keys. It modulates to the dominant, as does the answer, so each quarter of the piece covers three keys (pp. 186–187). The chromatic descending tetrachord is associated with E minor ('Wedge' fugue BWV 548/2, Crucifixus of the B minor Mass; P. Williams 1997 p.95) (p. 187).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** In double counterpoint throughout (p. 186).
- **Episodes:** Semiquaver figuration from b.15 (inverted from b.34) divides the triple-time bar in two. Duple division takes over completely in the octave passages (p. 187).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** The second half begins at b.20 and is the "mirror contrapuntal inversion" of the first. Both parts go into octaves at the end of each section (b.19, b.38), in the Vivaldi concerto manner (p. 186). Parallel 10ths at b.10 and b.29 [filled] (p. 186).
- **Form reading:** "a thoroughgoing exploration of duality". Divided "in the 1:2 ratio" [check: the halves bb.1–19/20–38 are equal, so probably a garbled "1:1" or "2 halves"] (p. 186). Note 74: Schulenberg: the octaves come from the 'rage aria'. Others call it contemplative (Riemann), decided (Busoni), fiery (Tausig), capricious (Bischoff) (n.74).
- **Genre/models:** C.P.E. Bach reports that his father's teaching of fugue began with two-part fugues, of which "there was once apparently a substantial collection" [filled] (p. 186). Note 73: Dehn reports a lost MS of 72 two-part fugues by Bach in Princess Amalia's library (p. 186; n.73). The Inventions seem more 'mature' than this rigid scheme (p. 186). Cantata 50 uses subject entries rising through 5ths (p. 187). Background models: Buxtehude BuxWV 142 (a dominant-modulating subject plus a falling chromatic 4th, not integrated; Stauffer 1980, Snyder 1987; Ex.7.14). Bruhns, E minor Praeludium (Möller MS), whose fugal subject outlines the falling chromatic 4th and cadences on the dominant (Ex.7.15) (pp. 187–188).

## Book I no. 11, F major, BWV 856 (pp. 188–191)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Praeludium 7 in the Clavier-Büchlein, after the E major, which had already left the C-family time signatures; this one uses 12/8 [filled] (p. 188). It treats the arpeggio in an Allegro, plus a study in the 'Doppelt-cadence' trill of the Clavier-Büchlein Explication. Both ornaments derive from D'Anglebert's 1689 table. An ornament is made a thematic element, as composers back to Monteverdi did (p. 189).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Time signature: originally 24/16, then 24/16 in the RH stave and 12/8 in the left (Clavier-Büchlein; cf. the G major prelude), finally just 12/8 [filled; corrects the digest's reconstructed 12/16 to 24/16] (pp. 188–189). The Clavier-Büchlein copy, "hastily and untidily" made by W.F. Bach (KB V/5 p.89), does not notate the complex signs, only two plain trill signs [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph; the two signs are lost in the PDF text too]. The second sign implies a long trill (p. 189).
- **Performance:** Long trills are written in early-version sources for bb.12–15. As a study in trills, they should be sustained through activity in the other hand. They culminate in the long LH trill at bb.14–15 (p. 189).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Three (tenor, cantus, bass named) (p. 190).
- **Subject:** Bach converts Fischer's gigue-type F major Fuga into a passepied [filled] (p. 190): "in 3/8-time, with a quaver upbeat", with decorative groups of four semiquavers. Four-bar phrases balancing offbeat and onbeat endings (Little and Jenne 1991) (p. 190). The subject ends with an offbeat tierce coulée at the beginning of b.4, replacing the firm perfect cadence of Fischer's subject and blending into the continuation (as in the C major and E major fugues). It flows into a second four-bar phrase with an onbeat ending into b.8. The tenor line b.1–8 forms the first section of a passepied (p. 190).
- **Exposition / later entries:** Tenor b.1. The 8-bar passepied section runs in the cantus from b.4, the bass from b.9, the cantus from b.17, and the bass from b.25 (p. 190).
- **Stretto:** From b.46, "the densest counterpoint (stretto)" and the tensest harmony (many diminished 7ths in G minor) (p. 191).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** First section to b.46. A move to D minor with a dominant pedal in that key, then "the first strong perfect cadence" into b.46 (p. 190). b.46 is the golden-section point (p. 191). b.49: the échappée B [check: probably B♭] as the second semiquaver in the bass makes the semiquaver motion continuous (p. 191). Final section from b.56: the bass climbs from G minor to B flat, the bass of a 6/4/2 chord at b.59. The cantus answers with a scale to g" while the bass falls to E under a 6/5 chord at b.63. From b.68 the climbing scale is in both outer parts, the cantus made from the échappée in sequence (p. 191).
- **Form reading:** It combines fugue, dance and the dance-based sonata. "No point" in seeking a binary division at the halfway point (b.36), "as most analyses have done", since the opening 8 bars are already a passepied first section. Sections are marked by perfect cadences. The sonata element is the moto perpetuo, which eventually supplants the dance metre (pp. 190–191).
- **Genre/models:** Kirnberger on Bach combining fugue with dance (p. 189). Fischer, *Ariadne Musica* F major Fuga, a French gigue (Ex.7.16, bb.1–9) (p. 189). Note 77 (Mather and Karns 1987, dance) probably attaches here [check] (n.77).

## Book I no. 12, F minor, BWV 857 (pp. 191–194)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** The last of the Clavier-Büchlein praeludia; it returns to C time, but in a cantabile manner [filled] (p. 191). Landowska (1965 p.188) sees a similarity to the end of b.2 of the G major French Suite Allemande. L. doubts it. More relevant are the Sarabande of Partita BWV 826 (crotchet stems for tenuti) and the Allemande of the E flat French Suite. It is "an exercise in harpsichord sonority through overholding" (p. 191). A sonata moto perpetuo, cantabile type (p. 191).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** First period to b.9, in crotchet groups of 6+4+4+8+11 (p. 191). LH false starts, then a "glorious" scale rising "from A[♭] to a[♭]", decorated with 9–7 suspensions (bb.6–8) [filled]. Its top note is on beat 2 of b.8; "all has come right" by b.9 (p. 192). Top c"' at b.11 is the climax of the first half. The dominant cadence at b.12 is the halfway point (p. 192). The reprise is built over "the highly expressive d[♭]" of the interrupted cadence at b.16 [filled] (p. 192). Dominant pedal with rising dissonant semiquavers to b" [check: likely b♭"] on beat 2 of b.20. Release on the bottom C at b.21 (not in the early version), answering the c"' of b.11 (p. 192).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The very earliest version lacked bb.16½–21½ [filled: fractions confirmed] (p. 192). The addition corrects an imbalance (only two four-bar phrases after the long opening period), adding a reprise and a better binary structure, with the climax c"' at b.11 and the dominant cadence at b.12 as the halfway point [filled] (p. 192).
- **Performance:** "Nobody with any feeling for the harpsichord could bear not to sustain these chords" (bb.6–8) (p. 192). Unequal temperament gives iridescence, especially since the note d[♭] "makes an enhanced contribution" when it supports a slightly wide major 7th at b.7 and b.16 [filled] (p. 192).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Four (quadruple counterpoint) (pp. 192–193).
- **Subject:** Chromatic. It completes the lower half of the chromatic scale (the B minor fugue completes it). Substantial fugues in nos. 1–12 fall at nos. 4, 8, 12, giving harmonic proportions 2:1 (24:12 or 8:4), the octave, and 3:2 (12:8) or 3:1 (24:8 or 12:4), the 5th [filled] (p. 192). It follows the chromatic ricercars of Frescobaldi's *Fiori musicali* (and Froberger, Pachelbel), but in black notes, as in the Langloz partimenti, which places it in the fuga minor/verset tradition with an unusually long subject (p. 193). Treated as a cantus firmus surrounded by figurae, the anapaest and the 'suspirans', "as the pleurants accompany a bier" (p. 193). Each pair of crotchets is a semitone step. A hint of saltus duriusculus "in the proximity of d' and e'" [check: accidentals lost] (p. 193).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** Quadruple counterpoint "presented in full five times" (p. 192). Compare lively choruses BWV 50 and BWV 182 (p. 192). Counterpoints have high incidental dissonance and cross-relations (p. 193).
- **Exposition:** dux–comes–dux–dux. Normal exposition up to three voices with tonic–dominant–tonic entries in bb.1–12. Tonic bass entry at b.7, three parts, close and low (p. 193).
- **Later entries:** A second triplet of entries, tonic–dominant–tonic, in bb.13–30: dominant entry b.19, tonic bass entry b.27 (the section's climax, full four-part texture). Second half: A flat (b.34) and E flat (b.40), then a reprise of dominant and tonic entries from b.47. The b.47 entry is three-part and as high as possible. Tonic bass at b.53. bb.47 and 53 correspond to bb.19 and 27 (p. 193). Three tonic bass entries: b.7, b.27, b.53 (p. 193).
- **Episodes:** "Interludes" in developmental progression: bb.10–13 with the anapaest rising; bb.16–18 falling; bb.22–26 both. bb.50–52 transpose bb.16–18 (p. 193).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** First half to b.30. The second half develops longer semiquaver lines (p. 193).
- **Form reading:** It looks odd (four entries, then two more dux entries) until a triple scheme is crossed with the quadruple squareness. Not monotonous but "a further concentration of mood" (p. 193).
- **Versions, sources, revisions / Performance:** Earlier sources, and Anna Magdalena's P 202 wherever playable, have a trill on the subject's minim (places listed in NBA V/6.1). Dürr: possibly an unauthorised addition by W.F. Bach (KB V/6.1 p.254), but Bach probably took it for granted (NBA V/6.1 p.VI). The trill Bach did write (b.6) is less obvious, especially if the minim is already trilled (p. 194). Note 78 (Busoni on repeated-note studies including fugues in F sharp major and minor) probably belongs to a later piece [check] (n.78).
