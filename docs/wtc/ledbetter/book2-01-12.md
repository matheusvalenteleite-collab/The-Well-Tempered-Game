## Chapter Eight: general

- In the converted text the range opens directly with the C major pair, so no chapter introduction survives. Cross-references point to Chapter Three section 6 (aria-ritornello / galant binary sonata form), Chapter Three section 9 (Book II evoking one medium on another), and Chapter Four sections 4–6, 8–9. (p. 235ff)
- Ideas that recur across these pieces:
  - Book II often evokes one instrument on another: the organ in the C major and F major preludes, the orchestra in the D major prelude, and the lute-harpsichord in the E flat prelude. None of these preludes suits the medium it evokes; "that is their nature and their charm". (pp. 236, 269, 285, 287)
  - Many Book II fugues come from the canzona/verset tradition: C major, C minor, C sharp major, D major, E flat major and E major. (pp. 249, 253, 259, 266, 280)
  - Many preludes use an expanded galant binary sonata form built on the "aria ritornello shape". (pp. 243, 257, 270, 273, 277, 287)
  - Agricola's copy P 595 holds early versions of the fugues in C minor, D minor, E flat major (in D major) and C sharp major (in C major). Ledbetter thinks Bach kept a stock of these for teaching and revised them from time to time. (pp. 250, 266)
  - The advanced-key pieces form the second layer of entries in the London autograph (c.1740–41). They are substantial works with a family resemblance; only the C sharp major pair has early versions. (p. 272)

## Book II no. 1, C major, BWV 870 (pp. 235–242)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** A praeludial manner "with its roots deep in the tradition". Manual figurations over a pedal point are the archetypal texture for an opening organ piece: compare Bach's Praeludium BWV 545a/1 and Fantasia 'Komm, heiliger Geist' BWV 651, which opens the 'Eighteen' chorales. Here organ style is evoked on the harpsichord, "in some ways more powerful than the real thing". The idiom would not work for organ pedaliter. (pp. 235–236)
- **Type/genre & models (continued):** The opening harmonic formula is a standard one for a pedal point, also found in BWV 939. Its subdominant emphasis may be a relic of Mode VII (Mixolydian) transposed to C with a one-flat signature; compare Georg Muffat, Toccata septima (*Apparatus*, 1690). The formula carries a subdominant, but for Bach it is "by no means wedded to C major" (p. 236); compare BWV 1010 and BWV 785. [filled] (p. 236)
- **Type/genre & models (continued):** Book I's C major prelude puts the scale first and the pedal second. Here it is the other way round: pedal in bb.1–3, then a descending bass scale outline in bb.3–5. (p. 239)
- **Other analysts:**
  - Keller (1950 p.222) and others link the piece to the Italian toccata di durezze e ligature. Ledbetter's verdict: that style continues rather in the French livre d'orgue; this prelude is free figural improvisation in stile moderno. (p. 239)
  - Note 2 lists preludes compared by others: Fischer, *Pieces de clavessin* (1696) nos. IV and V (Oppel 1910); Kuhnau (1689); J. Krieger (1699) (P. Williams). [placement inferred] (n.2)
- **Form, sections, cadences:**
  - The early version BWV 870a has an initial tonic sentence in bb.1–5, then deliberately avoids establishing secondary keys. (p. 239)
  - In the expanded version, the tonally diffuse stretch from the second half of b.5 to the start of b.14 is reprised in the subdominant at bb.20–8. (p. 239)
  - A new bridge runs from the end of b.14 to b.19. It is the "involution" of texture and chromatic density at the midpoint. (p. 239)
  - The reprise arrives at b.20. (p. 239)
  - The bridge is recalled from the end of b.28 to b.30. (p. 239)
  - The ending is expanded with a tonic pedal on the opening harmonic formula. (p. 239)
  - Every bar of BWV 870a is used in the expanded scheme. (p. 239)
- **Versions, sources, revisions:**
  - Four states are listed. (1) An early 17-bar version from the early 1720s (BWV 870a/1). (2) The London-autograph version, expanded as it was entered (BWV 870b). [filled] (p. 235)
  - (3) Bach's substantial revision of bb.14¾–19, entered at the end of the London autograph after he crossed out his first thoughts. [filled; corrects the garbled fraction: b.14¾, not b.14¼] (p. 235)
  - (4) The final version, in Altnickol's MSS (NBA V/6.2 p.156). (p. 235)
  - The first thoughts for b.15 (Ex.8.1b) strike d" five times before the start of b.16. The revision continues a descent of the principal cantus pitches from f" in b.13. (p. 239)
  - bb.18–19 were revised in stages. The second layer of BWV 870b breaks up the tenor and adds chromatic steps to the alto. The final version replaces a bland circle-of-5ths bass with jagged, dissonant leaps. (p. 239)
  - Altnickol's final touches add demisemiquavers, giving concertante brilliance and implying a more majestic movement. (p. 239)
  - Note 1: W.F. Bach sent another copy of an early version to Padre Martini (I-Bc DD 70). Dürr's reconstruction is the most satisfactory; Tomita gives detailed accounts of the revisions. (n.1)
  - Note 3: Keller (1933) published BWV 870a as an organ piece. Ledbetter: it is manualiter with pedal-like figurations, and the bass cannot be disentangled from the tenor. (n.3)
  - Note 4: P. Williams notes that b.19 of "this version" uses the same sequence pattern as the episodes of the Book I B minor fugue. (n.4)
- **Performance:** The demisemiquavers imply "a more majestic movement" than the little organ-style original. (p. 239)

### Fugue
- **Genre/models:**
  - Grew out of an early fughetta. Hofmann places the early fughettas in a south-German verset cycle, and Ledbetter partly agrees. BWV 870a/2 splits a continuous verset-type subject into head and tail (as Book I's G minor and Book II's D major fugues do), producing a galant subject. [filled] (p. 239)
  - A "very straightforward fugue". It creates a galant, characterful subject by separating the head and tail of a traditional one. Compare the subject of J.C.F. Fischer's C major Fuga in *Ariadne Musica*: head from the 5th to the 6th degree, tail a descending semiquaver pattern (Ex.8.2). (p. 240)
  - The signature changed from alla breve (four crotchets) to 2/4, that of a "galant 2/4 movement of the 1720s and 30s" (p. 241). [filled; confirms 2/4] Compare Quantz, Flute Sonata QV I:113/III (Ex.8.3). (pp. 240–241)
  - Note 5: Marpurg (1753) points to imitative use of the head in episodes. The often-made comparison with Book I's C minor fugue "only highlights its relative artlessness". (n.5)
- **Performance:**
  - The two crotchets of the subject should be "emphatic and separated", not phrased off as in Book I's G minor fugue. (p. 241)
  - The fingerings in J.C. Vogler's copy suggest a conservative keyboard technique (see Chapter Six section 3). (p. 241)
  - Note 6: only Michel gives a different time signature for the early version, and his copy is error-prone. (n.6)
  - Note 7: see P. Williams 1993 on the time signature. (n.7)
  - Note 8: Hotteterre's example is a Mascitti violin sonata with separate crotchets. (n.8)
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:**
  - The early fughetta ended with a perfect cadence at bb.67–8. (p. 241)
  - Bach composed an added "concert ending" directly into the London autograph. (p. 241)
- **Later entries / coda:**
  - The coda uses the reversed-hand technique: the left hand pivots over the thumb in expanding intervals beyond an octave. (p. 241)
  - Ex.8.4 shows bb.76–83 before correction, which were relatively straightforward. The correction builds an emphasis on bottom C. (p. 242)
- **Other:**
  - The tessitura is mostly high and reaches down to D only in b.49. In the early fughetta, C was reached only in the final chord. The repeated Cs of the new coda balance the bass pitches, giving a light fugue that contrasts with the bass-heavy prelude. (p. 242)
  - Models for the reversed-hand technique: Rameau's *Méthode* (1724) and *Les Cyclopes*; della Ciaia Op.4 (1727); D. Scarlatti; C.F. Hurlebusch, whose *Compositioni musicali* Bach stocked for sale in 1735–6. Hurlebusch may have prompted the Goldberg Variations. (p. 241)
  - Note 9: see Tomita 1993 p.29. [placement inferred] (n.9)

## Book II no. 2, C minor, BWV 871 (pp. 242–245)

### Prelude
- **Versions, sources, revisions:**
  - Among the first entries: Anna Magdalena Bach copied it in the first layer of the London autograph, c.1739–40. Her hand is often mistaken for Bach's, but she was an unskilful copyist with frequent corrections (Tomita 1990). (p. 242)
  - She copied from a lost autograph, which Altnickol also used in 1744. (p. 243)
  - Missing A naturals and redundant A flats point to a source with the Dorian two-flat signature, so the prelude is unlikely to date from the late 1730s. (p. 243)
  - Only the fugue exists in an early version, so the claimed motivic links between prelude and fugue (J.N. David 1962; Keller 1976) are unlikely to be original. (p. 243)
- **Other analysts:**
  - Schulenberg (1992) finds motivic similarities with the unfinished fugue BWV 906/2. He suggests Bach may have weighed BWV 906 against this pair for Book II. Ledbetter: perhaps the Fantasia was too overtly a display piece, and the B flat major prelude does the same thing more subtly. (p. 243)
  - Keller (1976) and Bergner (1986) call the prelude an allemande. Ledbetter rejects this: it lacks the defining upbeat opening, and its invention counterpoint takes it away from the dance. (p. 243)
- **Type/genre & models:**
  - An invention (finger exercise plus demonstration of composition) cast in a galant binary sonata form. (p. 243)
  - The initial motif looks like a finger "évolution", as in the Book I F sharp minor prelude. (p. 243)
  - It has toccata-like migration of semiquaver scales between parts (bb.5–6). (p. 243)
  - It belongs to the moto perpetuo chorale-prelude tradition: BWV 646 'Wo soll ich fliehen hin', BWV 734. (p. 243)
- **Form, sections, cadences:**
  - Sonata structure on the aria-ritornello shape: a closed tonic period to b.5. (p. 243)
  - The second idea descends into E flat major, reached at b.8. (p. 243)
  - The repeated a's of the closing idea (bb.7–8) stress the dominant 7th of the new key when the descending bass reaches B, followed by a conventional cadence. The accidentals are lost; presumably a♭ and B♭. [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph] (p. 243)
  - Inversions of the opening double counterpoint appear at the main key centres. (p. 243)
  - After the double bar the main motif is pseudo-inverted, as in the binary preludes in D major, E minor and A minor. (pp. 243–244)
  - There is no exact reprise, even when the second idea returns at bb.23–4. (p. 244)
  - Structural links: the climbing chromatic bass in b.4, the descending one in bb.17–18, and the final climbing one in b.27. (p. 244)

### Fugue
- **Genre/models:**
  - One of four early fugues in Agricola's copy (P 595, c.1738–9), all with links to the verset. The subject is generic. Model: J.C. Kerll, *Magnificat septimi toni*, Versus Quia respexit (Ex.8.5), linked to the copy made in Bach's brother's house. [filled] (p. 244)
  - Bach inverts the second half of Kerll's subject; the stepping-crotchet countersubject is the same. (p. 244)
  - Kerll uses stretto and double counterpoint at the 10th but has no inversion or augmentation. (p. 244)
  - Note 10 cites Frescobaldi's *Fiori musicali* (1635) canzona/capriccio "alio modo" pieces and Kerll's *Modulatio organica* (1686). (n.10)
  - Note 11: Ledbetter 1990 pp.60, 105, and Kirnberger: "the material is traditional". [placement inferred] (n.11)
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** Stepping crotchets, taken from Kerll. (p. 244)
- **Form reading:**
  - The first half has features of a partimento fugue: most entries are in the lowest sounding part; the inner part can be sketchy (bb.5–6); connecting passages are standard continuo progressions in style brisé (bb.8–9). (p. 244)
  - The second half has dense inversion and augmentation. It could also be expressed as a partimento, including the combination of the subject with itself in augmentation, which leaves an exposed direct octave at b.14, beats 3–4. [filled] (p. 244)
- **Stretto/inversion/augmentation:**
  - Inversion and augmentation are in the second half. Augmentation appears around b.14. The bass is "reserved for an augmentation entry two thirds of the way through" (p. 244). No bar numbers are given for the inversion. [filled in part] (p. 244)
  - Final stretti at bb.23–6, with quasi-pedal notes at bb.24–5. [filled] (p. 245)
- **Versions, sources:**
  - An early version exists, in P 595 according to the C sharp major discussion. (pp. 243, 250)
  - It went into Book II with very little change but shows signs of an original Dorian signature. (p. 266)
- **Performance/Other:**
  - Ends on an arpeggiated chord "implying a clavier rather than organ intention", possibly an evocation of organ style on the harpsichord. [filled] (p. 245)

## Book II no. 3, C sharp major, BWV 872 (pp. 245–251)

### Prelude
- **Versions, sources, revisions:**
  - (1) Early version copied by Anna Magdalena, c.1738, in P 226. It is in C major, with plain five-part minim chords marked 'arpeggio' in the first section (NBA V/6.2 p.344). (p. 245)
  - (2) A lost draft in C sharp with elaborate arpeggiation, from which Bach made the London autograph fair copy. (p. 245)
  - (3) Later improvements to the tenor line in the lost draft, used by Altnickol in 1744. This is "Bach's final and best thoughts". (p. 245)
  - Kroll's BG edition prints versions 2 and 3 side by side. Ledbetter believes the prelude and fugue originated together. (p. 245)
  - Tenor revisions in the London autograph avoid tenor quavers doubling the right-hand main note. In b.1, beat 2, g becomes e to avoid doubling the right-hand g'; similar changes in b.3 and later bars. (p. 248)
  - The final version gives the tenor a rounded shape in bb.1–3. In b.4, beat 4, d replaces g. (pp. 248–249)
  - The tenor is the most prominent part and the vehicle for the legatissimo touch. (p. 248)
  - The d for g at b.4, beat 4, stresses g as the 7th at the start of b.5. The turning figure at the start of b.6 marks the arrival on the dominant (and on the tonic at b.20). The arpeggio figure at b.7 is made stronger. [filled; displaced, restored: the fragment "is a 7th at the beginning", formerly under the fugue, belongs here] (p. 249)
- **Harmony/figured-bass abstract:**
  - A standard contrapuntal cliché underlies both sections. The first section decorates it chordally (Ex.8.6d, bb.1–6); the second section works it fugally (Ex.8.6e, bb.25–30). (pp. 246–247)
  - Compare Mauritius Vogt, *Conclave thesauri* (Prague 1719), and his lists of contrapuntal clichés ('De phantasia, et inventionibus'). Vogt gives a 'Phantasia simplex' and then works it fugally (Ex.8.6a–c). Whether Bach knew Vogt is unknown. [filled] (pp. 246–247)
- **Type/genre & models:**
  - Compare Fischer's Harpeggio 'Polymnia' (*Musicalischer Parnassus*), which has three layers of legatissimo (Ex.8.7). (p. 247)
  - Playing Bach's C major version with bass minims and repeated tenor quavers gives a similar effect. (p. 247)
  - Kittel's recommendation of this prelude for organ may mean a white-note polyphonic reading. (p. 247)
- **Form, sections, cadences:**
  - The most dissonant passage is saved as a climax near the end (bb.14–19). (p. 247)
  - Its harshest point is a 9/7 on the leading note at b.16, first chord. Forkel, Kroll and Dehnhard "corrected" the bass e to d (KB V/6.2 p.215). (p. 247)
  - A final minor tinge in bb.21–3. (p. 247)
- **Performance:**
  - The one substantial alteration in the Allegro (b.34) avoids all three parts leaping upward together. (p. 249)
  - Dolmetsch suggests playing the grace notes (hooks in P 226 and the London autograph) as quavers, which gives "the most satisfactory effect in b.30" (1946 p.117). (p. 249)

### Fugue
- **Genre/models:**
  - Comes from the verset tradition. (p. 249)
  - Subject type: a short quaver upbeat figure to a downbeat crotchet, here three quavers. Compare workings by Speer (1697) and Murschhauser (1703). The closest is the anonymous *Wegweiser* Versus 20 (Ex.8.8). (pp. 249–250)
  - Later versions drop the strict three-part texture at b.30 for toccata demisemiquavers "of pure joie de vivre" (p. 249). [filled] (p. 249)
- **Form reading / disputes:**
  - Bullivant (1960) says it is not a fugue at all. (p. 249)
  - The subject is variously taken as the first four notes (Iliffe), the first six (Riemann), or the "first bar and a half" (Tovey). Ledbetter blames a lack of perspective on the genre: the textbook rules began with Marpurg's *Abhandlung von der Fuge* (1753–4). His verdict: the subject is a verset type (three-quaver upbeat), so the debate misreads the genre. [displaced, restored] (p. 249)
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** four stages, shown in parallel as Ex.8.9 (Appendix A).
  - (1) BWV 872a/2: 19 bars in C major, in Michel's copy (P 563). Probably a teaching piece from Weimar or Cöthen. Michel pairs it with BWV 993, which is not significant. (p. 250)
  - (2) Agricola's copy, c.1738 (P 595): 30 bars in C major. (p. 250)
  - (3) Bach's revising score in the second layer of the London autograph, c.1740: 35 bars in C sharp major. (p. 250)
  - Version 3 adds bb.25–9, "important structurally for their two bars of dominant pedal", composed straight into the source and heavily corrected. (p. 250)
  - Version 3 also adds two extra bars of tonic pedal at the end. (p. 250)
  - Tomita: augmentation was probably an afterthought, after Bach had entered the outer parts of b.25. (pp. 250–251)
  - Bach first extended demisemiquavers into the second half of b.30 and the first half of b.31, then added them at bb.8–10, 17–18, 24, 28–9 and the first half of b.30. (p. 251)
  - (4) Further demisemiquavers and new corrections to bb.25–9, from a lost copy; reflected in Altnickol 1744 (Ex.8.9c). (p. 251)
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:**
  - Earliest version: cadences on the dominant (b.4) and the tonic (b.11). It shortens the crotchets to quavers with quaver rests. (p. 249)
  - In the revised version, the exposition phrases run to a tonic cadence at b.4 and a dominant cadence at b.7. The new semiquaver idea is introduced at b.8. [check: these cadence keys are the reverse of the earliest version's dominant b.4 / tonic b.11; probably different versions] (p. 251)
  - Dominant pedal in bb.25–9 (two bars of it); tonic pedal at the end. (p. 250)
  - The strict three-part texture is abandoned at b.30, running into toccata-type demisemiquavers for a cadenza-like ending. (pp. 249, 251)
- **Stretto/inversion/augmentation:**
  - Already in the earliest version: "witty play on inversion and diminution". (p. 249)
  - Augmentation around b.25 (Tomita). (pp. 250–251)

## Book II no. 4, C sharp minor, BWV 873 (pp. 251–255)

### Prelude
- **Versions, sources, revisions:**
  - No earlier version. The scale and style suggest the late 1730s, perhaps composed for Book II. (p. 251)
  - Faint signs in Altnickol's 1744 copy (missing A sharps, superfluous A naturals) of an original in C minor with a Dorian signature (KB V/6.2 p.268). (p. 251)
  - The leaf is missing from the London autograph. Anon.12's copy (P 416) heads it "Prelude", so it probably belonged to the second layer, c.1740–1, in Bach's hand. (p. 251)
  - Ornament notation is the main problem. Bach mixed old signs (hook for the appoggiatura, wavy line for the trill) with newer ones (small note, other sign). (pp. 251–252)
  - Dürr's 'Fassung A' follows P 587 for its accurate hook signs (NBA V/6.2 pp.16, 168). (p. 252)
  - The London-autograph tradition generally gives the maturer version. (p. 252)
  - Note 24: F. Neumann 1966 gives a facsimile of the relevant P 416 page. (n.24)
- **Type/genre & models:**
  - Often compared with the E flat Sinfonia BWV 791. (p. 251)
  - Cöthen violin sonatas show movements just as large and galant. (p. 251)
  - More restrained than BWV 682 'Vater unser' from Clavier-Übung III. (p. 251)
- **Performance:**
  - In some variants small notes stand for quavers (b.49, b.61); others imply longer values. (p. 252)
  - The London-tradition cadence at b.4 may be a fuller notation of how Altnickol's simpler version should be played: a small note of a whole dotted crotchet. (p. 252)
  - In b.36 (Altnickol), appoggiaturas of different lengths stand side by side: "variety is the essence". Model: the Sarabande of Partita BWV 829. (p. 252)
  - Appoggiaturas can be shaded off with rubato. (p. 252)
  - The London alto appoggiatura at b.6, beat 1, is bland next to Altnickol's reading. (p. 252)
  - The cantus appoggiatura in b.12, last beat, makes octaves with the bass if played as a quaver. P 416 replaces it with a mordent sign (BG XIV p.222). (p. 252)
- **Form:** A much-expanded version of the form of Book I's E major and F sharp major preludes. It is characteristic of Book II and shared with the large F major, F sharp major and minor, and A flat major preludes, "representing all three layers of the London autograph" (p. 252). It is a ritornello form. [filled] (pp. 252–253)

### Fugue
- **Voices:** Three, implied by "all three parts" at bb.66–8. (p. 255)
- **Genre/models:**
  - The first Book II fugue in an overtly dance metre: a lively gigue type in 12/16 (signature partly garbled), as in the French Suite in G major and the English Suite in D minor. (p. 253)

## Book II no. 5, D major, BWV 874 (pp. 255–261)

### Prelude
- **Performance (rhythm):**
  - First question: whether dotted figures are assimilated to the triplets. Bach could have notated the rhythm differently when he meant it (symbols lost). Tovey (AB II p.166) is cited. ## Book II no. 12, F minor, BWV 881 (pp. 288–290)

### Prelude
- **Versions, sources:**
  - The leaf is missing from the London autograph, so it is reconstructed from copies (Jones, special note, AB II p.207). (p. 288)
  - Altnickol 1744 (P 430) gives Bach's final thoughts; Kirnberger's Am.B.57(2) gives an earlier version. (p. 288)
- **Type/genre & models:**
  - The closest of all the Book II preludes to the galant sonata style of Bach's sons. (p. 288)
  - Opening appoggiaturas; a written-out Anschlag at b.58; four-bar phrasing; two contrasted characters sharing the first eight bars (compare the D major prelude). (pp. 288–289)
  - The semiquaver figure of bb.5–8 resembles Rameau's 1724 batteries. (p. 289)
  - The same appoggiatura figure appears in the Musical Offering's three-part Ricercar, the E flat fugue BWV 998/2, and the fugue BWV 906/2. (p. 289)
- **Form, sections, cadences:**
  - The two ideas alternate up to b.20. (p. 289)
  - A closing phrase combines their elements; from b.24 the falling appoggiatura steps move into bass and tenor. (p. 289)
  - The second section develops these elements until a "very expressive condensed reprise" at b.56. (p. 289)
  - An invention/binary-sonata amalgam. (p. 289)
- **Performance:**
  - Not necessarily for pianoforte. (p. 289)
  - No manual changes are needed, nor a pièce croisée, contrary to Landowska (1965). The semiquaver figure relies on the intimacy of the hands on one manual. (p. 289)

### Fugue
- **Subject:**
  - A traditional subject, an "old warhorse", recreated as a bourrée (Jenne 1974), as in Book I's F major fugue. (p. 290)
  - Bach shows "great resource in his melodic use of the diminished 7th" (p. 289) in his mature works. The diminished 7th is used expressively without cliché; compare Book I's D minor fugue. [filled] (pp. 289–290)
- **Countersubject(s):** None retained. (p. 290)
- **Form reading:**
  - Binary dance/sonata in minor-key form, with 2+2+4 phrasing (bb.17–24 etc.). (p. 290)
  - Relative major at bb.24–31. (p. 290)
  - Dominant section-ending cadence at the halfway point, b.40, the only internal one. (p. 290)
  - The second half is bass-led. (p. 290)
- **Later entries:**
  - Tonic entry from b.40; its semiquaver tail is extended to the highest textures (bb.46–7). (p. 290)
  - A bass phrase from the second half of b.55 extends the head in the subdominant, answered from b.61 in the tonic. (p. 290)
  - Final entries: subdominant (cantus b.71), then tonic (middle voice b.74). (p. 290)
- **Episodes:**
  - The episode at bb.17–24 is inverted at bb.50–3 and inverted again at bb.66–71. (p. 290)
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** Dominant pedal at bb.50–3, and another at bb.80–3. (p. 290)
- **Other:**
  - Reichardt (1782) heard "the deepest and sweetest feelings of grief" and compared the fugue to Strasbourg Cathedral. Knab (1914) heard the prelude's "sobbing" motif. Ledbetter treats both as later empfindsam misreadings, but agrees with Reichardt that the fugue refutes the view that fugues are for the eye (Pasquali). (p. 290)
  - "No notes clearly attached" among the surviving endnotes.
