## Book I no. 13, F sharp major, BWV 858 (pp. 194–197)

### Prelude
- Type/genre & models: its "lighter French character" and 12/16 signature (originally 12/8) set this subtle, delicate prelude apart from the more cantabile E major prelude and from the G major Invention [filled] (p. 194). Tovey (1924 p.105) says the original 12/8 signature should warn against too lively a tempo; Dürr reads the change of signature as meaning livelier [filled; confirms Tovey] (p. 194).
- Form, sections, cadences: a binary structure dividing at b.15½. L. calls this type unique in the WTC but like the first movement of Brandenburg Concerto 2 on a much larger scale (pp. 195–196). The first-half imitations of the head are at the octave (bb.1, 7, 12). The second-half entries are pairs imitated at the 5th (p. 196). The opening sequential continuation outlines a descending scale with 7–6 suspensions decorated by échappées. L. says this makes a motif out of an essentially ornamental figure, as in Quantz (Ex.7.18) (p. 194).
- Harmony: in the later version the harmonic 2:1 division is "more exact" (p. 196).
- Versions, sources, revisions: early version bb.1–2 in Ex.7.17. The figure is a hand-shape on the raised-key triad: in the early version the 5th finger takes d#" and, after the repeated c#", "the hand gradually opens out into the rest of the scale" [filled]. The later version obscures this, particularly through the trill, which is not in the autograph but appears in some closely associated sources (p. 194). In the early version the second half ran close-spaced entries of the head from b.15½. They went from A sharp minor through rising 4ths (D sharp major, G sharp minor, C sharp major) back to F sharp major (NBA V/6.1 p.185; Ex.7.19) (p. 196). The later version adds a closing idea in bb.17–18. This brings out the paired entries at the 5th and makes a more substantial development before F sharp returns (p. 196).
- Performance: tempo should not be too lively (see above) (p. 194).
- Other: a similar hand-shape is part of the material of the Book II F sharp major prelude (p. 194). Endnotes, attributed by number order and not certain: n.77 Mather & Karns 1987 pp.282–5 (probably on the French character); n.78 Busoni grouped "this prelude" with the F sharp major and minor fugues as elementary repeated-note studies, and L. says his point undermines that opinion; n.79 Quantz 1752 Tab.XIV Fig.15 (= Ex.7.18); n.80 Griepenkerl (1820) gives the same figure as one of Bach's finger exercises; n.81 the autograph has c" as the 4th semiquaver of the RH motif on beat 3 of b.10, where a few sources and editors after Kroll have a', and the c" "demands the thumb on f'" (p. 364).

### Fugue
- Motivic web: the fugue is woven from standard shapes. The opening of CS1 (cantus b.3) reverses, inverts and diminishes the end of the subject, and "much play is made with the cadential shape" of the subject's first four notes [filled] (p. 196).
- Subject: "hangs on the first inversion of the tonic chord" (Keller 1976 p.92). It touches the lower tonic in the bass at crucial points (b.7, b.22) (p. 196). It outlines a series of descending 3rds, given as "f", d", b', g'" [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph; presumably f#", d#", b', g#'] (p. 196).
- Countersubject(s): CS1 (bb.3–4: "e", c", a'" [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph; presumably e#", c#", a#']) also has falling 3rds. Spitta (III p.188) notes it works in double counterpoint at the 12th with the subject, forming 3rds above or below it (bb.15–16 and 32–3) (p. 196). CS2 develops the motif of 3rds after the first main cadence (b.7). Its falling 3rds become more extended up to bb.23–5. It "works as such only in its inverted version" (bb.12f, 21f), which links it to the subject's shape (p. 197).
- Exposition: seven bars (bb.1–7) (p. 197).
- Later entries: the cantus passages at bb.7½–11, b.18 and bb.34–5 are play on the cadential shape of the subject's first four notes, i.e. head-motif fragments, not full entries [filled] (p. 196).
- Sections, cadences, pedals: the main structural articulation comes from the bass touching the low tonic (b.7, b.22) together with perfect cadences at b.11, b.23 and b.33 (p. 196). The structure is "unusual": an exposition of seven bars, then two sections each roughly twice its length (bb.7–23 and 23–35). The second is "a kind of free contrapuntal inversion of the first" (compare bb.11ff and 28ff). The most decisive intermediate cadence, at bb.22–3, marks the changeover (p. 197).
- Genre/models: inverted CS2 is identical with the bass figure of the duet 'Wir eilen mit schwachen, doch emsigen Schritten' from Cantata 78. There it is the rhetorical figure gradatio, representing joyful progress (p. 197). (n.87 on the expression of joy, citing Pirro and Schweitzer, may belong here, though the numbering argues against it; see Unplaced notes.)
- Other: n.82 ("see also the subject of the F sharp minor Fuga, No.11 of Ariadne Musica") and n.83 (Dreyfus 1996 pp.158–9 sees the concealment of "this entry" as a connoisseur's touch; L. suggests it may belong to a progression from obscurity to clarity across the series of entries) belong to F sharp major or F sharp minor [attribution uncertain] (p. 364).

## Book I no. 14, F sharp minor, BWV 859 (pp. 197–200)

### Prelude
- Type/genre & models: built on Couperin's progrès de quartes; the hands open out to a 10th (b.8). In effect a two-part invention in 2:1 binary proportion [filled] (p. 197).
- Form, sections, cadences: a mid-"way point" at b.12½, marked by a dominant cadence [filled] (p. 197). The first half expands the rising 3rd of the basic material in two parts. The second half returns to the three-part texture of the opening with isolated quavers, a skeletal countersubject (cf. RH b.2) like the bass quavers at the opening of the B minor two-part Invention (p. 197). These quavers expand into chords (bb.14–15), and the 4ths motif is inverted into its rising form (p. 197). At b.22 the dominant inversion of the opening (bb.12–13) returns transposed down a 5th to the tonic, giving an intensified version of the opening's constricted mood (p. 197).
- Other: one of the most successful demonstrations of developing expressive possibilities together with shapes. Bach associates F sharp minor with "wistful melancholy" here, in the Book II pair, and in the third movement of BWV 1015 (p. 198).
- Performance: the two trill-sign forms are used as obvious equivalents in this prelude (b.12, b.18). This is noted in the G minor prelude commentary: the crossbeamed and plain trill signs are equivalent [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph; the sign shapes themselves are lost] (p. 203).

### Fugue
- Style: sets stile antico features (6/4 metre, cadential trill, flexible lines) against moderno affects (a rising chromatic supplication, a 'sigh' countersubject) [filled] (p. 198).
- Subject: "may be a reworking of the learned chromatic type", as in Froberger's Ricercar I (1656) (Ex.7.20) [filled] (p. 198). It is combined with a diatonic modal outline like that of the first Kyrie BWV 669 (Clavier-Übung III) (p. 198). L. calls it one of the most subtly expressive of Bach's subjects and "a great test of the maturity of the player". Its pauses can express "an extraordinary depth of experience" (p. 198). Elsewhere he says it may be a reworking of a traditional chromatic ricercar subject (p. 212). Subject and countersubject have few leaps (p. 198).
- Countersubject(s): continues the developing quaver movement in a related affect and comes "close to being its inversion" (p. 198). There is only "a moment of inversion of the countersubject at b.22" (p. 199). n.84: the countersubject's 'sigh' motif has a frequently cited parallel in BWV 618 'O Lamm Gottes unschuldig', which slurs the paired quavers. Pirro 1907 lists other examples [attribution to this fugue probable, not certain] (p. 364).
- Episodes: b.7, bb.11–14 etc., closely woven from subject elements (p. 198).
- Later entries: the only significant move to the relative major (bb.14–15) is "instantly cancelled by a tonic minor entry" [bar of entry not given] (p. 198).
- Stretto/inversion: the subject is inverted (the "aspirational subject") in the second half, in four entries: dominant inverso (alto), dominant recto (cantus b.25), tonic recto (tenor b.29), tonic inverso (bass b.32). The text gives the alto inverso entry first as b.15 and then, in the next sentence, as b.20; the fill takes b.20 as intended [filled; both readings kept, flagged]. The countersubject is inverted only at b.22 (p. 199). Two prototypes are offered: Lully's orchestral passacaille, where the descending minor tetrachord is inverted to rise five steps, giving this subject's outline; and the descending chromatic tetrachord of the operatic lament, of which the inverted subject may be seen as a version (pp. 199–200).
- Sections: the octave transposition of bb.7–10 at bb.28–31 is shared with the G sharp minor fugue (bb.7–8 / 11–12) (p. 212).
- Genre/models: similarities with the G sharp minor fugue (apparently based on a Kerll verset) suggest the two are "roughly contemporary" with the same objectives (pp. 198, 212). Inverting the countersubject would be expressively appropriate, as the often-cited parallel with the chorale 'O Mensch bewein dein Sünde groß', which closes Part 1 of the St Matthew Passion, shows [filled] (pp. 198–199).
- Sections, cadences: 2:1 proportion, with a dominant cadence at b.20. A first-half cantus entry at b.15 is matched by the final soprano entry at b.37 [filled] (p. 199).
- Other: L. argues against a 'personal' reading. Bach demonstrates how technical and expressive means are developed, and a connoisseurship of emotions is as essential as one of technique (p. 200).

## Book I no. 15, G major, BWV 860 (pp. 200–202)

### Prelude
- Type/genre: "the last of the finger-exercise preludes". Both it and the D minor prelude originally had 15 bars. Compared with the D minor, the G major "uses a more open hand position" and moves the hand around the keyboard faster [filled] (p. 200).
- Form, sections: semiquaver movement in both hands from b.13 (p. 201). Bars 16½–18 use an évolution covering a 7th, given as such by Marpurg (1755 etc. Tab.X Fig.1) (p. 200). It is the G major prelude as a whole (against the D minor), not this figure, that "transfers the hand around the keyboard more rapidly" [filled; corrects the earlier reading] (p. 200).
- Versions, sources, revisions: time-signature history (KB V/6.1 p.265). Originally both staves had C, "exactly as the D minor" but without the triplet indication [filled; corrects the guess of 12/8]. An intermediate version gave both staves C with 24/16 [filled], a signature already used in BWV 617 (Weimar 1715–16, Stinson 1996 p.17). In P 415 the signatures are split: 24/16 on the upper staff and C on the lower [filled]. Each signature applies to the motif it precedes, not literally to the stave (p. 200). L.'s reasons for this choice include a wish to include representatives of a wide range of time signatures, as in teaching lists, and a lighter metre than the D minor's (Kirnberger) [filled] (pp. 200–201). The additions in bb.14–17½ greatly expand the both-hands semiquaver feature. They make better use of the top range and build a more effective climax (p. 201).
- Other: n.86 (Renwick 2001) may belong to the G major or G minor pair [unplaced].

### Fugue
- Subject: Keller (1976 p.97) relates its 7ths to the évolution passage ending the prelude. R.L. Marshall (1972 I p.188) points to its rhythmic symmetry, a more subtle shape than the usual head-and-tail (Vordersatz/Fortspinnung) formula of BWV 543/2 and 564/3 (p. 202).
- Exposition: first tonic exposition recto [bars not given] (p. 201).
- Later entries: a second tonic exposition in inversion, bb.20–31. Two E minor entries, b.38 and b.43; the b.43 entry is inverso and begins on the 3rd of the scale, whereas most inverso entries begin on the 5th. A stretto recto in B minor at b.51. The final entry recto at b.79 begins on the 3rd of G major, a "striking and climactic reharmonization" (pp. 201–202).
- Stretto/inversion: inversion plus stretto is "the main contrapuntal argument". The inversion may have been suggested by the gigue metre, since gigues invert their theme after the double bar (p. 201).
- Episodes: concerto-style episodes. A bariolage-type figure is introduced at b.9 and immediately inverted at b.10, "presaging the subsequent plot". Demisemiquavers are added from b.47 (p. 201).
- Form reading: of the Book I fugues, the one closest to the concerto-allegro scheme of Vivaldi's Op.3 and Op.4. It follows Vivaldi in having a second ritornello statement in the tonic (Chapter Four section 11) (p. 201). It contrasts with the A minor fugue, an exposition fugue with inversion and stretto but no concerto episodes. Here Bach is content with "a few broad effects" (p. 201).
- Genre/models: Vivaldi concertos arranged at Weimar c.1713; the harpsichord part of Brandenburg 5, first movement. Dirksen (1992) proposes that Brandenburg 5 shows the harpsichord rivalling the violin, with links to Vivaldi's 'Great Mogul' concerto Op.7/ii No.5 (BWV 594) (pp. 201–202).

- F: figure introduced b.9, inverted b.10
- F: entry b.38 E minor
- F: entry b.43 E minor, inverso, starting on scale degree 3

## Book I no. 16, G minor, BWV 861 (pp. 202–206)

### Prelude
- Form, sections: bb.1–11 rest on the material of b.1, bb.12–19 on that of b.2 [filled] (p. 202).
- Type/genre: the opening is a sonata-Adagio messa di voce realised as a trill, hence "much discussion about the proper performance of the trill" [filled] (p. 202).
- Performance: an extended discussion of how to perform the long trill. Quantz recommends long, slower trills for sad pieces (1752 IX para.2). C.P.E. Bach says the trill begins on the upper note (1753 II.3 para.5) but also shows the ribattuta, in the context of a 'doppelt-cadence'. Agricola (1757) treats the ribattuta only as a singing exercise. The intended effect is a messa di voce (pp. 202–203). Bach notated the first trill sign with a long tail, implying a long trill. The crossbeam in the sign is not significant (p. 203). L. recommends "a version of the ribattuta": begin on the main note, slowly, perhaps after a slight pause, then get quicker; avoid "the effect of an alarm clock" (p. 203). He quotes C.P.E. Bach 1753 III para.7 on playing Adagios (p. 203). n.85 gives literature on the trill (Klotz, Troeger, Badura-Skoda, F. Neumann, Schulenberg 1992 p.187) (p. 364).
- Type/genre: may originally have been conceived as an exercise for this effect (p. 203).

### Fugue
- Genre/models: the subject has been compared since Seiffert (1899 p.230) [filled; confirms Seiffert] with the E flat Fuga of Fischer's Ariadne Musica. Schulenberg (1992 p.187) shows that the structure is also similar, so Fischer is "very probably the proximate model" (pp. 203–204). The subject is a generic verset subject: Langloz partimenti (Ex.7.21a); W.F. Bach D minor Fantasia F.19 (Riemann 1890 I p.102); Telemann chorale trio 'Christ lag in Todesbanden', c.1730–36 (Ex.7.21b) (p. 204). Fischer's Fuga (Ex.7.22) has two halves: an ordinary exposition cadencing on B flat (b.5), then a double stretto (bb.5–6) and a triple stretto (bb.7–8) (Fischer's bar numbers) (p. 204).
- Subject: two halves, the second combining in counterpoint with the first, so it is designed for stretto. Bach separates the halves with a rest and lengthens the leading note (Sumikura 1977 p.236), as in the Book II D major fugue. The source versions make the 7th leap explicit; Bach makes it subtler (p. 204). The pairs of crotchets at the end of each half are onbeat/offbeat (quantitas intrinseca). The subject starts as an upbeat to a weak beat, with the strong beat on the leading note (p. 204).
- Sections, cadences: main cadences at the 1:3 proportion points: relative major at b.12, tonic at b.24 and b.34. The structural argument (texture, contrapuntal density, pitch level) gives a 1:2 proportion with the halfway point at b.18½ (p. 205). The driving chromatic bass at b.21½ contrasts with the relaxing diatonic descent in b.33 (p. 206).
- Form reading: Bach greatly expands Fischer's plan and makes the articulation subtler (p. 205). He makes "much more of the stretti as a climaxing effect". Four-part texture is kept for the end of each half. A two-part stretto at the 12th (bb.17–18) returns inverted at the octave in the three-part stretto of bb.28–9, the main climax. The final tenor entry, over a descending scale, is in five parts. Major-key entries begin from b.12 [filled] (pp. 205–206).
- Performance: crotchet pairs as strong and weak syllables, probably part of the "cantable Art" of the 1723 Inventions title-page (p. 204).

Checkable claims:
- F: cadence b.12 relative major (Bb)
- F: cadence b.24 tonic; cadence b.34 tonic
- F: structural midpoint b.18½
- F: chromatic bass line from b.21½; diatonic bass descent b.33
- F: subject begins upbeat; strong beat falls on the leading note; a rest separates the two halves

## Book I no. 17, A flat major, BWV 862 (pp. 206–209)

### Prelude
- Form, sections: an aria-ritornello sonata form. An 8-bar head with a tail to the dominant; the head in E flat at bb.18–21; scales bb.22–6; the tail inverted at bb.26–34 (D flat to A flat); the return from b.35 [filled] (p. 207).
- Versions, sources, transposition: a violinistic style in A flat prompts theories of an original in another key: Siegele proposes a raised G major (violin idioms; n.32 earlier in the chapter), Stauffer points to the rhythm of the organ Praeludium BWV 541/1 [filled]. It is BWV 541/1 that "could be as late as 1730" [filled] (p. 206). L.: "one could make an equally strong case for an A major original", which gives an open e" string for the bariolage in bb.13–16. The second movement of BWV 1015 (A major) shares the figurations, and there the violin-like figures come only in the harpsichord part. Plenty of similarities exist in other keys too: the Preludio of BWV 1006 (E major); the F major Invention (same falling 3rds); the B minor Invention (character head plus sequential tail) (pp. 206–207). The sources favour A flat [filled] (p. 206). n.88 (Dürr KB V/6.1 pp.189, 362; 1984 p.43) probably relates to the transposition question (p. 364).
- Other: the formal description above ends by remarking that all this happens "in a piece which uses no more than ten notes" [filled] (p. 207). n.91: Suthoff-Groß says only four Book I preludes use fewer than 12 notes and this is the only one using fewer than 11. n.89: Bach does not use A flat as a tonic in instrumental works outside the 48 (Keller 1976 p.102). n.90: Candé links the prelude to an aria in Vivaldi's Ottone in Villa (1713) (p. 364).

### Fugue
- Subject: a square canzona-type subject that Bach makes subtler by starting on a weak beat (p. 208). It is related to the Mode I subject type of Pachelbel's Magnificat fugues (Ex.7.23a, n.92), which also relates to the subjects of the Book I D sharp minor fugue and of The Art of Fugue. The major-key equivalent is in the Langloz partimenti (Ex.7.23b). Mattheson (1739) used it to demonstrate genera of counterpoint (n.93: Butler 1983) (pp. 207–208).
- Countersubject(s): sinuous, offsetting the subject's squareness. The same type is used with square triadic subjects in BWV 546/2 (b.107f; P. Williams 1980 I p.154) and the manualiter fughetta 'Allein Gott' (CÜ III), which is related to BWV 547/2 (p. 208).
- Exposition: a full 'tutti' exposition [bars not given] (p. 208).
- Later entries: extra tutti in the tonic at b.10, after the first non-modulating solo break (bb.8–9). 'Tutti' entries in F minor (b.13), then B flat minor (bb.16–19). At bb.23–4 two entries have the 6th leap expanded to a 7th, becoming a dominant 7th and climbing in 4ths from E flat to D flat. Subdominant emphasis around b.25. A tonic reprise and final tonic exposition at b.27. A final broadly harmonized tonic entry in the last three bars (pp. 208–209).
- Episodes: built on sequential development of the subject's tail (b.3) in triple counterpoint at the 12th and octave (bb.11–12, inverted at bb.14–15, inverted again at bb.19–20). A working of the head follows at bb.21–2. A long bass sequence with subject shapes in the upper parts leads to b.27 (pp. 208–209).
- Form reading: Vivaldian ritornello, with tutti expositions at the start and end and Vivaldi's extra tonic tutti. L. rejects the "various formal interpretations" (details in Czaczkes 1982 I p.182) that inflict "a rigid sectionality where Bach plainly sought fluidity" (p. 208). It is a 'Spielfuge' ending with rhetorical clarity rather than contrapuntal complexity, as in the C minor fugue. It makes an ordinary subject memorable, as BWV 544/2 does (p. 209).
- Genre/models: the 7th expansion anticipates Contrapunctus 4 bb.61ff of The Art of Fugue (Tovey 1931 p.9) (p. 208).
- Versions, sources: the later version adds alto quavers "a | a'" [GAP: not recoverable, music glyph; presumably ab|ab'] over the barline b.10–11. This marks b.10 as a tutti, and the logic is clearer in P 415 than in modern editions. The awkwardness of b.10 is untypical (p. 208).

## Book I no. 18, G sharp minor, BWV 863 (pp. 209–212)

### Prelude
- Versions, transposition: L. rejects pairing this prelude with the C sharp minor prelude. The E sharps often lack their sharps, even in P 415, which points to a one-flat original, i.e. G minor/Mode II [filled] (p. 209). The reasons are "yet stronger" for the fugue, so this pair probably belongs with the D sharp minor fugue as pieces Bach transposed to fill their place (p. 209). n.94: Dürr 1984 p.45. He later toned down this view for the prelude but stayed convinced of a G minor origin for the fugue (KB V/6.1 pp.190, 364) (p. 364).

## Book I no. 19, A major, BWV 864 (pp. 212–218)

### Prelude
- Type/genre: a demonstration of triple counterpoint, mainly in the art of laying it out "in the most expressive, sonorous, and playable way" (p. 213). It is a subtle major-key working of the lamento-bass cliché. Compare the conventional treatments in BWV 901/2 (the basis of the Book II A flat fugue) and Ex.7.26, where each element stays in the same part (p. 213). The 7–6 appoggiaturas migrate to the middle part with second-practice resolution by leap. The bass moves round the circle of 5ths, while the running semiquavers take over the lamento outline (p. 214).
- Form, sections: the block of triple counterpoint is graded by trickiness. Opening: crotchets in the bass, melodic line on top, filler in the middle. bb.4–5: hands reversed, running part in the LH. bb.9–10: running part in the middle, migrating between hands (p. 213). The semiquaver theme is bent downward at its first minor-key appearance (b.12) because of the four-octave compass (p. 213; n.100). The last permutation turns the 7ths between middle and bass (bb.1–2) into 2nds (bb.20–1). The middle part has the theme bb.18 to 21; octave transposition avoids the flaw of a repeat (p. 213). Episodes: bb.6–8 on the appoggiaturas; b.11 introduces a descending scale; bb.14–17 combine the two, graded by playing difficulty. At the end the scales take over and open out to the widest range, probably to prepare the fugue (p. 214).
- Versions: an adjustment to the LH on beat 2 of b.9 shows concern for playability. "Some sources give the bass here in its usual form", but L. calls this "only pedantry" [filled] (p. 213).
- Performance: cultivate characterizing three simultaneous lines (the "singable manner"). Fade out the offbeat resolutions of the 7ths in the middle part (b.2 etc.). The chromatic ornament on beat 3 of b.1 is a kind of inverted Anschlag (as in the F minor Sinfonia). Perhaps hold the first semiquaver coulé-fashion. BWV 645 slurs the two semiquavers and dots the quaver (p. 214).
- Other: n.98 (Stinson 1996 p.109, comparison with BWV 612) and n.99 (Ledbetter 1990 pp.56–61) fall between the G sharp minor fugue and this prelude [attribution uncertain] (p. 365).

### Fugue
- Subject: two subjects (a due soggetti). It refers to the obligo of moving only by leap (Berardi; Frescobaldi's Recercar ottava, 'obligo di non uscir di grado', 1615; Wegweiser Ex.7.27a), though not rigorously (p. 214). The subject's falling 3rds and rising 4ths resemble the final section of Corelli Op.3 No.12 (Ex.7.27b) (p. 215).
- Answer: the alto at b.2 is shown by the soprano's continuation to be part of the subject, "in which case the opening seems to be a double fugue on one subject" [filled] (p. 216). The answer changes the intervals of the subject in order to combine at this point. This freedom is developed, notably in the free bass entries at b.16 and b.33 [filled] (p. 216).
- Form, sections: Bach "contrives a much more substantial and varied piece" than the partimento type by introducing a second subject (bb.23ff). Partimento traits: many entries in the lowest part in bb.1–20, successive bass entries at b.4 and b.6, free voicing (b.25) [filled] (pp. 215–216). Plan: bb.1–20; link bb.20–1; bb.22–42 with the second subject; recapitulating sections bb.42–9 and 49–54 [filled] (p. 217).
- Later entries: bass b.16; bass b.33; first and second subjects together at bb.23–4 (Ex.7.28c) (pp. 216–217).
- Stretto: bb.25–6, an ordinary figured-bass cliché in 3rds given "the very felicitous effect of a triple stretto" (p. 216).
- Episodes / figured-bass clichés: upper parts in 3rds at bb.25–6 and in 6ths at bb.42–3 (p. 216).
- Genre/models: learned prototypes (two subjects, obligo) and free ones (partimento: the opening as a double fugue "on one subject, as are some of Handel's partimenti" [filled], p. 216). Interlocking syncopations as a "rhythmic Chinese puzzle" in Frescobaldi (Cento partite 1637, vars. 17–18, 105–6), Froberger (D minor Toccata, 1649 No.2, final section) and Corelli Op.3 No.12 (Rome 1689) (pp. 214–216).
- Versions, sources: there are few differences between early and later versions except at bb.4–5, reworked when Bach entered the fugue into P 415 (KB V/6.1 p.29). Czaczkes (I p.195) derives the second subject from the original alto at bb.4–5. L.: from a combination of alto and cantus (p. 217). The final version avoids pitch stagnation and avoids anticipating the second subject (p. 218). Dürr's suggestion of a possible G major original (the argument turns on the compass BB–c"') has "nothing in the sources to substantiate this" [filled]. Spitta held that the cantus should reach d"' at bb.30–1 [filled] (p. 217).

## Book I no. 20, A minor, BWV 865 (pp. 218–220)

### Prelude
- Type/genre: has its origin in a keyboard-technical évolution, here mordents using thumb and fifth finger. Light and playful, the opposite of the fugue, whose anapaest rhythm it inverts (p. 218).

## Book I no. 24, B minor, BWV 869 (pp. 228–234)

### Prelude
- Type/genre & models: linked to the cantata BWV 150 'Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich' (also B minor), with its falling chromatic tetrachord (lamento bass) for yearning, in a similar texture (p. 228). n.112: BWV 150 was once doubted (Glöckner 1988) (p. 365). Riemann (1890 I p.156) contrasts it with the same material in the second section of the E flat prelude (p. 228). The Corelli trio-sonata prelude type: Ex.7.31, Corelli Preludio grave Op.4 No.2 bb.7–17 (figured) (p. 230). n.114: Corelli reception in Germany (pp. 365–366).
- Form, sections: after the double bar the idea appears in diminution and in close imitation; "the alto from b.32½ has an extension of the idea", which is syncopated from b.42 (Riemann 1890 I p.155) [filled] (p. 229). Corelli's chromatic inflexion before the final cadence is exaggerated so that the last bars seem to prepare the fugue's total chromaticism (p. 229).
- Versions, sources: there is "just a possibility that this prelude" (with the fugue) "may have originated in G minor": Dürr notes missing G sharps and the fugue's avoidance of c#"' (b.36, b.63), but finds this insufficient [filled] (pp. 228–229). n.113 attaches at the end of this G minor-origin paragraph [filled]: Dürr (KB V/6.1 pp.190, 382–3) on bending of motifs and the bass of the fugue's earlier version (b.23) not exceeding E. L. replies that the B minor Invention and Sinfonia descend to C and that Bach exploits the four-octave compass (p. 365). P 401 (Anon.5, 1722–3, probably copied from a draft for P 415) also includes the first 14 bars of BWV 923, which might suggest indecision about which prelude to use; L. thinks this unlikely [filled]. Other speculations, without evidence: that the original was only bb.1–16 (the only binary prelude), and that the ending (b.42½–46½) was added later [filled] (p. 229). n.115: Hermelink 1976 p.66; on Anon.5, Dürr 1986 p.163.

### Fugue
- Voices: four ("four-voice fugues") (p. 233).
- Subject: includes all 12 semitones. L.: "can only be meant to embody the word 'wohltemperirt'". It is a "musical labyrinth", the antithesis of the C major fugue's hexachord (p. 231). Background models: Langloz Fuga 39 in B minor (falling triad, leap to minor 6th, chromatic descent; Ex.7.32a) and Albinoni's subject of BWV 951 (offbeat start on the 5th degree, covering a 10th; Ex.7.32b) (pp. 231–232). It uses passus/saltus duriusculi instead of the chromatic tetrachord (also in BWV 154 opening aria) (p. 232). Kirnberger (1782 p.2) cites it as an expression of despair (p. 232). It is "Bach's most elaborate use of the device of chiasmus": each group of four quavers of the 'sigh' motif forms the cross shape, and the whole is framed by the tonic and dominant triads [filled] (pp. 232–233). n.117: Mellers' symbolism; Blankenburg on the downward minor triad (pp. 366).
- Answer: the subject is modulating, swinging tonic → dominant (p. 233). The answer shifts to E minor on its 5th note (cf. b.4 and tenor b.30) [filled] (pp. 232–233).
- Countersubject(s): "of the utmost clarity", stepping down five notes of a scale in crotchets (p. 233).
- Exposition: the fourth entry (bb.13–15) should start on the dominant and return to the tonic. Bach avoids the tonic cadence (b.16) and puts an episode before a fifth (tonic) entry, with a false entry at b.19 and the tonic entry at b.21 (p. 233).
- Later entries: b.21 tonic (tonic→dominant). b.30 subdominant→tonic. Half-entries at bb.34 and 35 (dominant→tonic). b.38 complete tonic entry at the exact midpoint. Half-entries in rising 4ths: B minor (b.41), E minor (b.42), A major (b.43). A complete entry with its answer in D major (bb.44–50). b.53 dominant. b.57 subdominant. b.60 return to the tonic. From b.70 a pair of entries in subdominant and tonic (pp. 233–234).
- Episodes: serenely diatonic episode material, traceable to trio sonata figurations. The same relaxing use appears in Frescobaldi's Capriccio cromatico con ligature al contrario (1626) and the fugue of Beethoven Op.106 (p. 232). The first episode is repeated at bb.65ff (p. 233). The falling triad in semiquavers at b.65 is used economically, compared with its permeation of BWV 951 (p. 232).
- Sections: much of the central part is in the tonic. The midpoint is b.38. The ending mirrors the opening: the repeated first episode, then the subdominant–tonic pair balancing the exposition's swing to the dominant (pp. 233–234).
- Genre/models: Albinoni/BWV 951, which L. calls "diffuse in comparison" and more standard in its chromatic effects (than both BWV 951 and BWV 951a) (p. 232).
- Versions, sources: the direction Largo goes back at least to the stage just before P 415 (Dürr 1984 p.55) (p. 234).
- Performance: per Walther (1732), Largo is "somewhat quicker" than Adagio (n.116 gives the German quotation). L. advises against taking the piece too slowly. Tempo similar to the first Kyrie of the B minor Mass (also Largo and in C [filled]) (p. 234).
- Other: n.118: Kirnberger 1773 p.53 (Dok.III pp.259–60) and Lester 1992 pp.248–9 relate to Kirnberger's (or J.A.P. Schulz's, 1773) figured-bass harmonic analysis of this fugue, "one of the earliest examples of such analysis" [filled] (pp. 233, 366).
