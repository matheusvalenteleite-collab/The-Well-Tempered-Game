## Book II no. 13, F sharp major, BWV 882 (pp. 290–294)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Ledbetter says the resemblance of the opening to the E flat organ prelude BWV 552/1 (Clavier-Übung III), with its implied overdotting, "is misleading" (pp. 290–291). The Altnickol appoggiatura makes the character gentler than the straight tirata of BWV 552/1 (p. 291). The dotted rhythms feel "light, tripping, and dance-like", like the accompanying parts of the Hornpipe in Handel's Op.6 No.7 (p. 291). The concertante semiquaver patterns give rhythmic flexibility and allow motivic development, so they are not "mere harmonic wash" (p. 291). This is another large-scale expansion of the several-section aria-ritornello format, as in the F major, F sharp minor and A flat major preludes (p. 291). It opens with two contrasting ideas whose underlying relationship it then explores (p. 291). The trills have been likened to the fife trills in "Jauchzet, frohlocket" from the Christmas Oratorio (Kluge-Kahn 1985 p.63) (p. 292). The prelude has none of the thorny connotations of F sharp major in François Couperin's *L'épineuse* (26th Ordre, 1730) (p. 292). Like the C sharp major and G sharp minor preludes, its character is that of the natural-key equivalent rather than a special advanced-key character, unlike Book I (p. 292).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** bb.1–3 introduce decorated dotted right-hand figures (bb.1–2) and a plain dotted rhythm in the bass. Bar 3 shows that the continuous semiquavers of b.4 grow out of the dotted rhythm (p. 291). The opening period has 8 bars, divided irregularly 3+5 (p. 291). A modulating phrase combining all three figures runs bb.8–14, followed by a closing phrase and cadence in the dominant (bb.15–17) (p. 291). A large expansion of the section shape runs from the dominant (b.17) to the relative minor (b.45) (p. 291). The reprise begins at b.57 and switches the order of events: the third element (bb.4–6) is reserved for a coda from b.68 (p. 291). The modulating phase begins on the subdominant (b.61), so that it modulates back to the tonic (b.68), the scheme also used in the D major prelude (p. 291). A flourish in the last three bars makes a large-scale hemiola (three minim beats in bb.73–4), like the end of the A flat prelude (p. 292) [displaced, restored].
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Bach probably added the appoggiatura to the right hand's second beat in b.1 at an early stage to Altnickol's 1744 copy (Tomita 1995 p.504) (p. 291). Bach notated the rhythms carefully (b.1 beats 2–3, etc.; NBA V/6.2 p.X) (p. 291). Ledbetter attributes the change from one dotted-rhythm notation (here) to another (in the A flat prelude) only to Bach's later notational trend (n.54, citing Dürr 1998 p.370) (p. 291 / n.54). In b.1, beats 2–3, Bach sharpened the dotted rhythm by adding a rest (p. 291) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs; the exact rhythm symbols are lost].
- **Performance:** The anticipations after the cadential trills in bb.44 and 67 are not exactly notated but are meant as semiquavers. The London autograph aligns them with the bass semiquavers (p. 291). Semiquavers after dotted notes are aligned with the fourth semiquaver of a group. In b.1 the first left-hand semiquaver is aligned with the penultimate right-hand demisemiquaver (p. 291). The elaborate demisemiquavers at b.57 imply measured demisemiquavers throughout, so no overdotting is needed (p. 291).
- **Other analysts cited:** Cecil Gray (1938 p.113) on the richness and brilliance from two parts; Ledbetter agrees ("rightly noted") (p. 291). Kirnberger is the probable subject of n.53 (Kirnberger 1776 pp.119–20, Dok.III pp.223–4, which identifies Couperin as "le grand"), likely tied to the Couperin reference (p. 292 / n.53; attribution inferred).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** three. The plan mentions treble, middle part and bass (pp. 293–294).
- **Subject:** It begins with a cadential trill on the 7th degree, which Ledbetter calls novel (p. 292). This echoes the cadential trill at the end of the prelude, like Haydn and Mozart minuets that open sections with a closing gesture (p. 292). A flattened 7th degree in b.2 of the subject is a galant feature beloved of Telemann (possibly from Mixolydian-tinged Polish music). It is also found in C.P.E. Bach's second "Prussian" sonata (1742) and in W.F. Bach's D major fugue (F.31) (p. 293). There is precedent for a trill-opened subject in the verset tradition, e.g. Kerll's fifth-mode Magnificat (1686; Ex.8.18, Versus Quia respexit) (p. 292).
- **Answer:** The 7th-degree start allows a "very neat answer" (noted by Marpurg 1753 p.54, Dok.III pp.30–1). It turns the tonic f[#] of the subject "into a dominant 7th in C sharp", which launches the countersubject's appoggiaturas (p. 292) [filled].
- **Countersubject(s):** There is only one retained countersubject (p. 294). Its galant appoggiaturas provide off-beat phrase endings (Jenne 1974c p.4) (p. 292). It has lamento falling chromatic steps. Spitta compared the countersubject and its derived episodes to the duet "Nun verschwinden alle Plagen" (BWV 32, 1726), which Ledbetter calls "particularly apt" (p. 293). The episode 1 triple-counterpoint block includes, in the middle part, the rising 6–7–8 notes from the end of the countersubject (p. 294).
- **Exposition:** bb.1–12 (p. 294). "the middle part from b.8 will not work as a bass", so there is only one retained countersubject (p. 294) [filled].
- **Later entries:** a further tonic entry in the treble, bb.20–24 (p. 294). In b.20 the cantus entry turns the trill into a turn beginning on the main note, because it continues a rising scale (pp. 292–293). Middle exposition: dominant b.32, tonic b.36, relative minor b.40 (p. 294). Subdominant entry bb.52–56 (p. 294). Final exposition from b.64: entries at b.64 and b.70 are each extended to six bars with appoggiatura-episode material. The final tonic entry at b.76 is extended to a full eight-bar gavotte phrase (p. 294). In b.70 the trill is in the bass, but the subject begins with the turn version in the middle part. In b.72 the turn version is in the bass, "not on a semitone and introduces nothing" (p. 293).
- **Episodes:** Episode 1, bb.12–20, is based on a triple-counterpoint block (end of b.12 to the beginning of b.14). Its bass figure is used in the pedals of the C major organ prelude BWV 545/1 and in the C major prelude of Book II (p. 294). Episode 2, bb.24–32, is based on the countersubject's appoggiaturas and worked in trio-sonata style (p. 294). Episode 1 returns at bb.44–52 and episode 2 at bb.56–64 (p. 294).
- **Sections, cadences:** Ledbetter calls the fugue's "seamlessness" remarkable, with a complete lack of intermediate structural cadences in its 84 bars (p. 292) [displaced, restored].
- **Form reading:** The form is closer to the Vivaldi concerto model than to binary dance/sonata (p. 293). The further tonic entry after episode 1 reflects Vivaldi's Op.3 practice of repeating the ritornello in the tonic after the first episode (cf. the G major fugue of Book I) (p. 294). The middle exposition covers the tonal ground of a binary second half (dominant, tonic, relative minor) but acts more like a central ritornello. After it, the earlier sequence of events is repeated (p. 294). Gavotte phrase units of four and two bars are reflected in the 4-, 8- and 12-bar periods of the plan (p. 292).
- **Genre/models:** a galant-style fugue in gavotte rhythm: two minims in the bar, beginning on the second minim, with minim harmonic rhythm (Jenne 1974c p.4). Little of the binary dance structure remains (p. 292). For a trivial idea serving as a fughetta subject, there is the verset tradition, e.g. Kerll (p. 292). (The stray fragment "Italian Concerto has" on p. 294 belongs to the F sharp minor prelude and is restored there [displaced, restored].)
- **Performance:** The trill should be played like a cadential trill, beginning with the upper auxiliary (p. 292). The trill and turn versions should not be made uniform (p. 293).

## Book II no. 14, F sharp minor, BWV 883 (pp. 294–297)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** It has been compared with several Bach slow movements. The comparison with the middle movement of the Italian Concerto "has the merit of suggesting the tempo as Andante", rather than the Adagio of the organ toccata BWV 564 (p. 294) [filled; the fragment "Italian Concerto has", filed under the F sharp major fugue, belongs here]. The Benedictus of the B minor Mass is more useful because it mixes triplets with other values and has a soloistic (probably flute) line (p. 294). The closest comparison is the Allemande of the D major Partita BWV 828 (1728), a synthesis of the keyboard allemande and the galant binary sonata, with an elaborately decorated right hand standing away in pitch from "two left-hand accompanying parts", the French allemande layout, combined with the galant ritornello shape (p. 294) [filled].
- **Form, sections, cadences:** cadences in the dominant (b.12), the relative major (b.21) and the tonic (b.30); the climax comes in the third section, with a fermata (p. 294) [filled].
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Altnickol has triplets in the first beats of b.7 and b.8. The London autograph replaces them with demisemiquavers. The London autograph seems to be Bach's final thoughts, with more precise rhythmic notation (AB II p.180) (p. 295).
- **Performance:** The dots are probably a warning not to play the triplets too glibly, as in a gigue (p. 295). The triplets should be articulated separately, judging by the staccato dots in "Vater unser im Himmelreich" BWV 682 from Clavier-Übung III (p. 295) [filled]. N.55: such dots are a feature of flute technique in the Adagio, tongued separately according to Corrette's flute method (c.1740) (n.55; attribution inferred). Ledbetter thinks Bach probably meant exactly what he wrote (triplets versus demisemiquavers kept distinct), because "the essence of this style… is variety rather than assimilation" (p. 295).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** three (all three subjects in "all three voices", bb.20–3) (p. 297).
- **Subject:** There are three subjects. This is the most systematically laid out of the three-subject fugues: each subject gets its own exposition before being combined with the preceding ones (p. 295). Subject 1 is built on the traditional outline 5654321 of the scale, as in the fugue subject of Böhm's G minor Prelude, Fugue and Postlude (Andreas Bach Book) (p. 296). It is a subject "with gaps" that can be filled by figures from the subject itself (p. 296). A galant resolution of the second suspension in b.2 (by an upward leap to another harmony note; Quantz 1752 ch. xiii para 28, Ex.7.18) enriches it (pp. 296–297). Subject 2 starts from the second half of b.20. When it first appears in all three voices (bb.20–3, also bb.30–1) it seems to include the inganno of a flattened leading note (Mann 1958 p.91). The precise extent of subject 2 is undefined (p. 297).
- **Countersubject(s):** The countersubject (bb.4–6) echoes the steps and leaps of the subject and fills its gaps (p. 296). This relates to the obligo of contrapunto (punto) fugato, where a melodic figure is repeated at various pitches (Butler 1983 pp.298–9; Mattheson 1739 III ch.22 para 25, based on Berardi's *Documenti armonici* 1687 pp.20–1; Ex.8.19) (p. 296). Ledbetter notes that Bach's demonstration is less straightforward, because Berardi's and Mattheson's figures run against a long-note cantus firmus. What Bach adopts is the derivation of the figure from the subject (p. 296).
- **Exposition:** first exposition bb.1–20 (p. 297). The subject's shapes are developed through imitations in b.7 and a style brisé version in b.8 (p. 297). The falling triad of the opening is imitated recto (bb.9–10), inverso (bb.10–12) and both (bb.13–15), then turned into a continuo bass pattern (bb.16–20) (p. 297).
- **Later entries:** The second exposition (subject 2) begins after a cadence in the relative major at b.20 (p. 297). The third exposition (subject 3) begins over a cadence in the dominant at b.36 (p. 297). A subdominant entry of the original subject and countersubject comes at bb.51–4 (p. 297). The final exposition has three permutations of all three subjects: tonic b.55, dominant b.60, tonic b.66 (p. 297). The normal rising leading note of subject 2 is reserved for the combination of all three subjects (bb.55–7 etc.) (p. 297).
- **Episodes:** episodes in trio-sonata style between the final entries, with the upper parts giving many variants of the first three quavers of the subject (p. 297).
- **Sections, cadences:** The main key centres coincide with the main contrapuntal events: relative major b.20, dominant b.36, subdominant entry bb.51–4, tonic b.55 (p. 297).
- **Genre/models:** a learned fugue that seems to demonstrate a traditional genus of counterpoint (oblighi), as Bach did around 1740 in The Art of Fugue and in some Book II fugues (see Chapter Four section 8). Here the genus is contrapunto fugato (Mattheson/Berardi) (pp. 295–296) [filled; corrects "Art of Fugue 8": the 8 is the Chapter Four section number].
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Altnickol's copy adds a trill on the dotted quaver of subject 2. Ledbetter thinks it is "if not by Bach then almost certainly at his instigation" (Dürr 1998 p.351). It is added to all dotted quavers in bb.20–23, with the implication that it should be added later wherever playable (p. 297). Altnickol has bass G (rather than B) on the first beat of b.23. Jones takes B as authentic (AB II p.180) and Tomita takes G as a probable correction (1995 p.565). Ledbetter prefers G: it avoids a definite perfect cadence in the course of a subject entry near the start of an exposition, and it gives a logical bass line from the end of b.22 (p. 297) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs; the printed outline "F | G A | a | a b | c'" has lost its accidentals, presumably E#|F# G#|a|a b|c#' (inferred)].
- **Performance:** add the subject-2 trill (see above) (p. 297).

## Book II no. 15, G major, BWV 884 (pp. 297–300)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** a student piece, grouped with the D minor prelude of Book II and the C sharp major prelude of Book I. These pieces make a brilliant effect without great technical demands (p. 298). It contrasts with the otherwise similar Allemande of the G major French Suite, which also has a sonata layout. Both share a minor-key tinge at section ends, a Corellian feature (p. 298).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** 48 bars, with a section end at b.16 and the main subdominant cadence at b.32 (p. 298). Irregular phrase lengths (three-bar phrases at section openings) and a general lack of internal cadences give fluidity (p. 298).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The earliest version is probably BWV 902/1a, "plainly a teaching piece" based on a finger-exercise pattern, like the little preludes in W.F. Bach's Clavier-Büchlein (p. 297). By 1729 Bach had replaced it with the prelude BWV 902/1, which was in turn dropped in favour of the final prelude (pp. 297–298) [filled]. N.56 (NBA V/6.2 pp.338–9, PF pp.32–3; sources in Dürr 1998 pp.355–7) and n.57 (AB II p.181; Tomita 1999 pp.60–3) probably belong here or to the fugue (attribution inferred).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** three obbligato voices in the final version (p. 298).
- **Exposition:** In origin, the entries run from the treble down, so the subject is always in the bass (p. 298).
- **Later entries:** a middle-part entry at bb.67–9, for which Marpurg's stemming shows the division between the hands. Kroll and Jones give it, NBA does not (p. 299).
- **Episodes:** Episodes are made from continuo progressions, with the action in the bass and free-voiced continuo chords in the right hand. This links the fugue with early pieces such as the A minor fugue BWV 947 and the early toccatas (p. 298). The apparent counterpoint at bb.17–19 simply figures out the right-hand chords of the early version (cf. the E minor fugue) (p. 299).
- **Sections, cadences, pedals:** Bach recast bb.53 to the end to include a dominant pedal and cadenza-like flourishes, in the spirit of the "concert" endings of some Book I preludes (p. 298).
- **Genre/models:** In origin it is the closest piece in the 48 to a partimento fugue (Schulenberg 1992 pp.219–20) (p. 298). In the final version it belongs to a series of light, lively sonata fugues (Spielfugen) (p. 299).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The early version is BWV 902/2 (NBA V/6.2 pp.332–3, PF pp.26–7), a very freely conceived piece converted into a three-voice fugue (p. 298). Dürr thinks it may go back to Arnstadt c.1707 or earlier (1998 p.359) (p. 298). Many fingered copies survive in the Kirnberger group, showing it was popular as a student piece (p. 299).
- **Performance:** The semiquavers must be articulated by slurring and separation to bring out the rhythmic shape (p. 299). Ledbetter rejects H.C. Wolff's idea of accenting the highest and lowest notes: "exactly what is not required" (p. 299; n.58, Wolff 1940/8 p.88, which Ledbetter calls "wrong-headed"). The first bar is essentially an upbeat and should be separate, with the following downbeats slurred. The highest note shifts from the third beat (b.1) to the first beat (b.3) (p. 299). Similar articulation is needed in the Courante of the G major Partita BWV 829 [filled] and in No.19 of the Goldberg Variations (p. 299) [displaced, restored]. Kirnberger's fingerings for bb.62–4 divide the scales between the hands (Ex.8.20), following the 17th-century roulade principle (Marpurg 1765 Tab.VII/32) (p. 299; n.59 on identifying the Am.B.57(2) fingerings, KB V/6.2 p.67).

## Book II no. 16, G minor, BWV 885 (pp. 299–303)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Bach has taken a "character texture" from a particular type of Italianate aria. Ledbetter says it is not French style, and the comparison with Contrapunctus 6 "in stile francese" is misleading (p. 301). Tempo words in the 48 define genre and are used only where several possibilities exist (p. 299). Without a marking, a piece in this time with dotted semiquavers would "normally be classed as an Adagio". Largo is somewhat faster (Walther, Telemann), a processional pace (cf. BWV 198) (p. 299) [filled].
- **Form, sections, cadences:** Structural cadences: C minor (b.5) and G minor (b.11) (p. 300) [filled]. A pedal becomes a 4/2 chord in the middle of b.7 and is then released; this gives the shape of the second half from b.11. In the middle of b.11 an A flat sounds over G. 4/2 chords "at bb.13½ and 14½" drive the line down for a moment until the peak at b.15 (p. 300) [filled]. A 4/2 chord at the start of b.16 is released into falling bass semiquavers, and the fall continues in the first half of b.18 (p. 300). Bach thus makes an improvisation formula the basis of a logical and dramatic structure (p. 300).
- **Harmony/figured-bass abstract:** The falling bass at b.7½ leads to a note moving "the wrong way" (upwards), followed in b.8 by another moving the wrong way (downwards). The effect is repeated at the end of b.18. This is a figure for death, found from Monteverdi to BWV 106 ("sterben") and the B minor Mass Credo ("mortuorum") (p. 301) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs; presumably b natural upwards, then b flat downwards in b.8].
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** In the London autograph Bach replaced Altnickol's rhythm in the second beat of b.1 and equivalent places with a clearer notation (p. 301) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs]. The alignment of notes in the London autograph gives no firm conclusion (Dürr KB V/6.2 p.245 assesses each instance) (p. 301).
- **Performance:** On whether the semiquavers after dotted quavers should be synchronised with the demisemiquavers after dotted semiquavers, Ledbetter says that differentiating them would be pedantic. Doing so would give the dotted-quaver version a thematic weight it is not meant to bear, so they should be assimilated (p. 301).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** It opens emphatically with a single note, like an exclamation or command, as in the Book I A major fugue. Here the opening comes on a weak beat, which gives it rhythmic subtlety (p. 301). Pirro (1907 pp.42–3) reads the repeated notes as affirmation, certain knowledge, decision, fidelity and submission. His examples are BWV 141 (now known to be Telemann) and BWV 679. Pirro later cites a similar subject by Fischer where the rests set "Seufzer" (p.116) (p. 301). The short-long-long rhythm strongly suggests Mattheson's bacchius metre, but his expressive associations for it "are unlikely for this fugue" (Chapter Four section 5, Ex.4.3) (pp. 301–302) [filled; corrects the digest, which attached this phrase to stile antico]. The subject, like that of the F sharp minor fugue, outlines a scale descending from the 6th degree, which can be harmonised as a circle of 5ths with a 7th in every other chord (p. 302).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** a "very learned presentation of double counterpoint at the octave, 10th and 12th" (p. 302). Bach concentrates on the 6th, which Fux (1725) forbids in counterpoint at the 12th because it inverts as a 7th. The 6ths in b.6 etc. invert at the 12th as 7ths in b.29 etc., which turns a weak inversion into the tonally strongest one (p. 302). The 7th resolves by leap (second practice) (p. 302). Because 7th chords are built from 3rds, the material also works at the 10th (p. 302). Inverting at the 12th means moving the countersubject of bb.3–4 from a 3rd above the subject to a 3rd below (Ex.8.22b, "bars 10–12, with countersubject inverted at the 12th") (pp. 302–303). The inversions are given in Ex.8.21 (Appendix A) (p. 302).
- **Stretto/inversion/augmentation:** Marpurg calls it a "canonic double fugue", because the countersubject grows out of the subject and forms a continuous line with it (1753 pp.140–1, Dok.III pp.37–8). This canonic aspect comes increasingly to the fore (bb.45ff, 51ff) until it is "fully unveiled at b.59" (p. 303). Inversion at the 12th at b.67 is joined by the octave and 10th "at b.67" (p. 303) [check: the same bar is given twice; probably a typo or a lost bar number].
- **Sections, cadences:** After the b.67 combinations come "non-contrapuntal but rhetorical chords and pauses". This is the most striking Book II case of a climax intensified by rhetoric rather than counterpoint (cf. the D sharp minor fugue) (p. 303).
- **Genre/models:** Bach's use of the metre gives an impression of learnedness (p. 302). Fux says the 6th should be avoided in counterpoint at the 12th, since it inverts as a 7th; Bach features it, "turning what is a weakness in stile antico" into the tonally strongest inversion (p. 302) [displaced, restored]. The texture recreates Fuxian counterpoint in a modern idiom (p. 302). The tradition is shown in a partimento (Ex.8.22a, Anon., Langloz MS Fuga 12, bb.1–4) (pp. 302–303). Counterpoint at the 10th was a traditional way to get four parts from triple invertible counterpoint (e.g. Purcell) (p. 302). Making a four-part piece from a bicinium by doubling two canonic lines in 3rds has a German lineage through Werckmeister, Buxtehude, Theile and Bernhard, back to Zarlino and Schlick (p. 302).
- **Other:** Its expressive meaning is disputed ("opinions differ") (p. 301).

## Book II no. 17, A flat major, BWV 886 (pp. 303–307)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** the largest and possibly the latest prelude in Book II (p. 303). Its textures draw on many directions, as in the E flat, F and F sharp major preludes, using the aria-ritornello model in sonata guise (p. 304). The b.1 and b.2 patterns are based on keyboard hand-shapes, so an improvised origin can be assumed (p. 304). Bach was not after French-overture stiffness. He combined the fleetness of dotted rhythms with continuous semiquavers, which links this prelude with the F sharp major (p. 305).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The opening section is re-run from the dominant (b.17), the relative minor (b.34) and the subdominant (b.50). There is no tonic reprise, but a tonic coda begins in b.64 after a strong marker cadence (p. 304). The opening period is irregular, of seven bars (p. 304). The main materials are the chords and bass pattern of b.1 and the semiquavers of b.2 (p. 304). C.P.E. Bach-style staccato arpeggios come in bb.5–6 (p. 304). The modulating phase begins at b.7 with falling 3rds in both hands, answered by rising 3rds in bb.11–14 (p. 304). It climaxes on a last-inversion dominant 7th (4/2) at b.15, released into a closing idea and cadence at b.16 (p. 304). The 4/2 returns at b.48 and, intensified, in the final chords at b.75 (p. 304). The closing idea marks section ends at bb.33, 49 and 62 (p. 304). Section 2 (from b.17) has rising and falling 3rds in contrary motion (bb.23–32) and a chordal dotted rhythm from b.24 (p. 304). Section 3 (from b.34) uses the similar-motion 3rds again, adapted to move from F minor to D flat major, with peak density in bb.38–9 (p. 304). Section 4 has contrary-motion 3rds at bb.53–5 and through most of the coda (p. 304). A strong Neapolitan harmony in b.74 perhaps matches the end of the fugue (p. 304). There is a large-scale hemiola in bb.75–6 (three minim beats), as in the F sharp major prelude (p. 305).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The London autograph has the earliest version, entered in a late layer (c.1741) [displaced, restored]. Its corrections suggest composing while writing, or writing after improvisation (Tomita 1990 p.269) (p. 303). A lost fair copy introduced significant alterations, reflected in Altnickol's copy (p. 303). Dürr says the changed demisemiquaver notation reflects Bach's later trend to exact notation and has no necessary performance implication (1998 p.370) (p. 304; n.54). The right-hand arpeggio in b.39 has no staccato dot in any source, which Ledbetter finds "perhaps curious" given the correspondence with bb.5–6 (p. 304).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** four (cantus, alto, bass named; the fughetta is "effectively in three parts until the final entry at bb.22–4") (p. 306).
- **Subject:** It has a circle-of-5ths pattern and ends with a lamento (chromatic tetrachord) figure. The lamento subject ends with a leading-note-to-tonic step (alto bb.4–5) (pp. 305–306). Pirro (1907 p.430) compared the main subject to the Allemande of Couperin's G major Concert Royal (1722), which led to its misreading as a dance (Kunze 1969 p.88). Ledbetter rejects the dance reading. He finds the theme closer to the first Allegro of Corelli's Op.5 No.3 (perhaps Couperin's model), which points to a trio-sonata origin (p. 305).
- **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** a triple counterpoint including the lamento bass. In the F major fughetta it is "the most ordinary" of Bach's lamento triple counterpoints, with each strand a standard figure (p. 305).
- **Exposition:** The fughetta's entries run tonic–dominant–tonic–dominant (bb.1–10), beginning with the upper parts, trio-sonata style (p. 306).
- **Later entries:** A second series runs tonic–dominant–tonic–tonic (bb.13–24) (p. 306). The inganno of the flattened leading note comes in the second exposition (cantus b.20, alto b.21) (p. 306). The final fughetta entry is at bb.22–4 (p. 306). Continuation: F minor entry at b.24 (p. 307). E flat minor entry b.32, B flat minor entry b.35 (p. 307). D flat major (subdominant) entry in the bass after the last-inversion major 7th at the beginning of b.37 ("per arsin et thesin" in Marpurg's sense) (p. 307). Tonic and dominant entries at bb.41–3 (p. 307). The final entry is at bb.48–50 (p. 307).
- **Episodes:** a triple-invertible episode at bb.10–13 (p. 306). An episode at b.26 grows from the F minor entry (p. 307). The episode from b.27 revolves the permutations of bb.10–13, with semiquaver-figure entries in the cantus (b.27), alto (b.29) and bass (b.30) (p. 307).
- **Sections, cadences:** a strong F minor cadence at b.16 gives the only tonal contrast in the fughetta (p. 307). There is a cadence on the relative-minor dominant at b.27, which strengthens the relative-minor phase (p. 307). From b.44 the flattening tendency reaches a rhetorical climax on the Neapolitan, "B major" (p. 307) [check: the Neapolitan of A flat is B double-flat; the conversion or text has dropped the accidental]. Ledbetter compares this with the dramatic endings of the D sharp minor and G minor fugues (p. 307).
- **Form reading:** The continuation develops the lamento's minor-key, flattening effect "massively" (p. 307). Bach exploited the chromatic tetrachord richly in his last decade (P. Williams 1997 pp.100–3) (p. 307). The fugue contrasts early schematic planning with late organic exploration, like the two D sharp minor fugues (p. 306).
- **Genre/models:** Prototypes include the fugue of Kuhnau's Sonata terza (*Frische Clavier-Früchte*, 1696, F major) and G.M. The verset roots are Froberger's Capriccio I (1656), Kerll (1686, Magnificat Sexti Toni, Deposuit potentes) and Pachelbel (Magnificat Sexti Toni, c.1701–5). Bach worked from the F major version up to b.24 and then from a draft of the continuation (Tomita 1990 pp.271–3) (p. 306). The London autograph version dates precisely to 1742, "one of the last things" for Book II. There is another copy by Bach of about the same time (P 274), and Altnickol has further emendations (AB II p.185) (p. 306). In the cantus of the episode, the original crotchet b' (F major) becomes two quavers d" d" (A flat) on the last beat of b.10, with the equivalent in the alto on the second beat of b.12 (p. 306) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs; presumably b flat' becoming d flat" d flat"]. W.F. Bach's copy for Padre Martini (DD 70) adds an Accent steigend (appoggiatura) to the subject on the second beat of b.2 and equivalent places. ## Book II no. 18, G sharp minor, BWV 887 (pp. 307–310)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** It belongs to the second layer of the London autograph (c.1740–1), with "modern" keyboard figurations. It forms a group with the F sharp major, B major and D sharp minor preludes (p. 307). Several writers note its G minor character. Accidentals in the principal sources give some evidence of transposition from G minor (KB V/6.2 pp.250, 320) (p. 307). Like the D major and F minor preludes, it contrasts opening ideas and then integrates them (p. 308). It does not relate directly to the aria-ritornello model (p. 308). It is an "invention" prelude like the D sharp minor and E minor, combining the opening idea with ever-new counterthemes (p. 308). Ledbetter sees a stylistic gulf from C.P.E. Bach's Württemberg Sonata no. 2 (1744) (p. 309).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The right hand of b.1 is the contrapuntal inventio and the left hand of b.2 is the accompanying figure. Both outline the 5th G sharp–D sharp with a dip to F double-sharp (p. 308). bb.1–2 are repeated piano as an echo (p. 308). An initial four-bar shape leads to a phase modulating to the dominant (bb.5–8). Motivic working follows (bb.8–15), then a dominant reprise from b.16 to the double bar (p. 308). Double counterpoint from b.8 links the b.1 semiquavers with the b.2 appoggiaturas (right hand b.8 beat 2 to b.9 beat 1). The bass steps down a chromatic octave from the d at the beginning of b.8 to the D half way through b.11 (p. 308) [GAP: not recoverable, music glyphs; presumably d sharp to D sharp]. The episode at bb.11–15 combines the upbeat figure with the bass in violin-sonata manner (p. 308). New counterthemes come at b.16 and form the main argument after the double bar (p. 308). The reprise at b.31 starts in the Neapolitan key. The bb.8–15 order is then reversed, with the violin-sonata manner first (bb.34–5), and the inverted double counterpoint leads down the chromatic scale to the tonic reprise at b.41 (p. 308). bb.47–9 invert the galant right-hand-plus-accompaniment of bb.5–7, with close dissonances in the left hand (pp. 308–309).
- **Performance:** The appoggiaturas are quavers, as the first halves of b.44 and b.45 make clear. Bach writes Accent hooks, not small notes (cf. W.F. Bach's Clavier-Büchlein) (p. 309). The piano and forte marks in b.3 and b.5 probably imply a two-manual harpsichord (cf. Clavier-Übung II and IV; Marshall 1985 p.267). This is the only use of such marks in the 48, where the contrast is a structural necessity, but they do not rule out the clavichord (C.P.E. Bach 1753 ch. iii para 29) (p. 309). Manual changes in the D major and F minor preludes are possible but illogical (p. 309).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** cantus, alto and bass are named (p. 310).
- **Subject:** two subjects in two-bar galant phraseology, with a clear simple outline (p. 310). Rhythmic profile is understated (p. 310). ## Book II no. 20, A minor, BWV 889 (pp. 314–316)

### Prelude
- **Harmony/figured-bass abstract:** It is based on two continuo formulas (p. 314). The first is a lamento bass with 7–6 suspensions (Ex.8.24a), also expressible as a circle-of-5ths progression (Ex.8.24b), as in the Book II A flat major fugue (pp. 314–315). The second is a chromatically decorated cadence formula with its inversion (Ex.8.24c) (p. 315). Bach fills whole-tone steps with semitones and decorates the 7th resolutions with a chromatic lower auxiliary, a galant trait (cf. the F minor three-part Sinfonia and the Book I A major prelude) (p. 315). Ledbetter compares this with the six-part Ricercar of the Musical Offering (1747), which chromatically elaborates the rising-scale formula of the Pièce d'orgue BWV 572 (p. 315).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** Tomita suggests that the apparent fair copy in the London autograph may be a "mature record" of improvisation, preceded at most by a brief draft (1990 p.277; 1993 p.119) (p. 315).
- **Other analysts cited:** Kirnberger (or Schulz) offered a fundamental-bass analysis of this prelude as an easier example than "the B minor fugue of Book I" (p. 314) [filled; resolves the digest's doubt: the analysis is of this prelude]. Ledbetter's verdict is that it makes the harmony "look more complex than it actually is" and ignores the physical feel at the keyboard (p. 314).

### Fugue
- **Subject:** A cliché head, which Bach parodies (cf. the diminished-7th cliché in the finale of Haydn's F minor quartet, Op.20) [filled], is extended by a continuation built on a falling 3rd and 7th. This gives a subject of all leaps (di salto), against the chromatic steps (alla diritta) of the prelude (P. Williams 1997 pp.93–4) (p. 315; n.73 "Avoidance of steps", attribution inferred). The subject proper enters in D minor at b.21 (p. 316).

- **Performance:** lightness of treatment (p. 315).

## Book II no. 21, B flat major, BWV 890 (pp. 316–322)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Hand-crossing [filled] is linked to the Gigue of the B flat Partita (1726) and the C minor Fantasia BWV 906 (autographs c.1726–31 and c.1735–40). In Book II, Bach treats such techniques as part of a musical argument and understates them (p. 316). The opening gambit of descending scales over a pedal is a common organ-prelude improvisation formula, cf. Fischer, Ariadne Musica Praeludium 13 in G major (Ex.8.25) (p. 317). Fischer's prelude also has the feature of presenting the opening idea immediately in inversion (p. 318). The prelude is a galant binary sonata movement but on the invention principle, with virtually no contrast (p. 317). It is Bach's "most complex exploration" of reshuffling in the second half combined with a tonic reprise. It is compared with the D major prelude (1 2 3 :|: 1 2 3; 1 2 3 :|) and with BWV 906 (1 2 3 4 :|: 1 4 3 X; 1 2 :|) (p. 318).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** There are seven motivic areas before the double bar: 1ab bb.1–8 (1a bb.1–2, 1b, its inversion, bb.3–4); 2 bb.9–12; 3 bb.13–16; 4 bb.17–20; 5 bb.21–3; 6 bb.24–7; 7 bb.28–32 (p. 318). The second half starts with the closing idea (7), whose next appearance is reserved for the very end (p. 318). The reprise contains the three elements omitted from the middle series (1, 2, 4) plus new development (X). It separates 1a and 1b with ideas 2 and 4 inserted between them (p. 318; the digest had p. 319). Middle series: 7 bb.33–6; 3 bb.37–42; 6 bb.43–4; 5 bb.45–8. Reprise: 1a bb.49–52; 2 bb.53–6; 4 bb.57–64½; 1b bb.64½–69; X bb.70–82; 7 bb.83–7 (p. 318) [filled]. Analysts have argued about where the reprise begins or doubted that there is one. Ledbetter's verdict is that "the plan and solution are clear" (p. 319).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The time signature appears to have been 12/8, perhaps reflecting an earlier version, corrected to 12/16. The lighter signature implies a warning against too deliberate a tempo (p. 317) [filled; confirms the reading: Altnickol first wrote 12/8 and corrected it to 12/16].

## Book II no. 22, B flat minor, BWV 891 (pp. 322–325)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** Ledbetter finds it hard to parallel. It looks like a three-part Sinfonia or a violin-sonata movement, and it evokes an instrumental trio on the keyboard, like the C sharp minor and A major preludes (p. 322).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The first treble entry (b.3) is non-thematic. The bass is thematic only twice (b.25, b.42) (p. 322). The subject has two elements. The first is in the middle part at bb.1–3½, with an outline similar to the B flat major fugue subject. The second has a rising shape with repeated notes, its ending indefinite, and is often likened to the opening of the Book I B flat minor prelude (p. 322) [filled; the second element is at bb.3½–5, and element 1 runs to b.3½, not b.3]. The initial statement is bb.1–5 (p. 322). A dominant cantus entry comes at b.8. From then on the figuration derives from the middle-part quaver figure of b.7 (p. 322). An episode (bb.16–24) leads to entries in the tonic (b.25) and relative major (b.31) (p. 322). Ternary reading: first section bb.1–41, closing with a cadence in A flat major (p. 323). Middle section: A flat entry in the bass (b.42) on the "wrong degree" (root, not 3rd). The second element migrates to the middle voice (b.44) and modulates to G flat major. ### Fugue
- **Voices:** not stated. At b.27 "the texture is reduced to a very aerated three parts", which implies a fuller texture elsewhere (p. 325).

## Book II no. 23, B major, BWV 892 (pp. 325–329)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** It may form a Book II group with the D sharp minor and F sharp major preludes, using similar material in different forms. The B major is in virtuoso concerto style (p. 325). Its nearest companion is the Praeambulum of the G major Partita BWV 829 (1730) (p. 325). It combines old and new scale techniques. The opening bar looks like the B major scale demonstration in C.P.E. Bach's Versuch (1753 ch.1 para 56), fingered over the thumb, unlike the Book I B major prelude (p. 325). From b.17 come old-style scales alternating between the hands (a roulade pattern; Pachelbel; P. Williams 1994) (p. 325).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** The scale (bb.1–2) is contrasted with a broken triad (from b.3, moving to the dominant by b.12), and "the broken triad merges back into the scale (from b.7½)" (p. 326) [filled]. There are two sections that alternate effects. The first has a trio-sonata texture with a scalic bass (from b.12), then roulades (from b.17). The second has a trio-sonata texture with a broken-triad bass (from b.23), then roulades (from b.29) (p. 326). A lead-back on the broken-triad figure (from b.33) leads to the reprise (b.37), which is freely recast and concentrates on the scale figure (p. 326).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** four (tenor, bass, alto named; "top three parts") (pp. 328–329).
- **Subject:** The fugue is alla breve; it was originally in a triple-time signature, whose glyph is lost (n.72) (p. 326) [filled]. The subject is a tonal circle of 3rds, nearly all leaps, which may "reflect the old obligo di non uscir di grado"; it has a cantus-firmus character (p. 326) [filled]. Written as a series of 3rds it outlines 7th chords (Ex.8.29a, bars 2–3) (p. 328). Its outline of a leading-note 7th is "strikingly galant", like the final aria of BWV 209 (p. 328) [filled]. - **Countersubject(s)/invertible counterpoint:** The first countersubject outlines a major 7th (b.6) and then a leading-note 7th (b.7) (p. 328). Butler sees its regular syncopation as Berardi's contrapunto alla zoppa (1983 p.297). Ledbetter says it could equally be contrapunto perfidiato (Berardi 1687 pp.18–20) (p. 326). The first section presents the subject and two countersubjects in triple counterpoint (p. 328). A group of four quavers is imitated between tenor and bass in bb.9–10, which provides material for the new subject (p. 328).
- **Exposition:** entries from the bass up, an old-style layout, as in "Sicut locutus est" from the Magnificat BWV 243 (p. 326).

There is a prominent bass entry at b.48 and a tenor entry at b.53, which is very hard to make clear on the harpsichord (p. 329). The third section opens with an entry at b.60, placed on the 3rd of E major, so that its cadence (bb.62–3) is in G sharp minor (p. 329). The final series of tonic and dominant entries begins with the bass tonic return at b.75 (p. 329). The entry at b.85 takes the subject from D sharp minor up to F sharp major (p. 329).

## Book II no. 24, B minor, BWV 893 (pp. 329–332)

### Prelude
- **Type/genre & models:** a variant of the several-section aria-ritornello type in sonata guise. In texture it is an invention type like the D sharp minor and E minor preludes, combining ever-new counterthemes with the main subject (pp. 330–331). It has unusually many galant performance indications: turns, staccatos and slurred "sighs" (p. 330). The opening treble soggetto looks like harmonic-wash figuration but is "the fundamental inventio of the piece" (p. 330).
- **Form, sections, cadences:** three sections, each running through the sonata phases (p. 331). Section 1 opens with a closed period of two four-bar phrases, the second inverting the first. A sequential passage (bb.9–12) and closing idea (bb.13–16) lead to the relative major, not the dominant, where a new galant countertheme comes at bb.21–4 (p. 331). Middle section: two statements of the main theme, with a new countertheme at bb.25–8, then an inverted reprise of bb.5–8. The sequential passage follows with the bass leading and a new treble counterpoint (bb.33–6), and a bridge leads to the subdominant reprise from b.41 (p. 331). The reprise compresses the eight-bar period into four bars (bb.41–4, with the exchange after two bars), followed by the sequential passage (bb.45–8) and closing idea (bb.49–52) (p. 331). A free extension follows, with a rhetorical pause before the tonic return, using the final countertheme of bb.21–4 (cf. bb.59–60) (p. 331).
- **Form reading:** an ambiguity between binary (the same theme closes each section) and ternary (successive aria-ritornello workings) (p. 331).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** The final version in the London autograph has a different time signature from an earlier version with note values half as long. The earlier version is reflected in Altnickol-group sources other than Altnickol's 1744 copy (NBA V/6.2 pp.298–300) (pp. 329–330). The earlier version was in common time (C) with the shorter note values; the final signature is lost, presumably alla breve (pp. 329–330) [filled in part; GAP: final signature not recoverable, music glyph]. The earlier, short-value version looks like the final C major prelude and the F sharp major and A flat major preludes (p. 330). Kroll and Bischoff surmised that the change was for notational convenience. Ledbetter rejects this and thinks it more likely a subtle adjustment of rhythmic projection, cf. Kirnberger on the F major fugue (Dok.III pp.233–4) (p. 330). "Allegro" was added in the London autograph (p. 330). The turn is written with an ornament sign at b.32 (London autograph) and b.53 (Altnickol sources); elsewhere it is written out (p. 330).
- **Performance:** In the final notation semiquavers and demisemiquavers are decorative and allow freer shaping. They "can on no account be played with the strict rhythm of fundamental note values" (p. 330).

### Fugue
- **Voices:** three (middle, bass, cantus) (p. 332).
- **Subject:** The octave leaps are the prominent feature and a source of wit. Such leaps are unusual in keyboard music but found in verset fugues and orchestral string music (p. 331). N.75 gives examples: Fischer's *Blumen-Strauss* (1732) and the Langloz partimenti (p. 331; n.75). The two-bar units match passepied dance steps (p. 331). Ellis (1980 p.424) juxtaposes this subject with that of The Art of Fugue [displaced, restored], a resemblance Ledbetter calls "no doubt entirely accidental" (p. 332).
- **Countersubject(s):** The first countersubject implies a stretto with the subject (bb.7–8), which would normally be a flaw (p. 332). Smooth-stepping figurations come at bb.9–10 (p. 332). A new countersubject with leaps enters at bb.29ff, and much of the rest of the fugue finds playable permutations of it with the subject (p. 332).
- **Stretto:** At bb.69ff a two-part play on the implied stretto leaves it unclear until b.73 which part has the subject (p. 332). From b.97 there is play in three parts (middle, bass, cantus) on the subject's opening figure (p. 332).
- **Episodes:** The b.16 migration of the countersubject semiquavers (see Versions) "is a small effect here", but it becomes the basis of a whole episode at bb.60ff (p. 332) [filled].
- **Sections, cadences:** There is a striking lack of structural cadences. The only one is the dominant cadence at bb.27–8, elided by falling in the middle of a subject entry (p. 331).
- **Genre/models:** passepied, though with freer use of dance phraseology than the Book I F major fugue (p. 331). The fugue relates to the galant French and flute associations of B minor (cf. Overture BWV 1067), where the Book I B minor fugue relates to its serious ones (p. 332). Couperin's 8th Ordre in B minor (1717) similarly ends with a small character piece, La Morinète, rather than the Passacaille (n.76).
- **Versions, sources, revisions:** In the London autograph version of b.16, the countersubject semiquavers move from the cantus to the middle part at the end of the bar, which emphasises the stretto in the countersubject (p. 332). N.77: Jones considers Altnickol's reading Bach's final thought (AB II), while Tomita argues the London autograph has the final reading (1993 p.142) (n.77; attribution to b.16 inferred).
- **Performance:** a slight accent ("touching down") at the start of each dance step, on even-numbered bars. Quavers should be light and semiquavers even lighter (Little 1975 pp.122–3) (pp. 331–332). Trills chase each other, giving joie de vivre as much as in the F sharp major and A minor fugues (p. 332) [displaced, restored].
