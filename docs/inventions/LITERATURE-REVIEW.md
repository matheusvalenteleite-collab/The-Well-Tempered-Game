# What is already known about the Inventions and Sinfonias (I0)

The INVENTIONS session's literature review, 12 October 2026. The references, with tier, question and
access, are in `docs/LITERATURE.md` (section INVENTIONS); this note says what they establish, where they
disagree, and what is left for a corpus study. A claim is attributed to a work only where its text, its
abstract, or a citing author's report of it was seen; the row in LITERATURE.md says which. Derr (1981), the
one systematic article on the two-part set, has **not** been seen: nothing below rests on it.

## 1. What the literature establishes

**The purpose, in Bach's words.** The 1723 fair copy (P 610) is a teaching book. Its title addresses
"Liebhaber" and "Lehrbegierige" and promises four things:
- to play cleanly in two parts, then in three;
- to obtain good *inventiones* and to work them out well ("wohl durchzuführen");
- a singing manner of playing;
- "einen starcken Vorschmack von der Composition".

Three of the four are about composition. Hall (2015) reads *durchführen* in two senses: to realise the
piece in playing, and to realise in composing what the *inventio* implies. Both senses are attested in the
period, and the title page gives no reason to choose between them.

**The genesis.** This is settled by the NBA reports and summarised in the Henle and Bärenreiter prefaces.
- The pieces were entered in the Clavier-Büchlein for W. F. Bach, probably c.1722/23. The two-part pieces
  are called "Praeambulum" and the three-part "Fantasia", in an order that runs up and then down the keys
  (C d e F G a h, then B A g f E Es D c).
- The 1723 fair copy renamed them Inventio and Sinfonia, put them in ascending key order (C c D d Es E e F f
  G g A a B h), and retouched the text. Invention 1 was recast in triplets, and ornaments were added
  later to several pieces: Henle names Inventions 10 and 11 and Sinfonia 5; Dadelsen adds Inventions 3,
  5 and 9.
- Kayser's copy (P 219, c.1724) shows a stage between the two.
- Dadelsen dates the pieces to 1722, the year of WTC I.

**Their place in Bach's teaching.** Three witnesses report it.
- Gerber's son (1790): Heinrich Nicolaus Gerber's first lesson was the Inventions, then suites, then the
  WTC.
- Dürr (1978) confirms that order from Gerber's surviving copies, the Inventions beginning late in 1724.
- Forkel (1802), at 80 years' distance, says the pieces were written during lessons and later reworked into
  "kleine Kunstwerke".

**The order of the first version.** Plath's report on the Clavier-Büchlein (1963, as cited by Hall), the
Bärenreiter preface and Dilaghi (2004, abstract) agree that the original order is graded. It runs from
simple imitation through inversion to canon and fugue, and shows the course better than the key order of
1723 does.

**Individual pieces.**
- Invention 8 is a strict study in double counterpoint at the octave. It uses only imperfect consonances
  and octaves outside the cadences, so that it inverts without fault (Hall).
- Sinfonia 3 uses all six permutations of triple counterpoint (Hall).
- Sinfonia 9 bends the treatment of dissonance and of the six-four so that each voice can carry the theme
  in the bass (Stoia 2015, abstract).
- The openings of the two-part Inventions follow the principles of concerto themes (Wagner 1979, abstract).
- Schulenberg (via Hall) distinguishes the two sets. The Inventions have "free forms"; the Sinfonias are
  built from roughly equal sections, some bipartite.

**What the WTC reader already says.** These points come from the project's digest of Ledbetter (2002),
cited by printed page; the book's ch. 6, "Bach as Teacher", is not yet digested.
- Ledbetter uses "the invention principle" for pieces in which all the material grows from one opening
  idea:
  - fugues (e.g. p. 154);
  - a set of WTC II preludes he calls "invention-type", which he ties to particular Inventions (pp. 281,
    308, 330-331).
- He grades a WTC II prelude as "at the level of the F major two-part Invention" (p. 262).
- He notes that the Sinfonias are strictly in three parts, so no freer three-part prelude can derive from
  them (p. 210).
- Against the rigid two-part fugues with which, by C. P. E. Bach's report, Bach's teaching of fugue began,
  "the Inventions seem more 'mature'" (p. 186).

## 2. Where scholars disagree

1. **What an *inventio* is.**
   - **Dreyfus (1996):** a segment of the whole texture, not a theme. It recurs while its harmonic function
     changes, and it is separated from its elaboration as rhetoric separates *inventio* from *dispositio*.
     BWV 772 is his central case.
   - **The textbook tradition** (Kennan, Benjamin) and **motivic analysts** (David, Weiss): a motive or
     subject, developed through episodes and middle entries.
   - **Väisälä (2009):** a surface figure whose enlargements organise the piece at several levels.
   - **Corduban (2005):** the pieces as orations.

   The disagreement is not idle: it fixes the unit that I1 would annotate. If Dreyfus is right, I1 must
   annotate a contrapuntal *complex* (motive with countermotive, at a given interval and distance), not a
   line.
2. **Whether *dispositio* is secondary.** Dreyfus subordinates it. Crist (1999) and Braunschweig (2001)
   object, and Hall (2015) argues that the very titles of 1723 mark the difference: Inventio for the
   pieces governed by the idea, Sinfonia for the pieces governed by the layout.
3. **Whether rhetoric is a good guide.** Ahlgrimm and Fiala (1954), U. Kirkendale (1980) and Corduban
   (2005) read Bach rhetorically. Williams (1983) and Vickers (1984) attack such readings as
   anachronistic or loose. Mattheson himself, the period's authority on *Erfindung*, says he sets little
   store by the *loci topici*.
4. **Whether analysis of the pieces shows Bach's teaching.** Remeš (2019) argues that it does not. Bach's
   teaching, he says, ran through thoroughbass and chorales over many basses, and modern analysis of
   finished works (the Inventions, the WTC, Dreyfus's book included) is no window into it. This is the
   strongest objection to the project's premise, and the study should meet it rather than ignore it. A
   corpus study can say what the pieces *contain*; it cannot by itself say what Bach *taught*. The claim
   that a habit counted in the Inventions was a lesson needs the documentary witnesses (title page, Gerber,
   C. P. E. Bach) behind it.
5. **What the fair copy was for.** Wolff proposes that the 1723 copy (with WTC I) served Bach's application
   for the Leipzig cantorate. The Henle preface reports this as a hypothesis; it does not contradict the
   pedagogical purpose.

## 3. The owner's hypothesis in the light of the review

"The Inventions are more conservative than the WTC and the Art of Fugue" runs together three claims that
the literature treats differently.

- **Pedagogical precedence: documented.** Gerber's course and the Clavier-Büchlein put the Inventions
  before the WTC, and Ledbetter grades WTC preludes against them. "Stepping stone" is the period's own
  order.
- **Chronological precedence: false for WTC I.** The Inventions and WTC I are both of 1722-23. Against the
  Art of Fugue and WTC II it holds, but only trivially.
- **Stylistic or technical conservatism: nowhere tested, and partly against the evidence.** No author found
  measures it.
  - The detailed studies point the other way for at least some pieces: Sinfonia 3 exhausts triple
    counterpoint; Sinfonia 9 bends dissonance rules; Invention 8 is stricter than most fugues.
  - Ledbetter calls the Inventions *more* mature than the two-part fugue scheme.
  - "Conservative" is therefore the claim the study must make precise before it can count. It could mean:
    - fewer kinds of imitation (the octave rather than the fifth, a fixed distance);
    - stricter dissonance;
    - a narrower key plan;
    - simpler forms;
    - less variety of invertible counterpoint.

  Each is countable in both repertories; they need not agree.

I propose to treat the hypothesis as **pedagogical precedence (granted)** plus a set of **technical
comparisons (to be measured)**. "Conservative" stays a summary that the counts may or may not earn.

## 4. What a corpus study can add

The searches found no published, machine-readable annotation of these pieces beyond which note belongs to
which voice:
- no list of motive or subject occurrences;
- no imitation entries with interval and distance;
- no sequences, invertible-counterpoint passages, cadences or harmony;
- none of them in the usual corpora (music21, When in Rome, DCML, JKU).

The computational work that exists is narrower:
- pattern discovery on the two-part set (Meredith 2006; Forth and Wiggins 2009; Lartillot on BWV 775),
  judged against published analyses rather than scored against an annotation;
- one corpus statistic (Huron 1993, onset asynchrony in the 15).

The Sinfonias are almost untouched. Concretely, the gaps are:

1. **Counts across all 30** of the imitation interval and distance, canon against free imitation against
   fugal answer, and the intervals of invertible counterpoint. The literature has case studies (Inventions
   1 and 8; Sinfonias 3 and 9), not a census.
2. **Tests of standing claims.** Each can be checked piece by piece:
   - the graded order of the Clavier-Büchlein (Plath, Dadelsen, Dilaghi);
   - the concerto openings (Wagner);
   - the free forms against the sectional ones (Schulenberg);
   - the enlargement of figures (Väisälä).
3. **The comparison with the fugues** that the owner's hypothesis needs, under the definitions in section
   3. No study compares the two repertories musically; the voice-separation papers only pool them as test
   data.
4. **A gold standard**, read by hand (I2), which would be the first such annotation of these pieces. It
   would also be the ANALYSIS CORE's second repertory for measuring the cadence finder.

## 5. What this changes in the plan sketched for I1-I6

- **I1 has to choose its unit before it counts.** It should take Dreyfus's (the motive with its
  countermotive, as a combination) or say why not, and it should record the imitation interval and
  distance, the invertible counterpoint, and the form type, so that Wagner's, Schulenberg's and the
  graded order's claims can be tested from the same readings.
- **I4 states which sense of "conservative" each count tests** (section 3), and sets the Inventions against
  WTC I first: the fair comparison, the same years.
- **I1 records the Clavier-Büchlein order beside the 1723 order,** so that the graded-course claim can be
  checked.
