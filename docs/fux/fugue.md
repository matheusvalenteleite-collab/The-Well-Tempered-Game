# Fux, *Gradus ad Parnassum* (Vienna 1725): the doctrine of fugue (Exercitium V)

Source: OCR text `gradusadparnassu00fuxj_0_djvu.txt` plus page images from `gradusadparnassu00fuxj_0.pdf`. The PDF page is the printed page + 14 throughout pp. 143–237; I checked this against the running heads.
Latin is normalised: long s becomes s, OCR ligature debris is cleaned up, and I have supplied the obvious spellings. Abbreviations follow the 1725 print (*&c.*). Alfred Mann's translation was not used. All English renderings are mine.

**Confidence tags.** **[read]** means the statement is in the printed text (or in an unambiguous caption or running head). **[inferred]** means it is my interpretation, a reading of a music image, or a conclusion the text does not state outright. I did **not** transcribe the musical examples note by note. The 90–300 dpi scans let me identify voices, captions and layout, but I was not reliable on clefs and pitches. Where I give pitches, they come from the solmisation printed under the notes or from letter names in the text.

**Speakers.** The book is a dialogue: *Aloysius* (the master, Palestrina's persona) and *Josephus* (the pupil). "Fux says" below means Aloysius says it.

*De variis Fugarum Subjectis* (pp. 231–238) is still part of the fugue doctrine. It holds Fux's most explicit statement on how to form the answer ("pars respondens"), so I cover it below as §8. The section on modes (§7) is covered only where it bears on fugue.

## 1. Lectio I: De Fugis in genere (pp. 143–146)

**1.1 Rejected etymology; fugue must be distinguished from imitation.** (p. 143) **[read]**
Many derive *fuga* from *fugere/fugare*, "as if the preceding part fled, chased by the following". That would be true, Fux says, "nisi id ipsum ... imitatio faceret". So "alia definitio, qua Fuga ab imitatione distinguatur, statuenda est".
*Rendering:* "...were it not that imitation does exactly the same; so another definition must be laid down, by which fugue is distinguished from imitation."

**1.2 Definition of fugue.** (p. 143) **[read]**
"Fuga est quarundam Notarum in parte praecedenti positarum, ab sequente repetitio, habita modi, ac plerumque toni, semitoniique ratione."
*Rendering:* "A fugue is the repetition, by a following part, of certain notes placed in a preceding part, with regard to the mode, and usually to the [position of the] tone and semitone."
*Note* **[inferred]**: the definition has two levels. Keeping the mode is obligatory. Keeping the exact tone/semitone pattern applies only "plerumque" (as a rule). Everything Fux later says about answers (§8) works inside the room this "plerumque" leaves.

**1.3 "Modus", not "tonus".** (p. 143) **[read]**
"Per Modum intelligo id, quod vulgo tonus appellatur". He says *modus primus* rather than *tonus primus* so as to avoid confusion with the whole-tone intervals 9:8 and 10:9. A full treatment of the modes is postponed to the end of the work.

**1.4 Definition of mode.** (p. 143) **[read]**
"Modus igitur est series intervallorum infra limites Octavae contentorum, atque in vario, disparique situ Semitoniorum collocatio".
*Rendering:* "a series of intervals contained within the bounds of an octave, and the placing of the semitones in varying and unequal positions."
There are six positions of the semitones, so there are six modes, on **D, E, F, G, A, C** in that order ("sex Modi ... nempe: D. E. F. G. A. C."). Hence Fux's "Modus primus" = D, "secundus" = E, and so on. The octave scales on p. 144 mark the semitones (*mi fa*) with black notes.

**1.5 The mode is bounded by fourth + fifth, and fugue subjects must be regulated by those bounds.** (p. 144) **[read]**
"Modus Quartae, & Quintae intra Octavam existentium limitibus terminatur: juxta quos limites Fugarum themata regulanda sunt."
*Rendering:* "The mode is bounded by the limits of the fourth and fifth that lie within the octave; fugue subjects are to be regulated according to these limits."
An example follows, labelled "E. C." (sic, for "E. G."): octave species divided as 5+4 and as 4+5 in two clefs.

**1.6 The fifth/fourth exchange (the core of the "tonal" answer).** (p. 144) **[read]**
"Adeo, si pars incipiens Quintae saltum efficiat, ne pars sequens Modi, vel Octavae limitem excedat, Quartae saltum faciat, necesse sit, & vice versa."
*Rendering:* "Accordingly, if the leading part makes the leap of a fifth, the following part must make the leap of a fourth, so as not to exceed the limit of the mode, that is of the octave; and conversely."
- Example (p. 144) **[read]**: two voices on one staff, *alla breve*. The first voice leaps a fifth, the second answers with a fourth. **[inferred]** The pitches are probably D–A answered by A–D in a C clef, but the scan does not let me read the clef with certainty.
- **[inferred]** This is the only general rule of answer-mutation Fux states in Lectio I. It is stated for the leap of the fifth/fourth that outlines the mode's division. He does not use the words "tonal" or "real", and he does not here speak of degree-to-degree substitution (^5 → ^1). The effect is the same as a tonal answer at the head of the subject.

**1.7 Imitation is not bound by this.** (p. 144) **[read]**
"Ad quod imitatio non obstringitur; cui sufficit, eosdem gradus, ac saltus sequi."
*Rendering:* "Imitation is not bound by this; it is enough for it to follow the same steps and leaps."
Example (p. 144): the same opening, but the leap of a fifth is answered by a fifth.

**1.8 Fugue may begin only at the unison, octave or fifth; imitation at any interval.** (p. 145) **[read]**
"Fuga, extra intervalla Modum constituentia, v. g. extra Unisonum, Octavam, & Quintam institui non potest: Imitatio autem ... in omnibus intervallis."
*Rendering:* "A fugue cannot be set up outside the intervals that constitute the mode, i.e. outside the unison, octave and fifth; imitation can be at any interval."
The fourth is reconciled with this on p. 148 (see 2.4).

**1.9 Each mode has its own proper subject.** (p. 145) **[read]**
"cuivis Modorum etiam proprium suum subjectum aptandum esse. Ex quo infertur, thema primo Modo proprium, secundo, tertio ... sexto minime convenire, propter tonorum, semitoniorumque situm".
*Rendering:* "to each mode its own proper subject must be fitted; a theme proper to the first mode will not suit the second ... sixth, because of the different position of tones and semitones."

**1.10 Test of a proper subject: answered at the fifth, it keeps the same tones and semitones.** (p. 145) **[read]**
Example "Subjectum primi Modi proprium": a semibreve subject solmised *re, fa, mi, re* (= D F E D in mode D, **[inferred]** from the solmisation and the mode). "Hoc thema, a parte sequente in Quinta reassumptum eosdem tonos, & semitonia servat". The answer is also solmised *re, fa, mi, re* (= A C B A **[inferred]**).
*Rendering:* "This theme, taken up by the following part at the fifth, keeps the same tones and semitones, which can happen to this subject in no other mode."

**1.11 Counter-example in mode A.** (p. 145) **[read]**
Mode A ("la, mi, re") is the one "qui solus Modo D ... aequalitate proximus est" (the only mode closest in make-up to D). In A, the subject *re, fa, mi, re* (= A C B A, **[inferred]**) answered at the fifth comes out *mi, sol, fa, mi* (= E G F E). "in partis sequentis penultima Nota loco mi, fa venit, & toni, semitoniique situs invertitur": in the following part's penultimate note *fa* comes in place of *mi*, and the position of tone and semitone is reversed. This could be corrected only "Diesis ... adminiculo" (with a sharp), which the diatonic genus does not allow.
*Point* **[inferred]**: in Fux's diatonic exercises, a subject counts as fit for a mode when its answer at the fifth needs no chromatic alteration and keeps the semitone pattern.

**1.12 No sharps or flats in diatonic subjects.** (pp. 145–146) **[read]**
"genus Diatonicum ... omni Diesi, & b molli in thematibus abstinere debet: alias enim nunquam genuinam Modorum naturam compertam haberemus."
*Rendering:* "...must abstain from every sharp and flat in the themes; otherwise we would never discover the true nature of the modes."
This is relaxed for the *modulatio* (the free continuation); see 2.3.

## 2. Lectio II: De Fuga duarum partium (pp. 146–154)

**2.1 The five-stage plan of a two-part fugue.** (p. 146) **[read]** The steps paraphrase one continuous sentence:
1. Take a subject of a few notes appropriate to the mode and write it in the part that is to begin. When it is finished, give the same notes to the second part "in Quarta, vel Quinta intrando". Meanwhile the first part continues in florid counterpoint.
2. "post aliquantulam modulationem ... primam clausulam in quinta Modi conficies": after some modulation, make the **first cadence on the fifth of the mode**.
3. After a whole- or half-bar rest, or after a large leap even without a rest, resume the theme "in parte ferme, qua incepisti ... sed alio, quam inchoatum est, intervallo": in about the same part, but at a different interval than at the start. The other part, after a rest, should try to enter "prius, quam subjectum in altera parte finitum sit": **before the subject has finished in the other part**. Then, after some modulation, "secundam clausulam in Tertia Modi construes": the **second cadence on the third of the mode**.
4. Finally, with the subject in one part, make the other part enter with it "in secundo protinus subjecti tactu, si res patitur": straight away in the subject's **second bar**, if possible. With the parts "conjunctis artissimo vinculo" (bound by the tightest bond), end with the final cadence.

*Remark* **[inferred]**: steps 3 and 4 are a graded stretto: a later entry overlaps the subject, and the last one overlaps from bar 2. Fux has no technical word for it. "Stretto" does not occur.

**2.2 Worked example: "Fuga a 2." in mode D**, with "Cadentia in 5ta" and "Cadentia in 3tia" marked over the staves. (pp. 146–147) **[read]**
His commentary (p. 147):
- the subject finishes in the Alto and the Cantus takes it "in Quinta Modi";
- the first cadence is on the fifth;
- the Alto restates the subject "in Quinta Modi inferius, absque pausa ... quia per saltum";
- the Cantus then enters after a rest "in Octava Modi", "maturius quam in principio, nempe in tertio subjecti tactu" (earlier than at the beginning, in the subject's third bar);
- the second cadence is on the third;
- finally the Cantus enters "extemplo in secundo tactu", then the final cadence.
**[inferred]** So Alto leads on ^1, Cantus answers on ^5. In the middle section the subject enters on ^5 below and on ^8.

**2.3 Chromatic notes are allowed in the free continuation, not in the subject.** (p. 147) **[read]**
Josephus objects that there are sharps and flats in the example. The answer is that the ban applies to the subjects, "ubi ad noscendam Modorum naturam ... Semitonia naturali ratione venire debent". "Alia ratio est in Modulatione, ubi Diesis, atque b mollis usus non solum non prohibitus, verum etiam ad evitandam asperam mi contra fa relationem nonnunquam necessarius est."
*Rendering:* "It is different in the modulation, where sharps and flats are not only not forbidden but sometimes necessary to avoid the harsh *mi contra fa* relation."

**2.4 Entry at the fourth reconciled with "fifth only".** (p. 148) **[read]**
Josephus: you said the following part may enter only at the fifth, octave or unison, but then you mentioned the fourth. Aloysius: the first statement was made with respect to the fundamental note ("respectus habitus est ad Notam fundamentalem"), and that octave species has no fourth. The second statement ignores the fundamental and considers the upper part's relation. "Quanquam etiam hoc in casu A. illud Quinta dici possit, quia Nota fundamentalis D. subintelligi debet":
*Rendering:* "...although even in this case that A may be called a fifth, because the fundamental D must be understood beneath it."
So an answer a fourth below the dux, on A, is still "at the fifth" of the mode. There is a two-part example.
**[inferred]** The point is that the answer's pitch level is reckoned against the final (^5 of the mode), not against the interval between the two voices.

**2.5 Changing note values of the subject is permitted, and even graceful.** (p. 149) **[read]**
Asked why the note values of the subject change near the end, he answers: "non solum permissum esse subinde solutas Notas ligari, verum etiam ... grati per hoc accedere Compositioni: imo persaepe, ubi subjecta in angustum contrahi aliter non possunt, hac figurarum fractione opus esse."
*Rendering:* "it is permitted now and then to tie notes that were separate, and some grace is added; indeed, where subjects cannot otherwise be drawn into close quarters, this breaking of the note values is often needed." This ties rhythmic alteration to stretto ("in angustum contrahi").

**2.6 Mode E: first cadence on the sixth, not the fifth.** (p. 149) **[read]**
"sicut ... in omnibus aliis Modis prima Clausula in Quinta fit, ita in hoc in Sexta facienda sit". The reason: when a third part is added and requires a major third, the modulation would be too alien to the mode, and the *mi contra fa* clash with the F that soon follows would offend.
*Rendering:* "as in all other modes the first cadence is on the fifth, in this one it must be on the sixth."
**[inferred]** In E, a cadence on B needs F♯ in the approach (and D♯ once a third part demands the major third), which clashes with the mode's F♮. So Fux cadences on C instead.
The rule stated: **first cadence on ^5 in all modes except E (on ^6)**; **second cadence on ^3** (stated for the D example; p. 146 gives it as general method).

**2.8 Mode A: answer mutated by a third leap; mid-fugue entry on a non-modal degree.** (p. 152) **[read]**
- Josephus: "Cur de secundae partis prima Nota ad secundam per saltum Tertiae processeris, comprehendo; quia fa illud in primae partis secunda Nota expressum alias haberi non licuisset."
  *Rendering:* "I understand why you went from the second part's first note to its second by a leap of a third: otherwise the *fa* expressed in the first part's second note could not have been had."
  **[inferred]** In mode A the answer replaces a step with a third leap so that it lands on the semitone degree (*fa*) matching the subject's. This is a mutation on the solmisation principle, in the same family as the fifth/fourth exchange. The exact pitches are not in the text, and I did not read them from the image.
- Josephus also asks why, after the first cadence, the Alto resumes the subject on B (*B mi*), a degree not proper to the mode. Aloysius: a bass on E prevents an entry on A, since A is a dissonance above E. "verum nihil perfectioni Compositionis derogatur, imo in melius vertitur, si id in medio Fugae fiat ... Aliud judicium ferendum est de principio Fugae, ubi ratio Modi, & chorda introitioni subjecti debita ... accurate tenenda est."
  *Rendering:* "...this takes nothing from the composition, indeed improves it, if it happens in the middle of the fugue. A different judgment applies to the beginning, where the rule of the mode and the degree owed to the subject's entry must be kept exactly."
  **Precept** **[read]**: entries on degrees outside ^1/^5 are allowed in mid-fugue. The exposition must use the modal degrees.

**2.9 Choose subjects that can be drawn together (stretto-able).** (p. 152) **[read]**
"Probanda itaque primo, inquirendaque subjecta sunt, hac ratione tractabilia: ideoque ante selectum ad id reflectendum erit."
*Rendering:* "Subjects must first be tested and examined whether they can be treated in this way [i.e. contracted]; one must reflect on this before choosing."
Aloysius admits the subjects are "trita, jejuna" (trite, dry). The aim is "non ... conceptus sublimitatem ... sed ... genuinam in Genere Diatonico Modorum naturam". Greater freedom comes with the mixed genus.

**2.10 Purpose of the diatonic discipline.** (pp. 153–154) **[read]**
The diatonic genus is narrower than the mixed, but "aptissimum ad dignoscendam Modorum naturam ... & ad Compositiones a Capella absque Organo decantandas magnopere necessarium".

## 3. Lectio III: De trium partium Fugis (pp. 154–168)

**3.1 Up to the third entry, everything is as in two parts.** (p. 154) **[read]**
"Quod de Fuga duarum partium dictum est, hic quoque subsistit, usque dum parti tertiae intrandi tempus sit". The third entry comes when the subject is complete in both parts, either after some modulation or at once, "prout partium constitutio aut patietur, aut postulabit".

**3.2 The third part's entry should add harmony.** (p. 154) **[read]**
It should enter so that "introitu suo aut Triadem harmonicam efficiat, aut in regulata per Ligaturam dissonantia (quod elegantius est) intrare". (The OCR breaks off at a column/page boundary. The sense is complete.)
*Rendering:* "by its entry either complete the harmonic triad, or enter on a dissonance regulated by a tie (which is more elegant)."

**3.3 Interval of the third entry.** (p. 154) **[read]**
"Illam plerumque, a qua subjectum primo inceptum est, ut varietati ... consultum sit".
*Rendering:* "[it should follow] usually the [interval] at which the subject was first begun, for the sake of variety." The composer may choose otherwise if the disposition of the parts suggests it.
**[inferred]** So the order is dux (^1) – comes (^5) – dux (^1 at another octave).

**3.4 Cadences in three parts differ from the bicinium.** (pp. 154–155) **[read]**
Erratum p. 154, line 22 reads "eadem ratio clausularum" (is the same rule about cadences valid?). "Minime. Imo nulla clausula formalis, aut absoluta (quae tertia majore clauditur) facienda, in qua subjecto potestatem intrandi non esse perspexeris. E contra, si subjectum intrare posse cognoveris, non solum in Quinta, Tertia, verum etiam aliis intervallis non nimis aversis ab natura Modi, clausulam, & absolutam, & fictam recte fieri assero."
*Rendering:* "By no means. No formal or absolute cadence (one closed by a major third) is to be made where you see the subject cannot enter on it. Conversely, if the subject can enter, a cadence, absolute or feigned, may properly be made not only on the fifth and third but also on other degrees not too foreign to the mode."
**Precept**: in three and more parts, cadence degrees are freed beyond ^5/^3 (to "other intervals not too alien to the mode") **provided a subject entry overlaps the cadence**.

**3.5 Formal vs feigned cadence (*inganno*).** (p. 155) **[read]**
"Clausulam formalem, sive absolutam, Tertia majore concludi, inde in Octavam labi". "Clausula vero ficta loco Tertiae majoris, minore utens ... auribus expectatam evitando eludit ... Unde ab Italis Inganno nuncupata est."
*Rendering:* "the formal cadence closes with a major third, which then slides to the octave. The feigned cadence uses a minor in place of the major third and cheats the expected formal cadence; hence the Italians call it *inganno*."
A formal cadence can also be avoided when, with the major third kept above, "pars fundamentalis Octavam effugiens aliam assumit Consonantiam" (the bass escapes the octave to another consonance). This is more elegant in more parts. Examples are on p. 155.

**3.6 A formal cadence is valid if a subject enters on it.** (pp. 156–157) **[read]**
p. 156: examples of a "clausula formalis" kept "beneficio subjecti intrantis", with the cadence formed on the subject's first note and then on its second. Such cadences are "non solum permitti ... verum etiam ... ornamenti" (allowed, even ornamental).
The reason (p. 157): "Clausula formalis, quietis indicium est; ideoque non nisi in fine, aut finito quodam articulo, novi cujusdam subjecti assumendi causa adhibenda. Subjectum autem in ipsa Clausula formali intrans, quietis opinionem abstrahendo, motumque ... usque ad finem desideratum promovendo, finem nondum adesse significat."
*Rendering:* "A formal cadence is a sign of rest, so it is used only at the end, or at the end of a section in order to take up some new subject. A subject entering on the cadence itself removes the impression of rest, drives the motion on to the desired end, and signals that the end has not yet come."
**[inferred]** This is the principle of the elided cadence.

**3.7 The "cadences" of the bicinium were not formal cadences.** (p. 157) **[read]**
They were made of 7–6 or 2–3 ("Septima, & Sexta, vel Secunda, & Tertia constantes, minusque quietis habentes"). They are "potius dispositiones ad clausulas formales": with a third part added, they become formal cadences. Example p. 157 "Accessione Partis tertiae".

**3.8 Bass under 7–6 and 2–3 progressions.** (pp. 158–159) **[read]**
He shows how a bass is fitted under two upper parts moving by 7–6 or 2–3, to give "modulationi facilitas". Conclusion: where a fifth and sixth stand together, the upper parts should have a 7 resolving to 6, or a 2 resolving to 3. The same holds for a fourth and fifth together. Such sequences help to "ingressuro subjecto viam sternere" (pave the way for an entering subject).
**[inferred]** These are Fux's equivalent of episode or link material. He does not name a separate "episode" category. "Modulatio" is his general word for the free connecting counterpoint.

**3.9 Analysis of his three-part fugue (mode D, p. 159 "Modi D."; commentary pp. 160–161).** **[read]**
- the third part enters "durante subjecto" with fitting counterpoint above;
- the Alto restates the subject "alio, quam incepit intervallo";
- the Cantus rests, then re-enters "diverso, quam in principio, intervallo ... in dissonantia valida ad subjecti praesentiam indicandam: clausulaque, sextae minoris ope, devitatur" (on a strong dissonance to signal the subject's presence; the cadence is avoided with a minor sixth);
- three bars' rest for the Alto, because the outer parts are too close and because the Alto must soon enter with the subject;
- "hunc Alti introitum in Quinta, & Sexta: maximam enim addit vim Compositioni";
- Josephus objects that the Tenor's entry has neither a triad nor a dissonance (only 6 and 8). Aloysius: that way of entering is "de consilio potius, quam de praecepto" (advice, not rule). Also the first note is a short minim, and the sixth above it is strong and makes the entry clear;
- later a cadence on F is evaded "per subjectum in Tenore post pausas intrans";
- then "figuris Notarum nonnihil immutatis, ut singulis in tactibus cuilibet parti cum subjecto intrandi potestas sit" (note values altered so every part can enter with the subject in successive bars: a stretto in every bar, **[inferred]**);
- final cadence.
Erratum p. 160: in bar 1 of the Cantus, read *fusae* (eighths) for *semiminimae* (quarters).

**3.10 Rests only before a subject entry.** (p. 161) **[read]**
Josephus concludes: "nunquam pausam ponendam esse, nisi immediate subjectum sequatur." Aloysius: "Ita sane. Aut subjectum vetus, aut certe novum, idque caeteris partibus deinceps repetendum, post pausam induci necesse est". He backs this with Matthew 22:12, the guest without a wedding garment.
*Rendering:* "after a rest either the old subject or a new one must be brought in, and the new one must then be repeated by the other parts."
Later qualified: the subject may return inverted (see 6.9).

**3.11 Mode E has a distinct final cadence; cadence degrees are left free.** (p. 161) **[read]**
Josephus is to compose in E, "neque haesitans, quo intervallo clausulas exstruas, dummodo in iis subjecto eleganter intrandi facultas detur". Aloysius first shows E's final cadence, "quae diversa ab aliis Modis est". The cadence example is on p. 161. **[inferred]** It is presumably the Phrygian close; I did not transcribe it.

**3.12 Repeated cadences on A in mode E are acceptable.** (p. 163) **[read]**
Josephus fears he has cadenced too often on A. Aloysius: the subject's nature led to it, and it is varied by the accompanying notes. Any lack of charm is due to the diatonic genus and "hujus Modi egestas" (the poverty of this mode). **[inferred]** In E, ^4 (A) is a regular cadence degree.

**3.13 Mode F: a "peregrine" passage is permitted only in the modulation, not in the subject.** (p. 164) **[read]**
"paululum peregrini, non nisi modulationis praesidio, subjecto immisceri posse". He praises the "subjectorum ingressum ubique opportuno tempore".

## 4. Lectio IV: De Fugis quatuor partium (pp. 168–174)

**4.1 Praise of tied dissonances (ligaturae).** (p. 168) **[read]**

**4.2 Entry order: Tenor follows Cantus, Bass follows Alto (usually).** (p. 169) **[read]**

**4.3 Don't crowd the parts.** (p. 169) **[read]**
"ne partibus nimis infarcitis, & in angustum contractis ab una alteri libere vagandi facultas adimatur". The OCR reads *admittatur*, corrected by the erratum p. 169, line 6 to *adimatur*. ## 5. Lectio V: De Contrapuncto duplici, in the octave (pp. 174–183)

**5.1 Definition.** (p. 174) **[read]**
"Per Contrapunctum duplex intelligitur Compositio artificiosa, eoque modo constructa, ut partes ejusdem inter se convertibiles esse possint, & pars, quae modo superior, nunc per inversionem inferior existat". It is called "double" because, with nothing changed but the inversion, it gives "duplicem & ratione acuminis, gravitatisque differentem ... Melodiam".
Its use is "maxime in Fugis plurium subjectorum ligamine combinandis".

**5.2 Only three species are treated: octave, tenth, twelfth.** (pp. 174–175) **[read]**
Some writers count double counterpoint at the 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 8ve, 10th, 12th. Fux keeps only those "quae & usitatiores, & in Compositione majoris momenti sunt. V.G. Contrapunctum in Octava, in Decima, in Duodecima". The others are of little use "ob angustias" or "cum aliis fere coincidunt".

**5.3 General rules for combining subjects.** (p. 175) **[read]**
1. "curandum est, ut subjecta differentes inter se sortiantur motus ... tribuendoque alteri subjecto minutiores, alteri majoris valoris figuras": give the subjects contrasting motion, one in smaller and the other in larger note values, so they can be told apart.
2. "ne simul, eodemque tempore incipiant; sed alterutrum positione cujusdam pausae tardius intret": they should not start together; one enters later after a rest.
3. "Non excedantur limites cuilibet Contrapuncti speciei infra praescribendi": do not exceed the range limit of each species.

**5.4 Counterpoint at the octave: definition and three rules.** (p. 175) **[read]**
Definition: "Compositio, ita instituta, ut inversa alterutra parte in Octavam acutam, aut gravem, variam reddat Harmoniam; nihilominus recte regularum rationi consentaneam."
Rules: "Primo: a Quinta abstinendum est. Secundo: Non procedatur in Octavam saltando. Tertio: infra limites Octavae te contineas oportet."
- Inversion table printed: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 over 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1.
- The fifth is forbidden "quia inversa dissonantiam, nempe Quartam efficit".
- p. 176: "Usurpata tamen cum ligatura locum habet": a fifth is admissible when used with a tie. **[inferred]** That is, as preparation of a syncopation, so that its inversion, a fourth, becomes a prepared or suspended dissonance. The wording is terse.
- p. 176: no leap into the octave, because inverted it becomes a unison, and the unison is approached by leap only "per modum clausulae". He praises Josephus for avoiding the octave on the downbeat ("in Thesi"), since its inversion, the unison, is used "nisi per Syncopen minus recte".
- pp. 176–177: the reason for staying within the octave: if the parts exceed it, "licet Consonantiae compositae in simplices mutentur, eadem resultabit Harmonia ... nec tam natura, quam situ tantum discrepans". A tenth inverts to a third, not a sixth, so the inversion gives no new harmony. Example p. 177.

**5.5 Examples of octave counterpoint.**
- p. 177–178: with a cantus firmus, and "Inversio in Octavam inferius". Erratum p. 178: example I, Tenor note 11, D for E.
- p. 178: without a cantus firmus.
- pp. 178–179: if every downbeat ("omnis Thesis") has contrary or oblique motion, the octave counterpoint can also be sung a 3 by adding a third part "cum translatione Contrapuncti in Decimam inferius" (the counterpoint copied a tenth lower) **[read]**.

**5.6 First fugue with *Contrasubjectum* (a 4, mode D, octave counterpoint).** (pp. 179–182) **[read]**
Caption: "Fuga a 4. Contrasubjecti artificio expressa: fundata in Contrapuncto duplici, cum inversione in Octavam". It uses "Fugae primae subjectum in Modo primo usurpatum".
Commentary (p. 181):
- "Contrasubjectum posita dimidiae mensurae pausa in Unisono instructum": the countersubject begins after a half-bar rest, on the unison;
- through inversion of the subjects it resolves into the octave, as at the places numbered "N. 1. 2. 3. 4. 5". There the countersubject is found now in the outer parts, now in a middle part, "semper in Octava respondet subjecto suo principali, ex qua immutatione diversa semper oritur Harmonia";
- Josephus notes that at one "NB" the unison inverts into the fifteenth, not the octave. Answer: compound intervals are equivalent in use to simple ones. Such a shift is sometimes granted "ut partibus mediis locus detur" (to make room for the middle parts) (p. 182);
- at N. 6, "relicto subjecto principali partes cum Contrasubjecto solo artificiose ludant, idque in arctum contrahant": the principal subject is dropped and the parts play with the countersubject alone in stretto (**[read]**; "in arctum contrahant" = stretto, **[inferred]** gloss);
- Josephus asks whether a stretto with both subjects was possible at the close. Aloysius: yes, "mutato unius, alteriusve figurae valore", with an example (p. 182). At the "NB" there, two crotchets replace two semibreves so that Tenor and Bass can enter in the subject's third bar. Such "figurarum fractio" is not only allowed but earns the composer esteem.
- Summary: thanks to double counterpoint, "si subjecta rite instituta sint, facili negotio Fuga instrui, ac in longum protrahi potest".

**5.7 Placement of the countersubject's entry may vary.** (p. 182) **[read]**
"non semper uno modo, id est, in primo subjecti tactu Contrasubjectum introducendum est; sed pro qualitate subjecti principalis, in secundo, vel tertio tactu". The illustration is "principium Fugae secundi Modi" (mode E), pp. 182–183, left to Josephus to finish.

**5.8 Everything else follows simple fugue.** (p. 183) **[read]**
"Caetera ex dictis de Fugis simplicibus, deque communibus Contrapuncti regulis petenda sunt." Each mode has its own way "modulandi, & protrahendi" because of the semitone position.

## 6. Lectio VI: Counterpoint transferred to the tenth (pp. 184–193)

**6.1 Definition.** (p. 184) **[read]**
"quia omissis quibusdam Consonantiis, & Dissonantiis pars alterutra in Decimam vel acutam, vel gravem transponi potest: manentibus iisdem prorsus figuris". Inversion table: 1–10 over 10–1.

**6.2 Rules.** (p. 184) **[read]**
- "duas Tertias, duasque Decimas, per motum rectum se invicem sequi non posse: quia ex illis duae Octavae: ex istis duo Unisoni per inversionem resultarent";
- "prohibentur duae Sextae, eo quod earum inversio in Decimam duas Quintas exhiberet";
- "abstinendum est a Quarta ligata in parte superiori; quia inversa efficit Septimam usu non receptam";
- "non excedatur Decima".
**[inferred]** So parallel imperfect consonances are excluded, and practically all similar motion is restricted.

**6.3 Examples.** **[read]**
- p. 184: with the cantus firmus.
- p. 185: "Inversio in Decimam, manente eodem Cantu firmo in loco suo".
- p. 185: the same result if the cantus firmus is raised a third and the counterpoint dropped an octave, since an octave plus a third = a tenth.

**6.4 The tenth gives a third voice by doubling.** (pp. 185–187) **[read]**
The counterpoint can be sung a 3 if the cantus firmus is copied note for note a tenth lower (or the counterpoint is). This works for every such bicinium "si ... quamvis Thesim, aut motum contrarium, aut obliquum obtinere contingat" (p. 187).
Free examples are on pp. 187–188. In one, the Cantus is placed an octave lower and a third higher, giving "perfectum Tricinium".

**6.5 Mode limits under inversion.** (p. 188) **[read]**
Josephus: the first cantus-firmus example leaves the mode under inversion. Aloysius: "Exit quidem: sed in Modum, Modo, quo inchoatum est, minime inimicum." These are examples for inversion, not use.
- "neque inversio statim in principio adhibenda, neque ad finem usque protrahenda est: sed post subjectum Modo congruum, aliud subjectum per Decimam convertibile introduci potest, quod deinde loco opportuno ... invertendum est."
- If one inverts at the very beginning, "a Tertia, vel Unisono Modi principium sumendum est; quo casu pars inversa intra Modi limites se continebit".

**6.6 In four parts.** (pp. 188–189) **[read]**
The fourth part may rest, fill in, or enter "cum subjecto per motum contrarium, vel alio licito modo". Example p. 189.

**6.7 A countersubject can work at the octave and the tenth at once.** (pp. 189–190) **[read]**
"Contrapuncta ista, nempe in Octava, & Decima, conjungi posse". Example p. 190: Alto with the Bass's countersubject "per meras Decimas incedere, Triciniumque efficere". The same holds if the countersubject is raised an octave and lowered a third "juxta Paradoxum de hac Contrapuncti Specie datum".

**6.8 Fugue using the tenth (mode D subject).** (pp. 190–192) **[read]**
Examples numbered N. 1–6. He calls it "Fugam breviter, & potius in gratiam hujus Contrapuncti in Decima factam, quam in exemplum Fugae omnibus artificiis instructae". The other five modes are left to Josephus, "subjectis ex Fugis simplicibus cujusvis Modi assumptis".

**6.9 Josephus's objections.** (pp. 192–193) **[read]**
- At N. 1–3 the countersubjects run in 3rds and 6ths, not 10ths. Answer: written another way they would fit exactly. In N. 1/2, Tenor and Alto "Decimam usque pertingere nequeant" (cannot reach the tenth), so they were given the third below. N. 3 and N. 6 were set so for closer spacing.
- At N. 4: "post pausam subjectum necessario reassumendum esse". Answer: "subjecta, aut recta, aut inversa introduci debere. Non vides, Contrasubjectum ibi inversum, vel contrarium esse? quod non solum conceditur, verum haud exiguam Compositioni tribuit varietatem". **Precept** **[read]**: after a rest a subject may enter in contrary motion (inversion).
- Warning: great judgment is needed so that the parts do not run too far off the staff, or the harmony becomes empty.

## 7. Lectio VII: Counterpoint at the twelfth, inversion by contrary motion, fugue with a cantus firmus, three subjects (pp. 193–217)

**7.1 Definition and table.** (p. 193) **[read]**
"cujus una duarum, vel plurium partium in Duodecimam aut acutam, aut gravem transferri potest". Table: 1–12 over 12–1.
"exhibet omnia intervalla praeter Sextam & Septimam in Sextam resolutam huic Contrapuncto usui esse. Caeterum ultra Duodecimam non exeundum est."
*Rendering:* "all intervals are usable except the sixth and the seventh resolving to a sixth; and the twelfth is not to be exceeded."

**7.2 Two-part twelfth: no further rule, and all motions are allowed.** (p. 194) **[read]**
"Omnes praeterea motus, id est, rectus, contrarius, & obliquus usui esse possunt." Example with cantus firmus. He ends the counterpoint on the fifth "ut translatio Contrapuncti in Duodecimam congruentius Modo suo terminari contingat": so the inverted part ends properly in its mode, although a transferred part may wander outside the mode.

**7.3 Accidentals in the transposed part.** (p. 194) **[read]**
In the twelfth inversion, the counterpoint transposed down a twelfth sometimes needs a flat so "ut intervalla per omnia Contrapuncto primo rite respondeant".

**7.4 Alternative layout and mode change.** (pp. 194–195) **[read]**
Alternatively, the counterpoint goes down an octave and the cantus firmus up a fifth. Josephus: this changes the mode entirely. Aloysius: this counterpoint has that privilege, the A degree is not improper to mode D, and in practice a formal cadence in the mode can be added "quadam modulationis appendice".

**7.5 Twelfth combined with tenth gives three parts.** (pp. 196–197) **[read]**
Caption: "Paradigma Contrapuncti in Duodecima, in Tricinium convertibile, adjuncto Contrapuncto in Decima". Rules for this (p. 197): besides the twelfth rules, "ut pars in Duodecimam translata infra Modi limites se contineat, fiat principium, & finis in Quinta. Procedatur per motum contrarium, vel obliquum. Evitentur ligaturae dissonantiarum."

**7.6 Harshness in minor-third modes.** (p. 197) **[read]**
In modes "quibus inest Tertia minor" the transfer under the cantus firmus produces harsh *mi contra fa* relations. This is to be tolerated with a cantus firmus but avoided in free composition. Example pp. 197–198.

**7.7 The sixth is allowed if syncopated.** (p. 198) **[read]**
"quod de Sexta soluta, seu non ligata intellige. Etenim Sextam Syncopatam adhiberi posse, sequens exemplum manifestum facit." Example: "Translatio in Duodecimam inferius".

**7.8 The three species combined.** (pp. 199–204) **[read]**
"quo modo praefatae Species unitae, duarum, trium, & quatuor partium conceptum ... efficere possint". Example sequence: "Primo in Duodecima a 2."; "Pars Cantus translata in duodecimam gravem"; "...in Decimam gravem"; "Alio modo" (several).
p. 202: if the twelfth counterpoint uses essentially only fifths and octaves by contrary motion and thirds by oblique motion, then counterpoints at the octave and the tenth can be extracted from it. Example "a 2. in Octava inferius" (p. 202). Then "si primum Cantum cum Tenore, & secundum Cantum cum Basso in Decima incedere facies, perfectum habebis Quatricinium" (pp. 202–203).

**7.9 Inversion "per contrarium".** (pp. 204–205) **[read]**
This applies only to a composition without tied dissonances ("ligaturis dissonantiarum destituta").
- **Contrarium simplex**: "quando Notae per contrarium simpliciter invertuntur, ut, quae modo ascendendo incedebant, descendendo progrediantur: nulla habita Semitoniorum ratione."
- **Contrarium reversum**: "ita invertendis Notis, ut ubique mi contra fa eveniat". The diagram (p. 205) pairs the scale ascending with the scale descending: "D. per inversionem nihilominus D. manere; E. in C, F. in B. mi, G. in A. inverti".
  **[inferred]** This is a mirror around D, which maps the semitone E–F onto C–B, so the inversion preserves the tone/semitone order.
- He inverts the third trio this way (p. 205, "Contrarium reversum").
- p. 206: only compositions without ties can be so inverted. Great caution is needed: "ne opinione grandis artificii concentus expectatae amoenitatis detrimentum patiatur". The following fugue (pp. 206–208) is offered "potius in gratiam Contrapuncti in Duodecima, quam in gratae modulationis exemplum".

**7.10 Double counterpoint is the only way to combine several interchangeable subjects.** (p. 208) **[read]**
"quam aliam viam inibis, Compositionem pluribus subjectis, iisque convertibilibus distinguere cupiens, si a Contrapuncti duplicis usu recesseris? Sine qua subjectorum mixtura, Compositio, praesertim Ecclesiastica simplex evadit, maximoque caret ornamento."

**7.11 Fugue or imitation woven through a cantus firmus.** (pp. 209–211) **[read]**
Example pp. 209–211, with the first subject able to enter throughout the cantus firmus. Method (p. 211): before choosing the subject, survey every bar of the cantus firmus to see whether the subject, "aut rectum, aut contrarium", fits "si non in omni tactu, saltem in quamplurimis". Alternatively, derive the subject "ex ipso Cantu firmo de Nota ad Notam, aut ad ejus similitudinem: immutata, aut syncopata nonnulla figura". Example p. 211.

**7.12 Release from the diatonic discipline.** (p. 212) **[read]**
From now on, subjects of free choice, the mixed genus, triple and quadruple time, smaller note values, "subjectis irregularibus, ac Modis transpositis utendi tibi deinceps arbitrium erit".

**7.13 Contrarium reversum in the mixed genus.** (pp. 212–213) **[read]**
"ad formandum Contrarium Reversum in genere mixto sufficere, si mi contra fa, & vicissim fa contra mi ubique resultet, non habita attentione ad regulam supra pro genere Diatonico praescriptam". Two inversions of the same theme are shown, and the second is "Modo ... longe congruentior". Its "principium, & finis intra Modi limites consistunt". This leads to a fugue outline (pp. 213–215) alternating *Rectum* and *Contrarium*. Erratum p. 215: Alto note 2, D for A.
p. 215: small note values let *Rectum* and *Contrarium* run simultaneously. "Nonnunquam Compositores relicto Contrario, cum Recto solo ad medium usque fere procedunt; & ibi primo Contrarium inducunt": left to the composer's choice. That fugue is *not* in double counterpoint (p. 215).

**7.14 "Fuga tribus subjectis instructa".** (pp. 215–217) **[read]**
"Fugam tribus instruendi subjectis, quorum secundum in Contrapuncto in Octava, tertium in Duodecima fundatum est."
Rules (p. 217):

## 8. De Modis (pp. 221–231), as it bears on fugue

**8.1 Six principal modes; all other octave scales are transpositions.** (pp. 221–226) **[read]**
"Totidem ergo Modi principales, & non plures necessario statuendi sunt. Caetera enim Octavarum Systemata ... ad unam harum Speciem, tanquam Modi transpositi reducenda sunt." Transposed forms of each mode are shown with signatures, e.g. "Modi transpositi primi Modi D".

**8.2 Authentic/plagal.** (pp. 226–230) **[read]**
The harmonic division (5 below 4) gives the authentic mode, the arithmetic division (4 below 5) gives the plagal. Reporting Zarlino: "principium, & finem, clausulasque utrique Modo fore communes; ut adeo differentia nulla sit, nisi, quod modulatio Modi Authentici in acuto, Plagalis vero in gravi existentiam suam habere debeat" (p. 227). Bononcini wants plagal subjects to descend to the fourth. He criticises Berardi's examples.

**8.3 Fux's advice: drop the plagal modes.** (p. 230) **[read]**
"non tam de praecepto, quam de consilio. Missis Plagalibus, utere sex Modis D. E. F. G. A. C. cum eorum transpositis, eo modo, quo supra de Fugis simplicibus edoctus es". This also applies to composition on chant (p. 231).
*Note* **[inferred]**: the answer doctrine of §1 is therefore not built on authentic/plagal ambitus pairs. It is built on the 5+4 division of a single modal octave.

**8.4 Order of the modes is indifferent.** (p. 231) **[read]**
Zarlino counted C first, recent practitioners D. "parumque admodum refert, quem ordinem quisque teneat".

## 9. De variis Fugarum Subjectis (pp. 231–238): Fux's fullest statement on forming the answer

**9.1 What "irregular" means.** (p. 231) **[read]**
Irregular subjects are those whose answering part does not correspond properly to the leading part "absque auxilio ♮, vel b." (without a natural or flat sign), unlike diatonic subjects. If made "ad Modi rationem", such subjects can still be regular.

**9.2 Two legitimate ways to form the answer ("pars respondens").** (pp. 231–232) **[read]**
Josephus proposes a subject (p. 231) and two answers: "Uno: quo ultima partis respondentis Nota Modo congruenter desinat. Altero: Solmisationem sequendo." The examples on p. 232 are labelled "Primo modo" and "Secundo modo".
Aloysius: "Uterque Modus, sub diversa tamen ratione legitimus est. Primus, quia in chorda Modi ultima sua Nota recte incidit. Secundus, quia ultima Nota sua Solmisationis rationem habuit."
*Rendering:* "Either way is legitimate, on different grounds. The first, because its last note falls properly on a degree of the mode; the second, because its last note kept the solmisation."

**9.3 Fux prefers the solmisation answer (the exact intervals), and "Cantus" outranks "Modus".** (p. 232) **[read]**
"Secundus respondendi Modus ob duas rationes haud aspernandus. Primo, quia sic canendi rationi magis consultum est. Secundo, quia ab Subjecti tenore non adeo receditur. Et firmum tibi sit, ut abrumpendo Subjecto, nisi gravi ratione postulante, quoad fieri potest, abstineas; potior est enim ratio Cantus, quam Modi; quia defectus Modi facile suppleri potest modulando cum altera parte; tenendoque Notam aliquam ad Modum pertinentem, supra illam extra Modi chordam existentem."
*Rendering:* "The second way is preferable for two reasons: it better suits the singing, and it departs less from the course of the subject. Hold firmly that you should, as far as possible, refrain from breaking the subject unless a weighty reason demands it; for the claim of the melody is stronger than that of the mode, since a defect of mode is easily made up by the modulation in the other part, by holding some note belonging to the mode against the note that lies outside the mode's degrees."
**[inferred]** This is a major qualification of the "tonal" mutation in §1.6. Fux allows the answer to end off the mode's degrees, which is a real-type answer, and to be corrected harmonically by the other voice. He prefers it whenever the mutation would "break" the subject. Another example (two voices, with b and ♯) follows with "&c.", plus "Idem judicium sit de Subjecto sequenti" (second example, p. 232).

**9.4 Those subjects are "irregular" because they are not in their proper mode.** (pp. 232–233) **[read]**
Being regular or irregular depends on viewpoint. They are "Irregularia, quia non in proprio Modo sua collocata sunt, ideoque Semitonium illud in parte superiori utrobique existens, beneficio b. mollis efficiendum erat. Regularia dici possunt respectu generis mixti hodie quam maxime usitati." The proper mode is A ("Modus A."), where that semitone exists without b (p. 233). A b at the start, outside the melody, signals mode A transposed up a fourth (i.e. to D with one flat, **[inferred]**). Then the subjects are regular. "Verum hodierno tempore, quo Genus mixtum vi magna invaluit, hae subtilitates ... non admodum attenduntur." (p. 233)
**[inferred]** For Fux, "D with one flat" is mode A transposed, not mode D. This bears directly on how the answer's degrees should be read.

**9.5 Subjects with a fourth between notes 1 and 2: keep note 3's distance from note 1.** (pp. 233–234) **[read]**
Josephus's subject is marked wrong at its third note. Aloysius: "Intervallum inter primam, & tertiam Subjecti Notam, Septima constans: nam interjecta Nota non est tanti roboris, ut operire valeret illius saltus improprietatem. In ejusmodi itaque Subjectis, quorum inter primam, & secundam Notam saltus Quartae intercedit, plurimum attendenda est Nota tertia, ut, quo intervallo illa distat a prima Nota Subjecti in parte Subjectum proponente, eodem intervallo distet in parte respondente. Jam in parte incipiente inter dictas Notas saltus Sextae reperitur: idem ergo intervallum parti respondenti dandum est."
*Rendering:* "The interval between the first and third notes is a seventh; the intervening note is not strong enough to cover the impropriety of that leap. In subjects with a leap of a fourth between the first and second notes, attend especially to the third note: its distance from the first note in the answer must equal its distance in the subject. In the leading part there is a sixth between those notes, so the answering part must be given a sixth."
**[inferred]** When the initial 4th is mutated (e.g. to a 5th), the answer's next note must follow the frame (1→3 interval) rather than the step from note 2.
Josephus objects that this leaves the mode. Aloysius: no. Since the leading part's subject does not end on the octave of the mode, how could the answer end on the fifth? And all intervals stay within the modal octave. With a sharp, i.e. a transposed mode, the answer must be formed somewhat differently. "Ita nonnunquam pro Modi natura, & differentia unum idemque subjectum quoad partem respondentem diversimode formandum est." (p. 234)

**9.6 Chromatic subject: two answers.** (pp. 234–236) **[read]**
- "Duobus modis huic subjecto responderi potest. Primus est habita ratione ad Modum: secundus ad Solmisationem."
- For the modal answer, "resolvendum est subjectum ex genere Cromatico in Diatonicum": reduce the subject to its diatonic skeleton, find the answer, then re-chromaticise (examples p. 235).
- Can the answer keep all the semitones? "Nequaquam. Quia E. molle nimis alienum est & minime conveniens Modo D." It would be different if E♭ were "substantialiter" part of the subject.
- The solmisation answer (p. 236) "utpote minus rumpens subjectum, prae primo ... amplectendus est". The same subject taken ascending "& Modo, & Semitoniis per omnia rite consentit".

**9.7 Instrumental subjects; summary rule of mutation.** (pp. 236–237) **[read]**
Josephus proposes wide-ranging themes "pro Fidibus" (for strings). Aloysius: these suit "hodiernae incontinentiae" (today's intemperance). Avoid them in four parts, where parts would obstruct each other. But the answer is well formed:
"ex eo, quod loco saltus in Quintam in parte prima existentis, saltum in Quartam: loco progressus in Secundam, saltum in Tertiam, parti respondenti quibusdam locis pro Modi natura nonnunquam attribueris".
*Rendering:* "...in that, where the first part leaps a fifth, you have sometimes given the answering part a leap of a fourth, and where it moves by step, a leap of a third, in certain places, according to the nature of the mode."
"Permultum tamen interest in subjectorum electione. Non enim omnia indistincte subjecta aeque bene deduci possunt" (choice of subject matters a great deal; not all subjects can be worked equally well).
**[inferred]** This is Fux's complete mutation repertory: **5th ↔ 4th**, and **step (2nd) → 3rd**, "sometimes", "in certain places", "according to the mode". He does not formulate a degree-mapping rule such as "^5 is answered by ^1". The mapping in the examples has to be read from the music.

**9.8 Time signatures and placement of tied dissonances** (pp. 237–239). **[read]**
- under cut time (*alla breve*), ties only on the downbeat ("in Thesi");
- with *Presto*, on the 1st and 3rd crotchet;
- under *Adagio*, on every crotchet.
It is not fugue-specific, so it is not developed here.

## 12. Where Fux and Bach part: checkable claims for a comparison with the Well-Tempered Clavier

Each item is phrased as a claim derived from Fux, with what to test in WTC I/II (48 fugues). "Fux" items rest on the precepts cited. The prediction about Bach is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

**A. The answer**
1. **5th↔4th exchange at the head.** Fux §1.6 (p. 144): a leap ^1→^5 in the subject is answered ^5→^1 (a 4th), and vice versa. Test: in every WTC subject that opens with ^1–^5 or ^5–^1 (or reaches ^5 early), is the answer tonal at that point? Count tonal vs real answers.
2. **Fux states no degree-mapping rule.** He gives only interval substitutions: 5th→4th, step→3rd (p. 237). Test whether Bach's tonal answers are better described by degree mapping (^5→^1 in the head, ^1→^5) than by Fux's interval rule. Look especially for cases where Bach mutates a step, not a leap (e.g. ^5–^6 answered ^1–^2 by step, not by a 3rd).
3. **Mutation by a third instead of a step** (pp. 152, 237). Test: count Bach answers in which a step in the subject becomes a leap of a 3rd in the answer. The prediction is that it is rare. Bach usually absorbs the mutation by repeating a note or by a 4th/5th change.
4. **Fux prefers the solmisation (interval-exact) answer over the modal one**, "potior est ratio Cantus quam Modi" (p. 232), and allows an answer that leaves the mode's degrees, fixed by the other voice. Test: in subjects that end on or move to the dominant, does Bach shape the answer so it returns to the tonic (Fux's *first*, "modal" way, which Fux ranks second), or does he keep the intervals exact (Fux's preferred solmisation way)? Count both.
5. **Third-note rule after an opening 4th** (p. 234): the answer keeps the 1→3 interval of the subject. Test on WTC subjects that open with a 4th (e.g. ^5 up to ^1).
6. **No answers needing accidentals outside the mode** (diatonic genus, p. 145). Test: Bach's major-mode answers routinely introduce ^4♯ (the dominant's leading tone). Count answers that require a sharpened ^4 in major or a raised ^7 of the dominant. The claim is that Bach treats the dominant as a key and Fux treats it as a degree of one modal octave.
7. **Chromatic subjects are answered by reducing them to a diatonic skeleton** (p. 235). Test with chromatic subjects (e.g. I/12 F minor, I/24 B minor) whether Bach's answers correspond to the diatonic-skeleton answer or to the exact (real) transposition.

**B. Exposition**
8. **The comes enters only at the 5th (or 4th below, still ^5), octave or unison** (pp. 145, 148). Test: any WTC exposition entry on another degree? The prediction is none in the exposition.
9. **Exposition degrees are strict; in mid-fugue an entry on a non-modal degree is allowed** (p. 152, entry on B in mode A). Test: map Bach's middle entries by scale degree. Fux would rate entries on ^3, ^6, ^2, ^4 as a mid-fugue licence. Bach's use of relative-key entries is far more systematic.
10. **Third entry at the interval of the first** (p. 154: dux–comes–dux). Test: in 3- and 4-voice WTC fugues, does entry 3 = dux form and entry 4 = comes form? Count exceptions (e.g. I/1 C major, which has a non-alternating, stretto-like exposition).
11. **Entry order pairs Tenor with Cantus and Bass with Alto** (p. 169). Test the voice order of 4-voice expositions: is it S→T or A→B pairing (e.g. A S T B or T A S B patterns)? Which of the 24 orderings does Bach use and how often?
12. **The countersubject begins after a rest, on the unison/octave, in contrasting note values, and may enter in bar 1, 2 or 3 of the subject** (pp. 175, 181, 182). Test: regular countersubjects in WTC. Do they begin after a rest, contrast in note values, and invert at the octave only? Or at the 10th/12th too?
13. **Rests are followed by a subject entry (old or new, direct or inverted)** (pp. 161, 193). Test: in WTC, does every voice's re-entry after a rest of ≥ half a bar begin with the subject or countersubject? Count re-entries with free material.

**C. Continuation and cadences**
14. **Cadence plan in a two-part fugue: first cadence on ^5, second on ^3, final on ^1** (p. 146), except mode E: first on ^6 (p. 149). Test: in the two-part WTC fugue (I/10 E minor) and others, list the internal cadences. Does Bach's first cadence after the exposition fall on V (major) or on III (minor)? Is ^3/III the second? Compare minor-mode fugues (Bach: often III first) with Fux's D-mode plan (^5 first).
15. **No formal (major-third) cadence unless a subject entry overlaps it** (p. 154–157). Test: in 3-/4-voice WTC fugues, are internal perfect cadences elided by a subject entry? Count clean internal PACs with no entry.
16. **Feigned cadence (*inganno*)**, i.e. a minor third in place of the major, or the bass escaping the octave (p. 155). Test the frequency of deceptive or evaded cadences in WTC middle sections.
17. **Episodes: Fux has no episode category.** Connecting material is "modulatio", built from 7–6 / 2–3 suspension chains with a fitting bass (p. 158). Test: are Bach's episodes sequential and motivic (from subject/countersubject fragments) rather than suspension chains? Measure the share of fugue length without a subject or answer entry. Fux's model fugues are nearly all entries.
18. **Stretto as the culmination: entries tighten from after the subject → bar 3 → bar 2** (pp. 146–147, 160–161). Test: do Bach's strettos increase in closeness toward the end? Note cases where stretto comes early (I/1 C major, I/8 D♯ minor) or not at all.
19. **Note values may be altered ("figurarum fractio") to make stretto possible** (pp. 149, 182). Test: in Bach's strettos, how often is the subject rhythmically altered or truncated? Compare with augmentation and diminution (I/8; II/2, II/9), which Fux does not discuss.
20. **Inversion: Fux's diatonic "contrarium reversum" mirrors around D, keeping semitone positions** (p. 205). In the mixed genus it is enough that *mi* meets *fa* (p. 212). Test: in Bach's inverted subjects (e.g. I/6, I/8, I/15), find the axis of inversion (the degree that maps onto itself). Does Bach choose an axis that preserves the semitone pattern, like Fux's D-axis, or one that maps tonic onto dominant?

**D. Several subjects and invertible counterpoint**
21. **Octave counterpoint: no 5ths (except tied), no leap into the octave, stay within an octave** (p. 175). Test Bach's invertible countersubjects for exposed 5ths and for spacing beyond the octave (which, per Fux, gives "the same harmony" when inverted).
22. **Tenth counterpoint: no parallel 3rds/10ths/6ths; no tied 4th in the upper voice; range ≤ 10th** (p. 184). Test: are any WTC subject/countersubject pairs inverted at the 10th? Search the double and triple fugues first. If so, check them against these rules.
23. **Twelfth counterpoint: no 6th (unless syncopated), no 7–6; range ≤ 12th; for a trio begin and end on the 5th** (pp. 193–198). Test for 12th inversions in WTC (e.g. I/4 C♯ minor triple fugue, II/18 G♯ minor double fugue, II/14 F♯ minor triple fugue) and check the 6th rule.
24. **A countersubject may be invertible at the 8ve and 10th at once** (p. 190). Doubling at the 10th makes a third voice (pp. 185–187). Test for passages in thirds/tenths that come from such a combination.
25. **Three subjects: distinguish by note value; ideally five voices; subjects may be broken off** (p. 217). Test I/4 (5 voices, 3 subjects) and II/14 (3 subjects, 3 voices): contrast of values, complete or truncated subjects. Fux's claim that three subjects "need" a fifth voice is tested by II/14.
26. **Order of introduction: Fux's model fugues introduce countersubjects with the subject from the start** (p. 181). His tenth-fugue advice brings the invertible subject in after the modal subject (p. 188); this is not the later "double fugue with separate expositions" type. Test which WTC double/triple fugues expose subjects separately (e.g. II/14, II/18, I/4) vs together.

**E. Mode vs key (framing claims)**
27. **Six modes, plagal discarded; "D with b" is mode A transposed (Aeolian a fourth up), not a variant of D** (pp. 230, 233). Test: in Bach's minor-mode fugues, does the answer behaviour fit Fux's Aeolian (A-type) or Dorian (D-type) model? Use Fux's p. 145 claim: the subject that is "proper" to D (*re fa mi re*) keeps its semitones when answered at the 5th, and the same subject in A does not. Note that Bach's sources sometimes use "Dorian" signatures (one flat fewer), so record the notated signature as well.
28. **Mode E (Phrygian) needs special cadence treatment** (pp. 149, 161). Bach's WTC has no Phrygian fugues. Note this as a point where the comparison is empty, not as a disagreement.
29. **Diatonic subjects with no accidentals** (p. 145) vs Fux's own admission that today's mixed genus ignores these subtleties (p. 233). Test the proportion of WTC subjects containing accidentals relative to the key signature. The prediction is a large majority. Fux's §9 is the relevant standard for those, not §1–4.
