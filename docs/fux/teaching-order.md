# What Aloysius teaches at each exercise (two voices, first and second species)

## The ordering principle

Within a species the exercises follow two nested orders and nothing else: the modes in their
"natural order" D, E, F, G, A, C (B excluded, p. 50), and within each mode the counterpoint
first above, then below the same cantus. Fux never chooses an exercise *because* it illustrates
a precept. Precepts are attached to exercises in two ways:

1. **Socratic correction.** Josephus writes; Aloysius marks a fault Josephus "could not have
   known" and states the rule (Figs. 6, 12, 15 in first species; Fig. 26 → 33 in second). The
   fault tends to arise where the mode or the voicing invites it: the tritone in E (Fig. 12),
   the out-of-mode opening fifth when writing below (Fig. 6), the major-sixth leap in G.
2. **The pupil's own question or observation.** Josephus asks (why the sharp, why the bar
   numbers) or notices (no penultimate fifth in E, Fig. 37), and the answer becomes doctrine.

So the order is pedagogically meaningful only at second hand: the mode and voicing make certain
faults likely, and the fault occasions the precept. About half the exercises teach nothing new.
